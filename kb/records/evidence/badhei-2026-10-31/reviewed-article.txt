# Badhei: woodworking, obligation and a name

By Ahimanikya Satapathy

A craft history can begin with what people made. It becomes more revealing when we ask what they owed, who settled disputes, and what happened when an official wrote their name differently. For Badhei history, these questions bring a woodworking description into conversation with an older account of community organisation and a much later court case.

The revised Ganjam district gazetteer associates Badhei with wooden agricultural implements and furniture, distinguishing their work from Kamar ironwork and Pathuria stonework. That is a useful regional description of work, but it cannot identify every carpenter or settle every family's community affiliation.[^1]

## Work with obligations attached

In his 1909 account, Edgar Thurston, assisted by K. Rangachari, describes carpenters and blacksmiths holding rent-free land in return for services. These included village festival cars, agricultural implements and tent-pegs for travelling officials.[^2]

It records hereditary headmen, council meetings and fines. Larger fines supported poorer members' marriage expenses and council costs. The account also treats Maharana as a title; it does not let us identify a person's community from a surname.[^2]

This is a colonial description, not a timetable of present customs. Read within that limit, it shifts attention from the finished object to the obligations and institutions surrounding its maker.[^2]

## When spelling had consequences

For Srinibasa Moharana, the difference between “Badhei” and “Badhai” was consequential. His application for an OBC certificate was refused on that distinction. In its judgment of 23 December 2015, the Orissa High Court treated the spellings as referring to the same community in the dispute and directed that the petitioner receive the certificate.[^3]

The case shows why naming deserves more care than a list of interchangeable words. A spelling difference can become a barrier in someone's dealings with an institution. This is a dated judgment, not advice about present eligibility; it does not establish that every occupational label, surname or related community name is equivalent.[^3]

## Asking more of a craft history

A fuller account would need the voices of working families: how they learned, how work changed, which names they use, and which relationships matter to them. Those questions remain open here. A district description, a colonial classification and a court's decision each answer something different. Keeping those differences visible makes room for a history in which people are more than the occupations recorded beside their names.
