# Ghumura: the drum carried into the dance

The drum travels with the dancer. In the Ghumura section of a fellowship report hosted by the Centre for Cultural Resources and Training, an earthen, pot-shaped instrument is secured in front of the chest, supported from the shoulders and waist. That small practical detail gives us a way into the performance: look at the instrument, then follow the body carrying it.

Ghumura brings this relationship between sound and movement into Kalahandi’s cultural life. Its name belongs to both an instrument and a dance. To get to know it, we can begin with the occasion, the ensemble and the people named in the record.

## The festival and the ensemble

Kalahandi’s district administration places Ghumura at Nuakhai, Dushara and other festivals. Its table describes a troupe of 18–20 artists. That is a useful picture of a group performance, rather than a fixed requirement for every troupe or a count of people earning a living from the form.

The Orissa Reference Annual of 2009 lists Ghumura, Nishan, Taal, Turi and Madal among its instruments. Its short description refers to about 15–20 male participants. Read alongside the district table, it helps us picture an ensemble while leaving room to ask how a particular group is organised.

At a welcomed performance, try following one player through a short passage. Then widen your attention to the group. What changes together? Who appears to give a cue? These are starting questions for observation, not a claim that we have analysed a recording.

## A history still worth asking about

Ghumura is often discussed in the language of martial performance. The 2009 Reference Annual explains that the force of the music and the spirit of the players lead observers to regard it as a war dance. Its wording describes an interpretation; it does not supply a dated record of the dance’s beginning.

That distinction matters when telling its history. An origin story, a researcher’s interpretation and an identifiable historical object each answer different questions. The sources used here do not establish a founding date or an uninterrupted line from an ancient battlefield to today’s festival.

A fuller history could bring these strands together by naming the storyteller, locating the document or object, and recording how practitioners understand the account. For now, the festival settings and named performances give us firmer ground from which to begin.

## From Kalahandi to a book-fair stage

A small item in the New Delhi World Book Fair’s Show Daily of 10 February 2013 records a Ghumura performance by Prativa from Odisha. It names the leader as Dhayananda Panda and lists an ensemble including Ghumura, Nisan, Jhanj, Turi, Madal and Bheri. The account places the form in the fair’s cultural programme at Lal Chowk Theatre.

The Ministry of Culture’s panel dated 1 March 2024 separately lists Dhyanananda Panda and PRATIVA, described there as a tribal folk academy in Bhawanipatna, with a Ghumura-labelled video link. These records offer named starting points for further research. They do not confirm a current performance offer, and we have not auditioned the linked recording.

## Making room for the next learner

Teaching also appears in the institutional record. Sangeet Natak Akademi’s approved-grantee list for the 2014–15 scheme cycle names Chintu Prasad Naik for a Ghumura training programme. The listing establishes that approval; it does not tell us whether classes are running now or what participants went on to do.

Those are questions a future, welcomed conversation could explore. Who teaches the first rhythm? Who makes or repairs the instrument? What must a new dancer learn before joining the group? Record the artist’s own terms and preferred credit, and agree separately on recording and public sharing.

For Sounds of Odisha, Ghumura opens a path through an instrument, a district’s celebrations and the work of learning together. Following that path carefully means leaving space for the people who carry the drum to explain what they carry with it.
