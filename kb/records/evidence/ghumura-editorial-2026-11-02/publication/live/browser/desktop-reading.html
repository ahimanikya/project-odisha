Ghumura: the drum carried into the dance

A Ghumura dancer carries the rhythm against the body. The pot-shaped drum hangs in front of the player, leaving both hands free to strike its skin-covered mouth while the feet move. In his 2015 study, Ashis Mohapatra describes ropes supporting it at the waist and shoulders, and learners first resting the instrument on their laps. Playing and travelling across the performance space are skills that have to meet.

In Kalahandi, this is part of a festival culture. The district administration names Nuakhai, Dushara and other occasions for Ghumura, describing groups of eighteen to twenty artists. The number gives a sense of an ensemble; it is not a rule for every performance. Looking more closely, that ensemble becomes a set of different jobs: making an instrument, carrying a rhythm, singing a passage and moving with other people.

Before the first beat

Chintu Prasad Naik’s training report describes a potter shaping, sun-drying and firing the drum’s clay body before its mouth is covered. Illustrations distinguish it from the bowl-shaped Nisan and paired metal Jhanj.

These differences help a listener look beyond a general impression of percussion. The instrument carried by the dancer is one part of a larger musical arrangement. Mohapatra describes the Nisan player controlling tempo and signalling changes of movement. He also describes a sequence in which the drums fall silent for a song, followed by dance after each couplet. Sound, silence and movement take turns carrying attention.

Learning where to stand—and when to turn

Naik diagrams a circular formation: Nisan at the centre, feather-bearing performer outside, dancers turning from inward to outward while circling. Other diagrams arrange players in triangles and rows. He connects expression to song, including devotional, agricultural, romantic and humorous material.

A player must attend to the people around them as well as to their own hands.

A named troupe on two stages

The New Delhi World Book Fair’s newsletter of 10 February 2013 records Prativa’s Ghumura performance under Dhayananda Panda. It lists Ghumura, Nisan, Jhanj, Turi, Madal and Bheri as accompanying instruments.

Another account brings the performance closer. Writing about the February 2013 Konark Dance and Music Festival, reviewer Lalitha Venkat describes Prativa from Bhawanipatna with a vocalist and two main drummers. She notices the small pot-shaped drums played by the dancers and a performer crossing the stage with peacock-feather bunches in his hands. Her account makes singing, drumming and movement separately visible within the same presentation.

Venkat also reports a belief that Ghumura may have been a wartime dance. This is an interpretation of its martial character, not a dated account of its invention. The force of a performance can be described without turning it into proof of a single ancient origin.

The work of passing it on

Sangeet Natak Akademi lists Naik’s project among its 2014–15 approved grantees. His report pictures seated learners with drums and groups practising standing movements. This documents one teaching project, not present classes.

Behind the moving ensemble are skills someone else must have time to learn.
