Kandhamal: leaves, learning and everyday knowledge

By Ahimanikya Satapathy

Reading · Kandhamal

A leaf plate and a language book offer two ways into Kandhamal's cultural life. One asks us to notice the decisions behind an everyday object; the other names people who help prepare material for readers. Both invite a closer question than what a place is famous for: whose knowledge makes these things possible?

Kandhamal: leaves, learning and everyday knowledge

Kandhamal's district survey places its headquarters at Phulbani and identifies Phulbani and Balliguda as its two subdivisions. Its account moves between plateaus, hills and cultivated valleys. These places provide a setting for particular accounts of work and learning.

Place, community and language need separate attention. CIIL describes Kui as a language spoken by Kandhas; that association does not make Kandhamal a district of one community or one language.

Before a leaf becomes a plate

The Balliguda Forest Division working plan for 2021–22 to 2030–31 describes siali as a woody climber with large leaves. Collected leaves are sun-dried for two or three days, then five or six are joined with thin bamboo slivers to make a plate. Plates are bundled for sale. The account also identifies October–February as a local peak in leaf quality and availability.

That description makes the finished plate less anonymous. Drying, joining and preparing it for sale are separate tasks, not something a leaf does by itself. The plate carries a sequence of decisions about material, preparation and the form in which it will reach a buyer.

Judgement, time and the market

A February 2017 JICA report follows a Kandhamal siali chain beyond collection. Buyers assess holes, dryness and size; plates move through local traders, with some later trimmed or pressed. It distinguishes hand stitching, sewing-machine stitching and press-moulded products. The material is shared, but the work and finished forms differ.

The report also records pressures that a celebration of craftsmanship could overlook: limited drying and storage space, and urgent cash needs that can lead to distress sales. Its proposed improvements are not evidence that those improvements happened. A plant-based plate alone tells us neither whether its maker was fairly paid nor whether harvesting was sustainable.

People behind a language page

Another kind of making appears in the credits of the 2024 CIIL–NCERT Kui Primer. Among its resource persons are teacher Bapuji Pradhan in Daringbadi, teacher Udhaba Kanhar in Balliguda and teacher educator Anita Kumari Mallick in Tikabali. These named contributions connect a published learning resource to people in particular places.

Our Kui introduction follows the book more closely. Here, the credits matter because they name people and roles behind the finished page. Preparing learning material is another form of work through which a place's knowledge becomes visible.

Seeing the work

The leaf accounts mostly describe roles—collectors, makers and traders—while the primer credits give us individual names. Read together, they encourage us to ask who becomes visible when work reaches its audience. A plate arrives ready to use; a book arrives ready to open. Looking back along their making brings decisions and people into view, and gives us a more attentive way to encounter Kandhamal.
