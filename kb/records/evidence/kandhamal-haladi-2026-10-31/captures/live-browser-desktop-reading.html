Kandhamal Haladi: the work behind a place-name

By Ahimanikya Satapathy

Reading · Kandhamal

Kandhamal Haladi asks us to follow turmeric beyond its colour. The name connects an ingredient with a growing area, but also with decisions about planting material, processing and sale. Following those decisions brings growers and their organisations into the story.

A reading idea, not a scheduled visit.

Research checked 2026-10-31 · Editorial preview

A name with a geography

The GI Registry records Kandhamal Haladi under application 610, filed by the Kandhamal Apex Spices Association for Marketing (KASAM), with a certificate dated 1 April 2019. The specification published in November 2018 names twelve tahsils within Kandhamal, including Phulbani, Daringbadi, Raikia and Baliguda. The name is tied to a defined production area.

Its cultivation account begins with rhizomes saved from a previous crop or borrowed from neighbours. It describes mostly rain-fed cultivation and leaf mulching. After harvesting, some rhizomes are retained for planting; others are boiled and dried for sale. Further processing includes cleaning, polishing and grinding. These are the application’s descriptions of practice, not a timetable for every farm today.

A crop still needs a buyer

A February 2017 JICA report traces another side of Kandhamal's turmeric: the work between harvesting and a sale. Its account includes gathering fuel for boiling, drying the crop and negotiating with a local trader after quality is examined. Traders sort, store and move the produce; processors may sell polished fingers or grind them into powder.

The report records constraints as well as activities: limited storage, drying conditions that could damage the crop, and lower purchase prices associated with traders' advances. These details matter because a sale is shaped by more than the reputation of a place. Having a crop ready and having room or money to wait for a buyer are different things.

What an organisation can reach

A 2022 study by Ankit Mahapatra and colleagues examined KASAM's services through 2021 interviews with leaders of twenty-two Spice Development Societies. It describes support including improved boiling units, drying sheets and market information. Only three sampled societies had their own storage facility.

The study classifies women as a minor rather than major target across the sampled societies. Its authors argued for more specific attention to women’s work, including harvesting, boiling and drying. Their findings raise a practical question: do the people doing the work also receive the support intended to improve it?

Reading the name more closely

A place-name on turmeric can prompt more attentive questions: where was it grown, how was it processed, and whose work connects it to the buyer? A GI record, a trading-chain account and a study of producer services answer different parts of that enquiry. Together they make Kandhamal Haladi more than a celebrated label: a crop whose value is worked on, negotiated and shared through particular relationships.
