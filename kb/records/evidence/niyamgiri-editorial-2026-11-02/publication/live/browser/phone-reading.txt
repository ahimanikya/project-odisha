Niyamgiri: the landscape behind the decision
By Ahimanikya Satapathy
A crop’s roots offer one way into the story of Niyamgiri. In an undated study commissioned by Odisha’s Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes Research and Training Institute, Dongaria Kondh informant Suresh Wadaka explained how different roots mattered when arranging crops on a slope. He described shallow and deeper systems working differently against soil loss, and the need to consider their spread and depth when deciding what to plant together. Here was a person explaining the ground through the work of cultivating it.
A reading idea, not a scheduled visit.
Knowing the ground: The Foundation for Ecological Security’s exploratory study included interviews and walks in selected villages served by the development agency at Chatikona. It acknowledges seasonal gaps, language barriers and differences between villages. Wadaka’s account offers a particular way of understanding cultivation, rather than a rule for everyone living in the hills.

Whose knowledge is heard when a cultivated and worshipped landscape is also considered as a mineral deposit?
What the court put before the villages: On 18 April 2013, the Supreme Court directed Gram Sabhas—village assemblies—to consider individual, community, cultural and religious claims in the Niyamgiri case. Worship associated with Niyam-Raja was specifically at issue. The court asked the assemblies to examine whether the proposed mining would affect those rights, including the deity’s abode. This brought the meaning of the landscape into the decision, beyond a measurement of the proposed mining area.

A judicial officer of district-judge rank was to observe the proceedings and sign the minutes certifying their independence. The assemblies’ decisions would then inform the Ministry’s final decision on Stage II forest clearance. Village deliberation and the Ministry’s clearance decision had different responsibilities. These proceedings concerned the proposed mine; the judgment separately addressed alleged violations at the Lanjigarh refinery.
Twelve assemblies in the later record: The Xaxa Committee’s 2014 report supplies a later account. It says Palli Sabhas were held between 18 July and 19 August 2013 in twelve hamlets in Rayagada and Kalahandi districts, selected by the state as likely to be affected. All twelve rejected the proposed mining. The report’s footnote identifies its evidence: English translations of the resolutions supplied by the state and held by the committee. That trail matters. It tells us how the committee knew, while leaving the original meetings’ voices beyond what this account can reproduce.

A parliamentary answer on 13 May 2015 records the same outcome: twelve village Gram Sabhas had decided against mining, seven in Rayagada and five in Kalahandi. Those are village decisions, rather than a count of individual voters or every settlement in the hills. The answer gives a firm dated checkpoint; it does not establish today’s permissions or a permanent ban.
Reading the landscape and the record together: Remembering Wadaka’s attention to roots keeps an individual’s reasoning in view as we read the institutional record. The village decisions become easier to approach as claims about a place people know.
