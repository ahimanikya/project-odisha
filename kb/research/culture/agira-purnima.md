title: "Agira Purnima: Which Way Will the Fire Lean?"
description: "On the full moon of Magha, farming families offer vegetables to Agni and watch a straw fire for signs of the next crop. What they see in its flames varies across Odisha."
---

On the full moon of Magha, villagers in Jagatsinghpur bring the straw left after threshing to an evening gathering on open ground. They light a heap of straw and throw vegetables into the fire as an offering to Agni. The district gazetteer calls the night *Agira Punei*, or *Agni Purnima*. It falls in January or February, when the grain from one season is already home and the fate of the next crop is still an open question. <sup id="ref-1-1"><a href="#source-1" aria-label="Footnote 1">1</a></sup>

For a cook, there is good reason to keep an eye on the fire: somewhere in it is a brinjal or a sweet potato. A farmer might watch the upper edge of the same fire for a different reason. Which way does it lean? Accounts of Agira Purnima (ଅଗିରା ପୂର୍ଣ୍ଣିମା) preserve more than one answer to what that movement might mean. <sup id="ref-2-1"><a href="#source-2" aria-label="Footnote 2">2</a></sup> <sup id="ref-3-1"><a href="#source-3" aria-label="Footnote 3">3</a></sup>

## Bringing a village fire together

In J. C. Mahanti’s account, the gathering begins a week before the moon is full. Girls sweep the village roads, women clean their homes, and men collect *kuta*, straw, and firewood. Each family contributes to a single pile. When the moon appears, people gather to light the fire, offer prayers and roast sweet potatoes and brinjals. In the Jagatsinghpur account, the whole occasion fits into a few lines. Mahanti gives us a sense of the work that has to happen before a village can spend an evening together around one fire. <sup id="ref-1-2"><a href="#source-1" aria-label="Footnote 1">1</a></sup> <sup id="ref-2-2"><a href="#source-2" aria-label="Footnote 2">2</a></sup>

Writing in Odia, Debadatta Rath describes another way of marking the spot. At the edge of the village stands an *agi*, a bamboo structure thatched with straw. In some places the fire comes from the village smithy, and the priest lights the structure after worshipping Agni. People circle it singing *kirtan*, with a *khol* drum accompanying the devotional singing. Potatoes and brinjals go into the fire as offerings. Rath connects the observance to an ordinary job awaiting householders: after worshipping Agni, they can begin thatching their roofs. People ask the fire that roasts their food to spare their homes. <sup id="ref-3-2"><a href="#source-3" aria-label="Footnote 3">3</a></sup>

In Garh Manitri, in Khordha, the place itself has a name. In fieldwork there in 1992, anthropologist Akio Tanabe recorded *agnijala padia*, the ground for the fire, in the village ritual calendar. Bundles of straw and vegetables burn there. Once the straw has been consumed, people eat the baked vegetables as *prasad*, food received back from the deity. Tanabe records the hope expressed in doing so: that Agni, now satisfied, will keep calamity from the village as the weather turns hot. <sup id="ref-4-1"><a href="#source-4" aria-label="Footnote 4">4</a></sup>

## What people read in the flames

Mahanti records three readings. An eastward lean is a warning of high tides carrying salt water into the fields. A westward lean raises the prospect of floods. When the fire inclines to the northeast, people welcome the prospect of a good crop. The distinction between the first two matters to a farming household. A field can suffer from too much water, and on the coast there is also the question of what kind of water will reach it. Mahanti gives these readings without assigning them to a particular village. <sup id="ref-2-3"><a href="#source-2" aria-label="Footnote 2">2</a></sup>

Rath records a different belief. The direction in which the fire leans points to the fields where paddy will do well. A person following that reading would look from the flames towards a stretch of farmland. In Mahanti’s telling, someone seeing the same movement might have tides or floods in mind. Both accounts describe people interpreting a sign together. Neither author follows the fire through to a measured harvest. <sup id="ref-2-4"><a href="#source-2" aria-label="Footnote 2">2</a></sup> <sup id="ref-3-3"><a href="#source-3" aria-label="Footnote 3">3</a></sup>

## What remains in the ashes

A report from Puri on 1 February 2026 describes wood fires in parts of the city and recalls the heaps of straw lit at the edges of villages. The report also records a belief concerning the ashes: scattering them on the land expresses the hope of a good crop. Even the little saying attached to the occasion looks ahead: *ଅଗି ଜଳିଲା, ଶୀତ ଟଳିଲା* — the fire is lit, the winter eases. A family can carry something home from the gathering besides the vegetables. <sup id="ref-5-1"><a href="#source-5" aria-label="Footnote 5">5</a></sup>

The preceding full moon, Pausa, brings [Puspuni](/stories/puspuni/) in western Odisha. There, children go from door to door to collect grain for a meal. On the Magha full moon of the coastal accounts, it is straw that households bring together, and the meal can be as simple as a roasted root. At [Makar Sankranti](/stories/makar-sankranti/), the new rice goes into a bowl for the sun or a friend. Agira Purnima gives the stalks left after threshing a place in the festival year too.

Tanabe records one more use of the previous harvest in Garh Manitri. A few bunches of paddy are deliberately left standing, tied and worshipped. Their grain supplies seed for the first ritual sowing. That gives two quite different destinations to the stalks of one crop: some burn on the full moon, while a few are kept for the field. <sup id="ref-4-2"><a href="#source-4" aria-label="Footnote 4">4</a></sup>

Much of [The Year That Feeds Us](/stories/the-year-that-feeds-us/) follows the grain itself. There is the first handful sown at [Akhi Muthi Anukula](/stories/akhi-muthi-anukula/), the forming ear at [Garbhana Sankranti](/stories/garbhana-sankranti/), the first rice tasted at [Nuakhai](/culture/osha/nuakhai/), and the grain measure set out for Lakshmi at [Manabasa Gurubara](/culture/osha/manabasa-gurubara/). Other stories attend to the resting earth at [Raja](/culture/osha/raja-parba/), the people standing in water at [Chitau Amavasya](/stories/chitau-amavasya/) and the cattle that worked the field at [Gamha Purnima](/stories/gamha-purnima/). On Agira Purnima the rice from those fields may already have been eaten. Its straw can still bring the village out to look towards the next harvest.
