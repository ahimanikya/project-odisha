title: "Akhi Muthi Anukula: The First Handful of Seed"
description: "Follow Odisha’s first ceremonial sowing from a farmers’ procession in Manijodi to fields in Koraput and a village in Nayagarh that keeps another date."
---

In Manijodi, in Cuttack district, the seed goes around the village before it goes into the ground. On Akshaya Tritiya in 2024, a reporter described paddy carried in a cane measure called a *gaunai*. Farmers walked in procession to the sound of drums and cymbals. When the procession ended, each took their own seed to their own land. For part of the day, the first sowing belonged to the village. The work that followed belonged to the person who knew the plot. <sup id="ref-1-1"><a href="#source-1" aria-label="Footnote 1">1</a></sup>

Odia farmers call this ceremonial beginning *Akhi Muthi Anukula* (ଅକ୍ଷିମୁଠି ଅନୁକୂଳ). On Akshaya Tritiya, the third day of the waxing moon in Baisakha, a cultivator sets aside paddy seed to sow with a prayer for the season ahead. A *muthi* is what fits in the hand. How many handfuls go into the earth, who carries them there, and even whether the field is free to receive them have different answers across Odisha. <sup id="ref-2-1"><a href="#source-2" aria-label="Footnote 2">2</a></sup> <sup id="ref-3-1"><a href="#source-3" aria-label="Footnote 3">3</a></sup>

## What a household takes to the field

In *Rice Rituals of Orissa*, Bhabagrahi Misra recorded two ways of beginning the sowing in the plains. In one, the family washed the seed basket and the grain measures the day before. In the morning, marks made with rice flour and water traced Lakshmi’s footsteps across the courtyard towards a lotus design. Paddy seed waited in its containers on a wooden seat. Soaked rice and molasses were offered as food. There were two uses for rice in that courtyard already. One portion fed the ritual, and another had to remain fit to sprout. <sup id="ref-2-2"><a href="#source-2" aria-label="Footnote 2">2</a></sup>

The householder carried the seed out on his head. A priest followed, and the ploughman brought the bullocks. After seven furrows had been opened, the landowner sowed seven handfuls. The ploughman went on with the rest. In Misra’s other account, the landowner went early with manure in a basket and seed in a measure. He heaped the manure, poured seed over it and placed a copper coin and a betel nut on top. Lakshmi was invited into the marked nut. After praying for a good crop, he scattered the seed and manure. The ploughman came later. Misra gives neither description a village name. Even within his account of the plains, the first handful could involve a procession or a farmer making an early start. <sup id="ref-2-3"><a href="#source-2" aria-label="Footnote 2">2</a></sup>

## A village honours the sower

In Manijodi, the farmers themselves are honoured before the procession. A life-size figure of a farmer stands in the village temple, wearing a hat, with a plough in one hand and a cattle stick in the other. Pratap Sahu, who established the temple in 2005, told *The New Indian Express* that cultivators had earned a respect their labour and pay seldom brought them. The villagers’ observance honours the *annadata*, the person who provides food. Seen from that temple, the journey of the cane measure says something about who will feed the village, as well as what will grow. <sup id="ref-1-2"><a href="#source-1" aria-label="Footnote 1">1</a></sup>

## When the old crop is still standing

Around Jeypore in Koraput district, farmers in Kotpad, Borrigumma and Kundra have marked the occasion by opening the soil, worshipping the earth and sowing seed. A local report describes heads of farming households gathering in a designated field. In 2020, during lockdown, the gathering largely fell away. The reporter saw a few farmers keep the rite privately. A household could still make a beginning even when its neighbours could not join it. <sup id="ref-4-1"><a href="#source-4" aria-label="Footnote 4">4</a></sup>

The previous year brought a different difficulty. J. Ramesh, a farmer in Jagannathpur, explained that irrigation had allowed him to grow a second crop. When Akshaya Tritiya came in May 2019, that paddy was ready to reap and still occupied the land. Other farmers around Jeypore faced the same problem. An auspicious date could arrive exactly on time and find a field already at work. For Ramesh, an account of why he missed the sowing tells us as much about his farming year as a description of the rite itself. <sup id="ref-3-2"><a href="#source-3" aria-label="Footnote 3">3</a></sup>

In Bhogra, in Nayagarh district’s Daspalla block, a different date carried a village memory. In 2023 *OrissaPOST* reported that residents still performed the seed-sowing rite on another auspicious day. They traced the choice to the death of Bharat Pradhan, a young villager killed by a leopard while collecting wood on Akshaya Tritiya decades earlier. Remembering him meant moving the first handful to another day. <sup id="ref-5-1"><a href="#source-5" aria-label="Footnote 5">5</a></sup>

## Following the seed

A handful of seed is a particular claim on the household’s grain. It must be kept through the months when it could have been eaten. It must then be let go, into a furrow whose yield nobody knows. That decision gives the other observances in this series a different starting point.

At [Raja](/culture/osha/raja-parba/), cultivation pauses for the earth’s rest. At [Garbhana Sankranti](/stories/garbhana-sankranti/), the family takes an offering to paddy that is already growing. At [Nuakhai](/culture/osha/nuakhai/), new rice can at last be offered and tasted. And at [Manabasa Gurubara](/culture/osha/manabasa-gurubara/), the grain measure has a place in Lakshmi’s worship. These observances belong to different local calendars. What joins them is something a farmer in Manijodi could hold in one hand. Before rice could fill a measure in the house again, someone had to carry a measure out.
