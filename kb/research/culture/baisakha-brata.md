title: "Baisakha Brata: Water for the Summer Month"
description: "A pitcher above a courtyard tulasi, a woman’s evening meal and a Bhadrak farmer’s roadside water pots tell three stories of Baisakha in Odisha."
---

Under a banyan tree on the Bonth–Agarpada road in Bhadrak, Natabar Nayak kept two pots of water apart from the ones he used to serve travellers. Those two were for the cattle and goats that came along the road. When reporter Hemant Kumar Rout met him in 2016, Nayak was eighty. A farmer from Gabharpur, he had spent nearly thirty summers bringing water from a tube well to this crossroads at Sakhipatana Chhack. He began each year on Bishuba Sankranti and kept going for three months. <sup id="ref-1-1"><a href="#source-1" aria-label="Footnote 1">1</a></sup>

In a courtyard, the vessel that goes up on [Pana Sankranti](/stories/pana-sankranti/) is small enough to hang over a tulasi plant. What it has in common with Nayak’s great earthen pots is the need for somebody to fill it again. Baisakha brings several kinds of observance together in the summer heat. One begins on the solar new year. Another, the *Baisakha Brata* (ବୈଶାଖ ବ୍ରତ), is a vow kept through the lunar month in devotion to Vishnu. And a person who gives water to others may keep to a calendar of their own, as Nayak did. <sup id="ref-1-2"><a href="#source-1" aria-label="Footnote 1">1</a></sup> <sup id="ref-2-1"><a href="#source-2" aria-label="Footnote 2">2</a></sup>

## A little shade for the tulasi

The *tulasi chaura* is the raised shrine where the household’s sacred basil grows. An Odia account in *Dharitri* describes the shade built above it from coconut fronds and branches of the jamun tree. A small piece of straw in the hole at the base of the earthen *theki*, or pitcher, slows the flow. Tip a vessel of water over the plant and it empties in a moment. Hang the *theki* over it and the water lasts, drop by drop. <sup id="ref-3-1"><a href="#source-3" aria-label="Footnote 3">3</a></sup>

A photograph from Tulasipur in Cuttack, published in the journal *e-planet* in 2023, catches the refill itself. A woman pours water into the earthen pot suspended above the plant. <sup id="ref-4-1"><a href="#source-4" aria-label="Footnote 4">4</a></sup> In a separate account from Cuttack, Parbati Sahoo described filling the pitcher with plain water. J. C. Mahanti also records households using *pana*, a sweetened drink, and refilling the pot daily for the month. He names the recipient of that care as Maa Brundabati, the tulasi honoured as a goddess. Putting up the shade and tying the *Basundhara theki* mark the new year. Fetching water for the plant becomes part of the days that follow. <sup id="ref-5-1"><a href="#source-5" aria-label="Footnote 5">5</a></sup> <sup id="ref-2-2"><a href="#source-2" aria-label="Footnote 2">2</a></sup>

## The woman keeping the vow

Mahanti’s account of [Baisakha Brata](/culture/osha/baisakha-brata/) locates the vow in the rhythm of a woman’s day. He describes married women and widows observing it. The woman keeping the vow, the *bratini*, worships Vishnu or visits his temple. She reads the *Vishnu Purana*. Towards evening she takes her one meal, which Mahanti calls *havishanna* or *kechudi*. He records no ceremonial thread worn for this brata. What would tell you that someone was keeping it was how she spent her day and when she sat down to eat. <sup id="ref-2-3"><a href="#source-2" aria-label="Footnote 2">2</a></sup>

Giving water has its own place in devotional writing about Baisakha. An Odia account in *Sambad* recounts the *Baisakha Mahatmya* teaching that a person can honour the divine by opening a *jala chhatra* for the thirsty. An umbrella for someone walking in the sun, food for someone hungry, a fan or a serving of pana also figure among the gifts. Each answers a need a traveller might have before reaching home. This is a teaching about the month. At a roadside water stand, the person serving can have a reason of their own. <sup id="ref-6-1"><a href="#source-6" aria-label="Footnote 6">6</a></sup>

## Who fills the roadside pots?

Nayak remembered being thirsty himself on a lonely stretch of road. By the time Rout visited, his summer mornings began with watered rice for breakfast and a trip to the tube well. On days when he could afford something extra, he might give people lemon *sarbat*. Some days there was *chhatua*, a roasted-flour preparation. A passer-by could count on the water. His neighbours called him *Pani Mausa*, their water uncle. <sup id="ref-1-3"><a href="#source-1" aria-label="Footnote 1">1</a></sup>

In western Odisha, the work of keeping a *jala chhatra* supplied could be shared across a town. Sambalpur Municipal Corporation’s May 2016 bulletin records water stands opened on Maha Bishuba Sankranti at crossroads including Kachery Chowk and Dhanupali Chowk. The corporation supplied earthen pots, mugs and ladles and assigned tankers to replenish them. Its instructions called for the pots to be cleaned daily. A stand that opened in the morning could serve an afternoon traveller only if someone had kept it filled. <sup id="ref-7-1"><a href="#source-7" aria-label="Footnote 7">7</a></sup>

Back in the courtyard, the next day’s task is on a smaller scale. The leafy shade stays in place. The hole in the pitcher stays open. What runs out is the water. In [The Year That Feeds Us](/stories/the-year-that-feeds-us/), the Pana Sankranti story begins with children scouting for fruit for the new year. Baisakha asks what happens the morning after, when the tulasi needs a drink again.
