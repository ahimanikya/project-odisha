# Boat songs of Odisha

What makes a song a boat song? The person singing it, the work it accompanies, or the boat imagined in its words? In Odisha, these questions lead towards different histories. A working repertoire, a fishing community’s theatre and a newly arranged song can all connect with water without having the same purpose.

Begin with the singer and the occasion. A song sung during work, a story performed at a festival and a family memory rearranged for a recording each connect music with life on the water in a different way.

## Nauria geeta: a name connected with work

In their 2024 survey of western Odisha’s folk music and dance, Dibya Ranjan Tripathy and Sontosh Kumar Mallik identify Nauria geeta as boatmen’s songs. They place the name alongside ploughmen’s and cartmen’s songs and describe these forms as unaccompanied by instruments.

The survey gives us a name and an occupational setting, but no boat-song transcription or named singer. The verse printed immediately afterwards is a ploughman’s song, not an example of Nauria geeta.[^nauria-survey-2024]

What did a particular boatman sing, and where did he learn it? Those questions remain open. The short reference cannot tell us the words, rhythm or history of a local repertoire.

## Chaiti Ghoda: a fishing community’s performance

Odisha Tourism associates Chaiti Ghoda with fishing communities along the coast. Its account describes a performer wearing a dummy horse and a performance combining mythological episodes with improvised dialogue.[^odisha-tourism-chaiti]

The Orissa Reference Annual 2009 gives a fuller description. A horse-shaped bamboo frame hangs at the dancer’s waist. The Rauta is the singer-commentator; the Rautani combines singing and dancing in the role of his wife. Dhol and Mohuri accompany the performance, which includes humour as well as mythological narration.

The annual connects the occasion with the full moon of Chaitra. It also recounts a legend in which Rama rewards a boatman with a horse. This is a traditional explanation recorded by the source, not a dated account of how the performance began.[^ora-chaiti-2009]

Chaiti Ghoda expresses a connection with fishing livelihoods through ceremony and theatre. What people sang while rowing, ferrying or fishing is a separate question.

## When the boat carries a feeling

Boats also enter the language of separation and danger. In a chapter published in 2020, historian Benudhar Patra discusses maritime imagery in folk sayings and songs, referring to Chakradhar Mahapatra’s Utkal Gaunli Geeta (1959). His examples include a damaged boat and a speaker cast into deep water. Read as images, they convey vulnerability; they need not describe the singer’s occupation.

Patra suggests that such perilous imagery may have developed as maritime activity declined. That is his hypothesis, not a dated history of the songs. His discussion also points to Upendra Bhanja’s Lavanyavati and its vessel reaching Sinhala. A literary voyage and a remembered song offer different kinds of evidence: neither, alone, tells us what a working boat crew sang.[^patra-maritime-2020]

These examples change the question. Alongside stories celebrating successful voyages, listen for dependence, uncertainty and the person left behind. A boat can carry emotional meaning even when its words cannot reconstruct a trade route.

## A family memory becomes a Cuttack recording

The Boat Song, released in early 2019, has identifiable makers and a remembered beginning. Producer Sumantra Misra, an architecture professor at PMCA in Cuttack, recalled hearing Amay bhashaili re from his grandfather in Calcutta. Later YouTube versions prompted him to attempt an Odia adaptation. His recollection explains the project’s inspiration, while leaving the older song’s authorship and transmission unresolved.

His student collaborators included singers Antara Ghosal and Chimera, Mondip Kalita on bass and Rex Walter Sangma on rhythm. Misra described an Odia–Hindi arrangement with a Hindi thumri passage and predominantly Western instruments. Bibhash Buragohain mixed and mastered it in Mumbai. The video was filmed at Dhabaleswar Ghata near Cuttack, with a dedication to boatmen on the Mahanadi and Kathajodi.[^boat-song-interview-2019]

Family recollection, online listening and student collaboration meet here in a contemporary adaptation with identifiable makers. Cuttack enters through the filming location and the dedication. The arrangement is a creative response to a remembered song, rather than a record of an unchanged working repertoire.

## Four connections and different histories

Nauria geeta names an occupational category. Chaiti Ghoda brings a fishing community’s stories into ceremony and theatre. Literary and folk imagery can make a boat carry feelings about danger or separation. A modern production can deliberately rearrange a remembered song for new singers and listeners.

These connections do not establish one birthplace or founding date for “Odisha boat songs”. Work, theatre and recording are different settings, not stages in a proven historical sequence.

For a work song, technique matters: does someone begin a line and others answer; does a phrase coincide with a shared task; is it also sung away from the boat? Those are questions for a documented performance, not features we can assign from the name Nauria geeta. The survey’s brief reference does not settle them.

## Listen for the song’s own history

If a singer welcomes a conversation, ask for one song rather than a general account of an entire tradition. What is its local name? Who taught it to them? Which words or occasions have changed? A family version and a staged arrangement may each have a history worth hearing.

Keep the singer’s preferred credit, place and date with that account, and ask separately before recording or sharing. Then return to the central question: what makes this song belong to this person and this occasion? That answer can reveal more than the sight of a boat in a music video.
