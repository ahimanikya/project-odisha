# Boat songs of Odisha

In 2019, a group of architecture students and their professor made a music video dedicated to the boatmen around Cuttack. Its producer, Sumantra Misra, told My City Links that a Bengali song remembered from his grandfather had inspired the project. A song could cross languages while finding a new setting beside Odisha’s rivers.

That journey offers a useful beginning. When we say “boat songs of Odisha”, are we asking about songs used by people at work, a fishing community’s ceremonial performance, or music composed about life on the water? Each question leads us somewhere different.[^boat-song-interview-2019]

## Nauria geeta: a name connected with work

In their 2024 survey of western Odisha’s folk music and dance, Dibya Ranjan Tripathy and Sontosh Kumar Mallik identify Nauria geeta as boatmen’s songs. They place the name alongside ploughmen’s and cartmen’s songs and describe these forms as unaccompanied by instruments.

This brief reference gives us a name and an occupational context. It does not supply a boat-song transcription, a named singer or a dated recording. The verse printed immediately afterwards is a ploughman’s song. We should not borrow it as an example of Nauria geeta.

To learn more, the next question belongs to a particular singer: what do you call this song, where did you learn it, and on what occasions do you sing it? A label in a survey cannot tell us the words, rhythm or history of every local repertoire.[^nauria-survey-2024]

## Chaiti Ghoda: a fishing community’s performance

Odisha Tourism associates Chaiti Ghoda with fishing communities along the coast. Its account describes a performer wearing a dummy horse and a performance combining mythological episodes with improvised dialogue.

The Orissa Reference Annual 2009 gives a fuller description. A horse-shaped bamboo frame hangs at the dancer’s waist. The Rauta is the singer-commentator; the Rautani combines singing and dancing in the role of his wife. Dhol and Mohuri accompany the performance, which includes humour as well as mythological narration.

The annual connects the occasion with the full moon of Chaitra. It also recounts a legend in which Rama rewards a boatman with a horse. This is a traditional explanation recorded by the source, not a dated account of how the performance began.

Here the connection with fishing livelihoods is expressed through ceremony and theatre. That makes Chaiti Ghoda important to this enquiry, while leaving open the separate question of what people sang while rowing, ferrying or fishing. A description published in 2009 also cannot promise a performance on a particular visit today.[^odisha-tourism-chaiti][^ora-chaiti-2009]

## A contemporary song takes another route

Neha Jha’s interview, published on 1 March 2019, places The Boat Song in a specific creative project. Misra described an Odia and Hindi production, filmed at Dhabaleswar Ghata near Cuttack, and dedicated to boatmen ferrying people on the Mahanadi and Kathajodi. He named the Bengali song Amay bhashaili re as its inspiration.

The interview describes a blend including thumri and Western instruments. This is evidence of a contemporary adaptation with identifiable makers. Its river setting and dedication do not turn it into an archival recording of an inherited Odia work song.[^boat-song-interview-2019]

## What can we say about origins?

These sources offer an occupational name, a ceremonial tradition and a dated creative project. They do not establish one birthplace or founding date for “Odisha boat songs”. Keeping those histories distinct gives each kind of music room to be understood on its own terms.

A boat in a poem or song may also carry an imagined journey. Before treating such a work as evidence of everyday labour, identify its author, text, edition and performance history. The presence of a river or a boat alone cannot establish that boatmen used the song at work.

Odisha’s maritime heritage is a valuable reason to ask these questions. It is not, by itself, evidence that a surviving tune was sung by ancient overseas traders. That connection needs a documented history of transmission.

## Begin with the singer’s explanation

For a future listening visit, begin with an invitation. If someone welcomes questions, ask for the local name of the song, the occasion and the person from whom they learned it. Ask whether it belongs to work, worship, family memory, the stage or a recent composition; leave room for an answer that crosses those categories.

Write down the place, date and the singer’s preferred credit. Ask separately before recording or sharing. A family version, a public performance and a commercial recording can require different permissions. Listening does not have to result in a clip.

This collection begins with reading. A future contribution could add a credited, permission-cleared account of one song in one setting, with its words and context checked by someone who knows the tradition. That would tell us more than assigning an entire coastline a single soundtrack.
