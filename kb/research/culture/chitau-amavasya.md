Lopamudra Parida remembers chasing after everyone on Chitau Amavasya. Her father was headed for the paddy field with cakes on a banana leaf. Her older sister had another portion and instructions from their mother to set it afloat. The child wanted to go both ways. In her Odia memoir, the family’s movements tell us something about the feast that a recipe alone could scarcely convey. Her father was taking food to the land in the hope that it would bear well. Her sister was carrying a request that the creatures around the house and fields would let the family go unharmed. <sup id="ref-1-1"><a href="#source-1" aria-label="Footnote 1">1</a></sup>

Chitau Amavasya (ଚିତଉ ଅମାବାସ୍ୟା) falls on the new moon of Shravana, during the rains. Its *chitau pitha* is made from rice and coconut. In Jagatsinghpur, the district gazetteer records cultivators’ families throwing these cakes into their paddy fields in hope of a good harvest. It also records boys and girls scattering small pieces for snails and oysters. A piece small enough for a child to throw has a particular place in this account of growing rice. It gives someone at home a way to look after someone out at work. <sup id="ref-2-1"><a href="#source-2" aria-label="Footnote 2">2</a></sup>

## What the cook keeps under the lid

Parida’s mother ground coconut with rice, thinned the mixture with water, and beat it with a ladle. She greased the *palama*, the cooking pan, with ghee on a piece of coconut husk or the cut end of a brinjal. Once a ladleful of batter went in, she covered the pan and wound a wet strip of cloth around the lid. The cake cooked on one side. The children waited again while the pithas went on a brass plate to the household deities. Later, Parida ate hers with sugar, her sister dipped hers in *khiri*, and their father ate his with curry. How their mother ate hers, Parida admits she never saw. <sup id="ref-1-2"><a href="#source-1" aria-label="Footnote 1">1</a></sup>

That last detail leaves the cook in view. Someone had to grate the coconut, judge how much water the ground rice would take, and lift each cake in time for the offerings to leave the house. In an interview with Debamitra Mishra, the teacher Sudha Mishra recalled cooking chitau over wood in earthen pans and later using other utensils. The pan can change within a cook’s lifetime. The reason for putting aside a portion is another kind of knowledge for a family to pass on. <sup id="ref-3-1"><a href="#source-3" aria-label="Footnote 3">3</a></sup>

## What a child asks of a snail

In flooded paddy, a worker has to place each bare foot in mud that hides what lies beneath it. A sharp shell can cut the skin. An Odia account in *Eodisha Samachar* describes girls throwing pitha into a pond and addressing Gendeisuni by name. In the words the report preserves, the speaker asks first for her own feet to be spared, then for her father’s, her mother’s and her brother’s. She asks for the crop to be spared as well. The report gives *Gendakata Amavasya* as a name used in Balasore, Mayurbhanj and Keonjhar. <sup id="ref-4-1"><a href="#source-4" aria-label="Footnote 4">4</a></sup>

Listen to whom the girl includes. Her mother is among those whose feet need protecting. So is the child herself. A description that speaks only of a daughter praying for the men going out to farm loses part of what she says. And the creature that might hurt someone receives a share of food. The prayer makes sense on its own terms. Tomorrow the family will have to enter that water again. <sup id="ref-4-2"><a href="#source-4" aria-label="Footnote 4">4</a></sup>

Jagatsinghpur’s gazetteer gives boys as well as girls a part in the offering. Parida remembers separate errands for her father and sister. In her younger aunt’s home in Sambalpur, she remembers chitau too, alongside a different gesture of protection. Her aunt drew marks below the navels of two children. Parida tells us what she saw in that household. The pond-side words about Gendeisuni belong to the account that records them. <sup id="ref-1-3"><a href="#source-1" aria-label="Footnote 1">1</a></sup> <sup id="ref-2-2"><a href="#source-2" aria-label="Footnote 2">2</a></sup> <sup id="ref-4-3"><a href="#source-4" aria-label="Footnote 4">4</a></sup>

## When the cake goes to a shrine

In Puri the same new moon is known for *Chitalagi*. Temple servitors restore jewelled gold forehead ornaments, the *chita*, to Jagannath, Balabhadra and Subhadra. They had been removed at Snana Purnima. In Banapur in Khordha district, Amritesh Khatua describes a different evening offering at the shrine of Bhagabati. Designated temple cooks prepare chitau in earthen pans for the goddess. After worship the cakes are distributed as *prasad*. A piece set afloat for a field creature and a piece received back from a shrine have each passed through someone’s hands, with a different recipient in mind. <sup id="ref-5-1"><a href="#source-5" aria-label="Footnote 5">5</a></sup> <sup id="ref-6-1"><a href="#source-6" aria-label="Footnote 6">6</a></sup>

At [Akhi Muthi Anukula](/stories/akhi-muthi-anukula/), a farmer carries rice out as seed. At [Garbhana Sankranti](/stories/garbhana-sankranti/), a family takes an offering to the standing crop as its grain develops. Chitau brings the person standing in the crop into sharp focus. A good harvest matters, and so does the condition of the feet that will carry someone home from work.

Elsewhere in this series, [Raja](/culture/osha/raja-parba/) follows the pitha made ahead so a household can rest, [Nuakhai](/culture/osha/nuakhai/) follows the first new grain to a shared meal, and [Manabasa Gurubara](/culture/osha/manabasa-gurubara/) asks who may eat in a house that has rice to give. At Chitau, a child takes a portion outside before the field has yielded its new rice. There is already someone to feed.
