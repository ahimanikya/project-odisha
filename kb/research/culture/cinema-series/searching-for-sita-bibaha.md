# Searching for Sita Bibaha: the film beyond the missing print

By Ahimanikya Satapathy

A song, a contract, a poster: small things can hold a large absence.

The first Odia film, *ସୀତା ବିବାହ (Sita Bibaha)*, has a place in our histories that few readers have been able to encounter on a screen. In 2011, Mohan Sunder Deb Goswami’s grandson, Kabiraj Deb Goswami, told a reporter that no print was available.[^memory] That was a statement about what could be found then, not a reason to stop asking.

Years later, filmmaker **Lipika Singh Darai** made the search the subject of a new project, with **Surya Deo** as collaborator. Their point of departure includes recordings and paper records he has collected.[^search] A history that seems to end with an unavailable film opens into the possibility of another.

## Returning to 1936

An earlier Calcutta screening preceded the film’s **28 April 1936** Puri opening.[^beginning]

Our [cinema overview](../odia-cinema.md) places this beginning within a longer story. Here, the question is what survives around it—and what those traces might allow us to understand.

## The people before the screen

The 2011 report identifies a play by **Kamapal Mishra** as the source and names **Makhanlal Banerjee**, **Aditya Ballav Mohanty** as Lakshman, and **Prabhabati Devi** as Sita among the performers.[^memory] A beginning commonly remembered through one filmmaker’s name was also an adaptation and a collaboration.

Piyush Roy’s research connects Goswami to touring rasalila and records Surya Deo’s interpretation of the film as primarily *gitinatya*, or musical drama. Roy also distinguishes Goswami’s credited role from accounts of Kali Films and technical collaborators in Calcutta.[^roy] These are questions for closer historical reading, rather than grounds for silently rewriting the credits.

An existing dramatic text and Goswami’s performance experience met a new recording medium. That connection is specific to this production; it does not make every Odia performance tradition its direct ancestor.

## What a surviving object can tell us

India Foundation for the Arts describes the material informing Darai’s project: gramophone recordings of musically narrated versions, artists’ contracts, copies of posters and newspaper coverage.[^search]

Each kind of record invites a different question. What does a contract say someone was expected to do? What did a poster promise an audience? How was a story presented in a recording? A record may answer one question and leave another open.

These musical narrations have not been established as the film’s original soundtrack. Heard alongside a poster or contract, they can still help us explore the world around its making.

## A filmmaker who listens for memory

Darai’s own account gives this project a human setting. She trained in sound recording and design at FTII. She describes **Ho as her mother tongue and Odia as the language of her schooling and filmmaking**. Her biography also recalls how tracing her late childhood music teacher became the subject of her first film.[^darai]

Language here is a place of meeting, with different personal histories entering it. That belongs in the story of Odia cinema as much as a release date does.

Her filmography includes *Backstage*, concerning puppeteers in Odisha, and works shaped as imagined conversations with her late grandaunt.[^films] These descriptions suggest an interest in the relationship between memory and the form a film can take. They do not tell us in advance what *Rediscovering Sita Bibaha* will become.

IFA’s 2024–25 annual report records the project as a proposed **docufiction**, combining documentation and fictional interpretation.[^annual] The distinction is useful for a viewer: a new enactment can be part of a filmmaker’s response to the past while remaining recognisable as something made in the present.

A completed film, release date or recovered print has not been confirmed.

## What the search leaves open

This story returns us to the work of [keeping cinema’s memory available](../../people/surya-deo.md). A collection acquires another life when a filmmaker brings new questions to it. Research can support an artwork; that artwork can lead a viewer back to the records.

There are still things to seek: a traceable copy, original credits, fuller accounts of the performers, and the relationship between the surviving music and the film. A responsible telling can acknowledge these gaps without making absence the whole story.

For Darai, the process of looking has itself become a subject. The newspaper, the song and the conversation are material for a new work, even while the older film remains unavailable.

*Sita Bibaha* remains a beginning. The search around it offers another.
