# Dalkhai

*Sounds of Odisha*

A line of dancers is only part of Dalkhai. There is a song to hear, an occasion to understand and a group of musicians whose work deserves attention. Looking beyond the formation changes the question: what are these people doing together, and for whom?

In western Odisha, Dalkhai connects festive singing and movement with community life. A stage performance can introduce that world, but the stage and the observance have different purposes. The difference becomes clearer through particular accounts of learning, worship and professional work.

Sources: [^dance-sambalpur-district].

## A song belongs to an occasion

Sambalpur district's cultural account describes young women from Binjhal, Soura and Mirdha communities performing Dalkhai during Dussehra, Bhaijuntia and other festive occasions, standing in a line or semicircle and singing Dalkhai songs.

The same district account describes Bhaijiuntia as an observance in which girls and women fast and seek Durga’s blessing for their brothers’ long lives. The relationship between sisters and brothers matters here: an occasion for dancing may also be an act of care, devotion and family obligation.

That does not make every Dalkhai performance a complete Bhaijiuntia observance. A festival programme can present a dance without the fast, household preparations or worship that give a community gathering its setting. Ask which occasion and which community a performance draws on before treating one version as the whole tradition.

Sources: [^dance-sambalpur-district].

## What the name can and cannot tell us

Angelica Marinescu’s 2021 study connects Dalkhai with goddess worship and explanations involving leaves and branches. Such accounts describe cultural meaning; they do not establish the first performance or a single inventor. A history of belief and a dated history of a dance are different enquiries.

Sources: [^dance-marinescu-2021].

## Follow one song through its movements

Marinescu documents Pooja Purabi Bohidar’s 2021 educational reconstruction: bending forward while holding a towel in both hands. Her example is a named demonstration, not a universal movement rule.

The accompanying song’s published English rendering connects sounding anklets and a moving nose ornament with girls exchanging mango blossoms in friendship. Ornaments become part of movement and relationships. Listening for what happens between the refrain’s returns reveals more than simply recognising the word Dalkhai.

Sources: [^dance-marinescu-2021].

## Learning for a different audience

In the same study, Pranita Nayak describes school learning and choreography of her own; Swagatika Nayak recalls school competitions and representing Odisha elsewhere. These accounts show another route of transmission alongside Bohidar’s reconstruction. Asking whether a performer is explaining an inherited practice, developing choreography or representing a region helps us understand the choices, without assuming every community changed together.

Sources: [^dance-marinescu-2021].

## The musicians and the cost of being seen

Shakti Sekhar Panigrahi’s reporting for the People’s Archive of Rural India gives the music a working life. Dhol player Krushna Chandra Bagh describes learning through his family. He also connects instrument-making and playing with the caste discrimination experienced by Ghasi and Ganda communities. These are people’s skills and histories, not anonymous background sound.

Instructor Durga Prasad Dash recalls a widening audience and new groups in the late 1980s. His recollection is one account of change, not a date when Dalkhai began. Dancer Tiki Meher describes pursuing dance as paid work and meeting social disapproval even as her performing opportunities grew.

Their accounts complicate a simple success story. More visibility can bring work and recognition while prejudice persists. When we credit a performance, the dancers, musicians and makers all belong in the picture. Applause does not tell us whether the people sustaining an art can make a living from it.

Sources: [^dance-pari-artists].

## Listen beyond the visible movement

Sambalpur's broader instrument account names Dhole, Madal, Nishan, Tasa and Muhuri among the region's instruments. It is a regional list, not a fixed Dalkhai ensemble for every village or stage.

At a performance, ask the host to name the instruments and players. Notice the relationship between a sung phrase and the movement that follows or accompanies it. Avoid guessing an instrument from its outline or treating a regional list as a prescription for every group.

A useful listening note records the song’s local name, the singer’s explanation of a line, and the occasion in which that version is used. Those three details keep a clip attached to its setting. They also leave room for a different answer from another singer.

Sources: [^dance-sambalpur-district].

## Visit with a question worth asking

Odisha Tourism places Dalkhai among folk dances associated with festivals and community gatherings, and describes cultural performances and artist sessions as ways to explore the state's arts. For a real journey, start with a confirmed programme or an invitation from a willing host.

Ask what the host wants a visitor to understand about this particular performance. Let the singer explain a verse before assigning it a meaning, and ask how the musicians learned their part. A visit becomes richer when the people making the sound can speak about their work.

Confirm whether visitors may attend and when questions or photography are welcome. Credit the people involved when sharing, and obtain permission to record. The aim is to return with an understanding of the occasion and its artists, as well as a memory of the dance.

Sources: [^dance-tourism-culture].
