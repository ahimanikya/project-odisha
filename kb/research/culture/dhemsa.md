# Dhemsa: a rhythm held in company

In a 2019 account of Kangrapada village near Lamtaput, Jitu Mishra names the person who made a performance possible: Deepa Sisa, a Gadaba woman and Odia graduate of Jeypur’s Vikramdev College. She showed the visitors around and arranged Dhemsa. The encounter begins with someone choosing how to share her culture.

Mishra describes women moving in a semicircle, leaning forward and working their heels, with men playing the accompaniment. His account gives us a place, a host and an occasion. It also makes a distinction worth keeping: this was a performance arranged during a visit. We should not silently turn it into a witnessed harvest ritual.

Sources: [^dhemsa-mishra2019].

## A shared name, several communities

The District Council of Culture’s Koraput Odyssey, published in 2016, describes chains linked at waists and shoulders. It names Paraja, Gadaba, Bhumia, Bhotra and Kondh communities, and places Dhemsa in festive gatherings and relaxation after work.

The regional name therefore needs a local description. A Gadaba account can help us understand one practice; it cannot supply all the steps, songs or meanings of another community. The booklet’s sweeping language about universal participation is not a village survey.

Sources: [^dhemsa-odyssey2016].

## How the line moves

Madhusmita Sahoo and Chiranjibi Behera’s 2024 Gadaba study describes linked waists, heel work and bending knees. Its sequence includes groups separating and rejoining, then rows passing through one another. The pattern changes; the dancers must stay responsive to their neighbours.

This gives the eye something more precise to follow than a general impression of togetherness. Watch how a formation opens and closes, and where a change begins. The link between bodies makes coordination visible. That is a reading of the described movement, not a claim that every step has one symbolic translation.

Sources: [^dhemsa-sahoo-behera2024].

## Hear the players as well as the dancers

The Odisha State Tribal Museum’s Gadaba profile names tamudibaja, madal, tamak and mahuri among instruments played by men. It also says that songs are composed for particular occasions and connects women’s Dhemsa with festivities. These are the museum’s descriptions of Gadaba practice, not a required ensemble for every Dhemsa troupe.

An instrument list helps a listener ask better questions, but it cannot reproduce a performance. Which player gives a cue? Does a song accompany this sequence, or does the instrumental phrase carry it? Who explains the words? Identifying those relationships takes us closer to the people making the dance than treating its sound as an anonymous background track.

Sources: [^dhemsa-museum-gadaba].

## The occasion changes the question

SCSTRTI’s Gadaba profile names Bandapana Parab, Dasara, Puss and Chait among the community’s festivals. It separately describes Dhemsa and women’s handwoven striped kerang cloth. A festival calendar gives a setting for further enquiry; it does not tell us that an identical dance accompanies every observance.

Compare three kinds of evidence: a community overview, Mishra’s arranged village visit and a national festival listing. Each records a different reason for presenting the dance. To understand a particular performance, first find out why people have gathered and who chose what visitors would see.

Sources: [^dhemsa-scstrti-gadaba], [^dhemsa-mishra2019].

## A voice already heard in this collection

The indexed description of a Gadaba Dhemsa entry at the 2022 ADI KALAKAR festival names a hulahuli chorus at its opening. That is a specific connection with our Huluhuli story. The original event page remains inaccessible to us, and we have not listened to its video.

The detail belongs to that description. It does not establish a universal opening. A public-stage introduction can preserve something valuable without documenting the full life of the practice.

## Cloth, language and change

SCSTRTI identifies Gutob and Ollari in its Gadaba language overview. That list cannot identify the language of a particular song. Nor can kerang cloth alone identify every person in an uncaptioned photograph. Names and explanations supplied by participants matter.

Sahoo and Behera describe changes in instruments, choreography and the use of market-bought clothing. Their study used a purposive group of 50 people in Lamtaput block; it is not a statewide measure of change.

A change of dress raises several different questions: whether weaving is still taught, what a performer can obtain, and what people choose for a stage. A visitor cannot settle those questions by deciding that one visible costume looks more authentic. The dancers and makers have histories beyond the few minutes presented to an audience.

Sources: [^dhemsa-scstrti-gadaba], [^dhemsa-sahoo-behera2024].

## What can be said about origins?

The 2024 study places a movement-based explanation of the name beside an association with percussion instruments. It also reports an oral account linking the dance to Nandapur’s kings. These are explanations recorded by the authors, not a securely dated founding history.

A remembered royal connection, the history of a word and the development of a dance need different kinds of evidence. The material inspected here does not establish a first performance, a migration route or a connection to Boita voyages. Those questions remain open; resemblance between cultural practices cannot answer them on its own.

Sources: [^dhemsa-sahoo-behera2024].

## A visit begins with an invitation

Confirm that an occasion is open to visitors and ask where to stand or sit. Photograph or record only with permission; ask separately before sharing. A family or community gathering need not become a tourist performance because someone arrives with a camera.

If invited to participate, let the hosts explain the movement and what is appropriate. Listen for one phrase, learn who played it, and keep the place and occasion attached to the account you carry home.
