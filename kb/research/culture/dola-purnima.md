In Madanpur, on the outskirts of Bhubaneswar, Narendra Champati knew where the deity would be eating on the day the new almanac was read. Chandrasekhar Dev would go to Jagasarapatna for *bhog*, the food offered to a visiting deity. On his rounds he also stopped at people’s doors. Families greeted him with colour and a food offering of their own, *chacheri bhoga*. When reporter Rashmi Rekha Das visited Madanpur in 2022, residents described a festival that occupied a fortnight. The reading of the year ahead had its place among all those visits. <sup id="ref-1-1"><a href="#source-1" aria-label="Footnote 1">1</a></sup>

Dola Purnima (ଦୋଳ ପୂର୍ଣ୍ଣିମା) falls on the full moon of Phalguna, in February or March. The *panjika*, often called the *panji* (ପାଞ୍ଜି), is the annual almanac. A person can consult one at home. At Dola, a village can also hear it together. Among the people listening are those who have ploughed for the coming crop, those who will sow it, and those who depend on it for their wages. What they want to know about the year can be as particular as whether there will be rain when the soil needs it. <sup id="ref-2-1"><a href="#source-2" aria-label="Footnote 2">2</a></sup> <sup id="ref-3-1"><a href="#source-3" aria-label="Footnote 3">3</a></sup>

## From the doorstep to the gathering ground

In Madanpur the travelling image is Chandrasekhar Dev, who represents the village’s Nilakantheswar Dev. Residents decorate the *bimana*, the palanquin that carries him, with flowers and mango leaves. Music accompanies the rounds. The movement runs both ways. As the local deity goes visiting, neighbouring villages bring their deities to the *melana padia*, the ground where they meet. Resident Bibudhendra Bidyadhara counted twenty-three visiting villages. He also named the person whose voice people come to hear: the *ganaka*, or astrologer, who reads the new almanac aloud. <sup id="ref-1-2"><a href="#source-1" aria-label="Footnote 1">1</a></sup>

These visits give neighbouring villages a reason to keep different dates. At Harirajpur, on the road between Jatni and Pipili, the four-day *Panchu Dola* gathering begins five days after the full moon. The host is Paschimasambhu Somnath Dev. Here too, households offer food as the visiting palanquins pass. At the gathering, the images are lifted from their swings before the astrologer reads the new almanac. A family could mark the full moon at home and still have Harirajpur’s melana ahead of it. <sup id="ref-4-1"><a href="#source-4" aria-label="Footnote 4">4</a></sup>

## What the reader says, and what the farmer hears

The Jagatsinghpur district gazetteer describes an astrologer explaining the movements of heavenly bodies and the coming solar and lunar eclipses. Historian Prasant Kumar Pradhan records another part of the recitation. On the Phalguna full moon, astrologers addressed elders in the villages traditionally assigned to them. People gathered to hear about the prospect of rain, floods or famine, and could put questions to the reader. An eclipse has a date that can be calculated. The coming monsoon enters the reading as a prediction whose outcome the listeners will learn in their own fields. <sup id="ref-3-2"><a href="#source-3" aria-label="Footnote 3">3</a></sup> <sup id="ref-5-1"><a href="#source-5" aria-label="Footnote 5">5</a></sup>

At Garh Manitri in Khordha, anthropologist Akio Tanabe recorded in 1992 just how much farm work lay behind that moment. The village deities went in procession. The earth was worshipped, and the new calendar read in front of deities and villagers. By then a cultivator was expected to have finished the first two preparatory ploughings. Payments due to labourers and others were also supposed to have been settled. For the person waiting to be paid, the full moon marked a deadline with consequences well before the next rain. <sup id="ref-2-2"><a href="#source-2" aria-label="Footnote 2">2</a></sup>

Tanabe’s village calendar makes a distinction that is easy to miss. Dola closed the lunar year there. Holi, with people throwing colour, came the following day. *Pana Sankranti* in April marked the start of the solar year. So a new panjika could be heard in Phalguna even while the solar new year was still weeks away. <sup id="ref-2-3"><a href="#source-2" aria-label="Footnote 2">2</a></sup>

## One full moon after Agira

At [Agira Purnima](/stories/agira-purnima/), on the preceding full moon of Magha, some farming families watched a straw fire for signs of the coming crop. A month later a reading voice can hold the attention that the flames held. And the calendar varies locally. In Khunta block of Mayurbhanj, a study reported in 2022 described elders watching the direction of flames from burning straw on the **eve of Dola**. They read it for the possibility of drought as they considered when to sow. The researchers placed that practice in Phalguna. Giving it its own date preserves what those families knew about their year. <sup id="ref-6-1"><a href="#source-6" aria-label="Footnote 6">6</a></sup>

There was room to question what the panjika said. A 2017 article in *Down To Earth* quotes Tikeswar Tripathy of Satibhata in Sambalpur district. He remembered being able to rely on its weather predictions, but for more than a decade they had disappointed him. He was talking about weather across the years. For him, the test of the almanac came a long way from its pages, in the rain that actually reached the fields. <sup id="ref-7-1"><a href="#source-7" aria-label="Footnote 7">7</a></sup>

In Astaranga, a March 2026 report in *Dharitri* names what people brought out to Radhamadhab: tender coconut, *lia* (popped rice) and *ukhuda* (sweetened popped rice). At [Nuakhai](/culture/osha/nuakhai/) the first of the new rice is offered before the family tastes it. Here the grain has been popped, sometimes sweetened, and set aside to welcome a guest at the door. <sup id="ref-8-1"><a href="#source-8" aria-label="Footnote 8">8</a></sup>

In [The Year That Feeds Us](/stories/the-year-that-feeds-us/), [Puspuni](/stories/puspuni/) also marks the settling of wages in accounts from western Odisha. Garh Manitri reckoned its obligations by a different full moon. For that village the work after Dola leads towards [Akhi Muthi Anukula](/stories/akhi-muthi-anukula/), when someone will carry seed to the field. By the time that first handful falls, the family has already heard one account of the season ahead. Now it must live through its own.
