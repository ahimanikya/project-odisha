title: "Gamha Purnima: A Meal for the Bullocks"
description: "Seven rice cakes at a tethering post, a plough left idle in Mayurbhanj, and a western Odisha cattle shed whose walls recall the work of the farm."
---

Seven rice cakes were set aside for the post where the bullocks were tethered. Then the bullocks ate them. In Bhabagrahi Misra’s 1966 account of rice farming in the Odisha plains, the household had washed the shed, renewed its earthen plaster and decorated the walls with rice flour mixed with water. The *manda pitha* were made at night. The offering ended with the animals getting a good feed in their own shed. <sup id="ref-1-1"><a href="#source-1" aria-label="Footnote 1">1</a></sup>

The occasion was the full moon of Shravana, known in Odisha as *Gamha Purnima* (ଗହ୍ମା ପୂର୍ଣ୍ଣିମା). It is also observed as the birthday of Balabhadra, or Baladeva, the deity who bears a plough. The implement gives a farming household a familiar way to recognise him. A bullock knows the other end of that implement through the yoke. After the work of opening the ground, farmers honour the animals that supplied the pull. <sup id="ref-1-2"><a href="#source-1" aria-label="Footnote 1">1</a></sup> <sup id="ref-2-1"><a href="#source-2" aria-label="Footnote 2">2</a></sup>

## What goes into a cattle shed

A survey of festivals in western Odisha describes families making pictures on the freshly plastered walls of the shed. They draw bullocks, carts, ploughs and other tools. Then they attend to the living animals. The bullocks are bathed, their horns oiled, a new piece of cloth laid across their backs. Their feed for the occasion includes rice cakes and pulses. The survey calls the day *Gamha Punei*. The survey describes the practice at the regional level. <sup id="ref-2-2"><a href="#source-2" aria-label="Footnote 2">2</a></sup>

A cart on a wall is easier to read if one knows what a cart carries. In a 2023 account for the People’s Archive of Rural India, Madhab Nayak, a student reporter in Gayaganda, Ganjam district, described a bullock cart built of wood and bamboo except for its wheels. It brought paddy home from the fields and sometimes took dung out to them. The writer remembered rice bran and water from cooked rice going into the animals’ feed. The report concerns ordinary work in one southern village. It helps explain how closely the food in a family’s kitchen and the feed in its cattle shed could be connected. <sup id="ref-3-1"><a href="#source-3" aria-label="Footnote 3">3</a></sup>

Misra records a further job for bullocks at threshing time. They trod the sheaves to loosen grain from the stalks. The animals honoured in Shravana could have work to do again when the crop came in. Even the pitha offered at their tethering post was made from a grain their labour helped bring into the house. <sup id="ref-1-3"><a href="#source-1" aria-label="Footnote 1">1</a></sup>

## A full moon without the plough

An account of Santal festivals in Mayurbhanj describes cattle being fed *dhubi* grass, *matkom* and *bulung*, or salt, on Gamha Purnima. People oil the horns and put vermilion on the forehead. The account also records a rule about the day’s work. The plough stays off the ground and people stay out of the fields. Honouring the cattle changes what the household does with its own time. <sup id="ref-4-1"><a href="#source-4" aria-label="Footnote 4">4</a></sup>

The study gives a story for that pause. Kalo and Kuili, an orphaned brother and sister, are sent out by their aunt to work in heavy rain on the festival day. By the time their uncle comes looking for them, they are turning into snakes. In the telling recorded here, the children eventually flow away as two rivers. It is a hard story to give a child and a pointed one to give the adult who decides when others must work. The plough left idle that day is part of a Santal account of obligation that has its own names and characters. <sup id="ref-4-2"><a href="#source-4" aria-label="Footnote 4">4</a></sup>

## A thread for the animal

In a 2012 interview about the festival along the coast, the Jagannath culture scholar Surya Narayan Rath Sharma recalled an observance centred on a *gamha bedi*, an altar prepared by members of the milkmen’s community. Cows were bathed and garlanded there. After an offering to Balaram, protective threads were tied to the animals. Sharma also remembered *enduri* and *chakuli pitha* being offered to cattle. His recollection puts the cow beside another familiar recipient of a rakhi on this full moon, a brother receiving a thread from his sister. <sup id="ref-5-1"><a href="#source-5" aria-label="Footnote 5">5</a></sup>

In Balia Bhagabatpur in Kendrapara district, a dated record shows the day taking a public form. Odisha’s animal resources department records cattle worship, a cattle show, an animal health camp and training for farmers there on 7 August 2017. The camp belongs to that particular gathering. It adds a concrete question to the festival’s expressions of gratitude. Once an animal has been thanked for its work, who will attend to its health? <sup id="ref-6-1"><a href="#source-6" aria-label="Footnote 6">6</a></sup>

Sabita Mishra and her co-authors record a household detail in *Odisha Review* that gives the meal its order. Men and women collect grass and flowers. Women prepare rice cakes, *kheer* and *khechuri*. The animals eat before the family does. For a day, the people who usually decide when a team must work arrange their own meal around the animals. <sup id="ref-7-1"><a href="#source-7" aria-label="Footnote 7">7</a></sup>

## The other worker in the furrow

In [Akhi Muthi Anukula](/stories/akhi-muthi-anukula/), the householder carries the seed, while the ploughman brings the bullocks to open the furrows. Read that scene again after Gamha Purnima and a different participant comes into view. Someone has fed the pair, harnessed them and walked at their pace. At [Chitau Amavasya](/stories/chitau-amavasya/) on the new moon of Shravana, a family’s prayer follows bare feet into the wet paddy. At the full moon, the family’s attention can turn to the animals that worked that ground.

Elsewhere in the series, [Raja](/culture/osha/raja-parba/) marks a rest for the earth, [Garbhana Sankranti](/stories/garbhana-sankranti/) attends to the grain forming in the plant, and [Nuakhai](/culture/osha/nuakhai/) asks who receives the first new rice. [Manabasa Gurubara](/culture/osha/manabasa-gurubara/) brings that question of sharing into Lakshmi’s household. Gamha Purnima gives one answer in the cattle shed. A portion of the rice goes back to the bullocks.
