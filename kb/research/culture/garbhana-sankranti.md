In Basudevpur, in Odisha’s Bhadrak district, the first stop on the way to the rice field was the manure heap beside the house. In a local report from October 2020, farmers gathered bamboo twigs, branches from several trees and a plant from the paddy field. After worship at home they set a stalk in the heap. Only then did they go out among the rice, plant another, remember Lakshmi and bow to the earth. Even the manure that fed the crop had a place in the day’s devotions. <sup id="ref-1-1"><a href="#source-1" aria-label="Footnote 1">1</a></sup>

The occasion was *Garbhana Sankranti* (ଗର୍ଭଣା ସଂକ୍ରାନ୍ତି), also called Tula Sankranti. Its name gives the standing rice a condition familiar to any household. The paddy is expecting. There is grain to hope for, and still some waiting to do. That interval has a ritual of its own. <sup id="ref-2-1"><a href="#source-2" aria-label="Footnote 2">2</a></sup>

## What goes out to the field

The Basudevpur report follows the offering closely enough for us to see how it could travel. After worship, the participants wrapped the offering in taro leaves to make small packets. It included banana, jaggery, milk and the kernel from inside a palm seed. Women and children joined in the field. The bamboo, guava and fig branches gathered that morning are details of this particular account. A reader looking for a single tree called *garbhana* could miss what these families actually carried. <sup id="ref-1-2"><a href="#source-1" aria-label="Footnote 1">1</a></sup>

In Nirakarpur panchayat in Khordha district and in neighbouring panchayats, an October 2025 report in the Odia newspaper *Sambad* draws attention to something else. Farmers cooked at the *bila huḍā*, the raised bank that borders a paddy plot. The report calls the offering *jau* in its headline and describes the preparation of *khiri*, a rice pudding, for Lakshmi. A field bank usually gives a person somewhere to stand or walk between plots. Here it also gave the cooks a place to make an offering beside the growing rice. In Basudevpur we can follow a leaf packet out from the house. In Nirakarpur we can follow the cooking itself as far as the field. <sup id="ref-3-1"><a href="#source-3" aria-label="Footnote 3">3</a></sup>

An older account helps explain why the journey mattered. Writing about rice rituals in the Odisha plains, Bhabagrahi Misra distinguished two successive solar observances. Kanya Sankranti marked the beginning of the crop’s imagined pregnancy. A month later, at Tula Sankranti, people prayed for the ear of rice to emerge safely. In the sequence he recorded, a household set a *garbhana* branch in its courtyard on the previous day, sprinkled it with water mixed with rice flour, and offered a cake made of rice and black gram along with a vegetable curry. The branch then went into the paddy. The prayer made over it at home continued out in the field. <sup id="ref-2-2"><a href="#source-2" aria-label="Footnote 2">2</a></sup>

A survey of festivals in western Odisha also records Garbhana, describing offerings to Lakshmi among the ripening plants. Its account gives a regional setting but leaves the village unnamed. The two coastal reports let us see differences between particular places. The western survey tells us how its authors understood the occasion, while leaving open what a family in any one western district prepared or carried. <sup id="ref-4-1"><a href="#source-4" aria-label="Footnote 4">4</a></sup>

## A meal for the season

What was in Misra’s vegetable curry? He leaves that detail open. Food writer Sujata Dehury gives a more recent answer from her own cooking in a 2024 account of Garbhana. Her *ghanta* brings vegetables, sprouted pulses and coconut together in one pot. Pumpkin can go in alongside plantain and tubers. After cooking comes the pleasure of sending portions to neighbours. Read beside Misra, her account gives us two distinct glimpses of festival food, a curry offered beside a branch and a cook who remembers the occasion through a dish she can share. <sup id="ref-5-1"><a href="#source-5" aria-label="Footnote 5">5</a></sup>

## The pot at the centre of Garba

Look across to Gujarat during Navaratri and the movement changes. At Garba, people sing, clap and move in a circle around a perforated earthen vessel containing an oil lamp, or around an image of the goddess Amba. Light passes through the openings as the dancers go round. UNESCO describes the dance as worship of Shakti, feminine divine energy. In her account of Garba, Shrinkhla Sahai connects its name with *garbha*, the womb, and reads the lamp inside the vessel as an image of life developing within it. <sup id="ref-6-1"><a href="#source-6" aria-label="Footnote 6">6</a></sup> <sup id="ref-7-1"><a href="#source-7" aria-label="Footnote 7">7</a></sup>

That reading brings Gujarat’s pot close to Odisha’s standing paddy. In one, people gather around a light the clay shelters. In the other, they bring food and prayers to a plant whose ear has yet to emerge. Each tradition supplies its own reason for the gathering. Their resemblance gives us a way to read the two images together. A common historical origin would need evidence of a different kind.

At Nuakhai, a family can take new grain in hand and decide whom to offer it to first. Garbhana asks something else of a household while its crop is still growing. The rice everyone hopes to eat is still out there. For now, it is the household that takes a meal to the field.
