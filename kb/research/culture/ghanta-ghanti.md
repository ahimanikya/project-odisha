# Ghanta and ghanti: the hands behind the ringing

Imagine standing at the edge of a procession. A metallic pulse reaches you before you can see who is playing. Your attention turns towards it. Somewhere inside that ringing is a pair of hands—and before those hands, another pair that shaped the metal.

This is an imagined opening, not a recorded encounter. It offers a way into a real question: when we recognise a sound as Odisha's, how often do we remember its maker?

## A bell is not every ringing thing

The November 2004 *Orissa Review* article “Metal Craft of Orissa” distinguishes **ghanta**, a gong, among beaten-metal products, from **ghanti**, a bell, among cast brass objects. Its broader ritual passage loosely pairs the English labels; the specific product descriptions give the clearer distinction. These are useful starting terms, not a guarantee that every speaker uses them identically.[^ghanta-metalcraft2004]

Ask to see the object. Ask what its player calls it. A name without a shape, a place or a person can flatten a whole musical world.

## The sound has a working life

The NID resource by Bibhudutta Baral and Antony William connects this craft with Kansari metalworkers and documents tools such as hammers, wooden mallets and chisels. Its account places ritual gongs, paired manjira discs and cattle bells within a wider world of metalwork.[^ghanta-dsource]

That is a lovely change of perspective. The familiar ringing becomes a reason to notice craft. Who made this particular instrument? Was it cast, hammered or repaired? What does the player look for when choosing one? Those are questions for a maker, rather than answers we can supply on their behalf.

## From worship to a gathering of players

The 2004 article describes ghanta and ghanti during arati and food offerings, and rhythmic gong playing during the Car Festival. It is a dated account of practice, not a present-day timetable or a rule for every temple.[^ghanta-metalcraft2004]

Elsewhere, the Kalahandi district culture table identifies **Ghanta Vadya** around Bhawanipatna and Kesinga: group playing at auspicious events. Its listed troupe range is 16–20. That local description does not prescribe the size of every ensemble in Odisha or count how many groups exist today.[^daskathia-kalahandi]

One object in worship, many players in company: follow the occasion as well as the instrument. The story changes with the people using it.

## Listen for the people, too

Odisha State Museum's musical-instrument introduction places temple bells and ghanti within its interpretation of the state's cultural soundscape. This is the museum's cultural framing, rather than evidence that ringing produces a measurable spiritual or medical effect.[^ghanta-museum]

For your own visit, try three small acts of attention:

- Notice the moment the ringing begins and what happens around it.
- Watch how players coordinate; ask afterwards how they learn their parts.
- Ask a maker or player which detail they wish visitors would notice.

Follow the host's directions. Request permission before touching an instrument, photographing a maker or recording a ceremony. An invitation to listen is not automatically an invitation to interrupt.

## A sound worth carrying home

A useful souvenir might be a name remembered correctly: the maker, the player, the local word for an instrument. If a conversation is welcome, let that be the beginning of the story you share.

We have not established an invention date or a single origin for these instruments. What the sources do give us is a connection worth following: ritual, shared performance and skilled work meeting in a sound.
