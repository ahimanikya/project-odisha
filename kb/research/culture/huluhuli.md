# Huluhuli (ହୁଳହୁଳି): the sacred sound of Odisha

By Ahimanikya Satapathy

How does a moving tongue become a voice of welcome? Follow huluhuli through Odia words, wedding thresholds and the people who give the sound its meaning.

Huluhuli begins with the tongue. Its rapid movement turns a sustained voice into a rippling trill; opening the mouth alone does not produce it. The Odia name, also written hulahuli, belongs to the wider family of sounds called ululation.[^huluhuli-praharaj-9190][^huluhuli-indiavideo]

At a wedding arrival or a gathering for worship, that voice can welcome someone, express joy or mark an auspicious moment. Understanding huluhuli means listening for both the movement that makes the sound and the occasion that gives it meaning.[^huluhuli-thurston-1906][^huluhuli-joshi-2013]

## The tongue, the word and the occasion

Calling huluhuli a “cry” catches its intensity but can miss its intention. Calling it a “song” can suggest words and melody that are not required. Ululation describes the broad kind of sound; huluhuli names it within an Odia cultural world.

Gopal Chandra Praharaj gives the tongue a precise place in that description. His Purnachandra Odia Bhashakosha, published in 1931–1940, records ହୁଳହୁଳୀ as a shrill vocal sound made as women move the tongue inside the mouth. The accompanying verb, ହୁଳହୁଳୀ ଦେବା, describes making a vibrating sound with the tongue. Voice and movement work together.

The dictionary marks the word as imitative of a sound. That describes how a word sounds; it does not tell us who invented the practice. Its headword has a long final ī, while this article uses the familiar spelling ହୁଳହୁଳି; the same page also records the variant ହୁଳାହୁଳୀ. Following those forms matters when looking for the practice in older writing.

Praharaj records another meaning in Garhjat usage: festive occasions such as marriage. In that entry, the word can name the occasion as well as its sound. The relationship helps explain why a merely acoustic description feels incomplete: huluhuli belongs to a social moment, with people who recognise what it is doing.[^huluhuli-praharaj-9190]

The entry also pairs men’s haribol with women’s huluhuli at celebrations. That is a gendered account from the dictionary’s own period, rather than a rule for every household today.[^huluhuli-praharaj-9190]

In worship, the sound carries devotional meaning. Calling it sacred describes that meaning; it does not establish a physical power to purify a room or heal the body.[^huluhuli-joshi-2013]

## An old tradition without a known birthday

Who first made huluhuli? Its inventor, starting century and birthplace remain unknown. A written record can show that people already practised it, without telling us how it began.

In Edgar Thurston’s 1906 Ethnographic Notes in Southern India, women of the bride’s party make huluhuli as the groom reaches the house. Conch shells and recited verses accompany his arrival. The passage describes marriage among Badhoyis and other communities in Ganjam.[^huluhuli-thurston-1906]

This puts the named practice in a Ganjam marriage setting by 1906. It is an attestation, not a birth date. The colonial account’s terminology and social classifications also need to be read in their period; one description cannot stand for every community in Odisha.

There is a literary trail too. Under ହୁଳାହୁଳୀ, Praharaj cites Jagannath’s Bhagabata in a scene with women, lamps and huluhuli along a path. The dictionary does not identify the canto or verse. Without a checked edition and its textual history, the passage cannot yet give us a secure earlier date.[^huluhuli-praharaj-9190]

## From the wedding threshold to worship

The Ganjam account places the sound at a threshold: someone arriving is being received. The women’s voices are part of that welcome, alongside the conch and the spoken verses.[^huluhuli-thurston-1906]

Household worship gives the voice another purpose. Dina Krishna Joshi’s account of Sabitri Brata places hulahuli alongside haribol in religious gatherings. The observance recalls Savitri and Satyaban, and the recovery of a husband’s life from Yama. This is the devotional setting of the sound, not a story of its invention.[^huluhuli-joshi-2013]

Srinibash Samal’s 2019 cultural essay also associates huluhuli with Manabasa worship in Margasira and with Khudurukuni observances. His account offers a personal view of these connections, rather than a survey of every household.[^huluhuli-samal-2019]

At a ceremony, a useful question is specific: what does huluhuli mean here, and why is it made at this moment? A family’s answer may reveal more than a general description of an auspicious sound.

## Odisha contains more than one sound tradition

Huluhuli is also named in a government description of Gadaba Dhemsa. The Ministry of Tribal Affairs' ADI KALAKAR entry for a 2022 dance festival says the dance begins with a chorus of hulahuli, alongside its description of women's group dancing and instrumental accompaniment.[^huluhuli-dhemsa-2022]

A festival description opens another setting for the sound, but does not establish the performers’ preferred term in every village or make Gadaba practice identical to coastal Odia worship.

Who leads the sound? Does it invoke, signal a beginning or express joy? Which language do the performers use to explain it? The differences matter as much as the resemblance.

## Women's voices and the work of celebration

In the marriage and worship accounts, women help make an occasion auspicious through their voices. They are participants in its making, not simply people watching it happen.[^huluhuli-thurston-1906][^huluhuli-joshi-2013]

That contribution deserves recognition without making the skill a test of womanhood or Odia identity. Someone who cannot ululate has not failed to belong.

Nor is the history entirely about spontaneous joy. In her 2005 account of the Ranpur Prajamandala movement, historian Binodini Das mentions Hulahuli Jagir, explaining it as land granted for performing hulahuli. The article places this institution within a discussion of obligations and oppressive practices under princely rule.[^huluhuli-ranpur-2005]

The land-grant records behind that account still need examination. Even so, Hulahuli Jagir raises questions that a celebration alone might conceal: who had to provide the sound, under whose authority, and on what terms?

## A sound that travels with people

A voice travels without an instrument case. In her Michigan chapter report for the Odia community magazine Utkarsa in 2013, Swati Mishra records sankha and hulahuli at a Ganesh Puja gathering. A sound associated with home had a place in a celebration overseas.[^huluhuli-osa-2013]

A different public setting appears in a 2015 OrissaPOST report: a hulahuli competition at Ekamra Haat in Bhubaneswar. The report connects participation with assertions of Odia identity. Here the sound becomes something to perform for an audience and compare with others.[^huluhuli-identity-2015]

These two events show different uses of a familiar practice. They cannot tell us whether participation in everyday rituals is growing or declining; that would need accounts from many more households and communities.

## Learning a voice and knowing when to use it

India Video lists related regional names: uruli in Assam, kulavai in Tamil and kurava in Kerala. Resemblance opens a useful question: how does someone learn both the movement and the moment when it belongs?[^huluhuli-indiavideo]

An account from another region makes the distinction concrete. Reporting fieldwork in Jordan, Syria, Lebanon and Egypt in 2004, anthropologist Jennifer E. Jacobs observed young children imitating ululation from people and media around them. Some adults described the skill as instinctive; others recalled private practice. One woman described rehearsing tongue movements in front of a mirror.

Jacobs also heard men disclaim knowledge in interviews, then observed young men ululating at concerts and public gatherings. Their performances could be treated as playful while women’s were treated as genuine. Physical ability and social permission were different things.[^huluhuli-jacobs-2004]

Jacobs was studying Arabic zaghareet, not Odia children learning huluhuli. The comparison suggests a question to ask locally: who teaches the movement, and who teaches when to join? A recording of the voice cannot answer both.

Odisha’s place in this wider family of voices remains a historical question. Similar techniques do not establish that traders carried huluhuli along a specific maritime route, or that one ancient culture gave it to all the others.

## How to listen when you visit

If you encounter huluhuli at an invited wedding, a public cultural performance or a permitted ritual, give it the attention you would give an unfamiliar language. Notice when it begins, which voices join, and what changes around it.

Ask a willing participant what the sound means in that setting. Let them choose whether to demonstrate it. Ask before recording, and follow the rules of the household, venue or shrine. A private ceremony is not automatically a visitor experience.

A useful recording keeps the performer’s explanation with the sound: their preferred name for it, the occasion, locality, language and permission for reuse. A generic sound effect cannot give us that context.

The few seconds of a trill are only part of what there is to hear. The person who makes it can tell us how they learned, when they join in and what the occasion means to them.
