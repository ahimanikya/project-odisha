## A faith expressed through poetry

Mahima Swami, also called Mahima Gosain, is described as the movement’s founder; Bhima Bhoi gave its ideas a widely remembered poetic voice. The existing literary introduction remains the person’s canonical narrative. This extension adds historical context and [credited reading routes](../works/stuti-chintamani.md).

### Alekha and the formless divine

Mahapatra’s monograph explains **Alekha** as indescribable and **nirakara** as formless, alongside other names for the divine. Its discussion of **sunya** belongs to a particular religious and literary interpretation. The use of a shared term does not make Mahima Dharma identical to Buddhist philosophy, nor establish one undisputed origin. ## Bhima Bhoi reading introduction · 20 October 2026

Whose suffering should a prayer ask to end? Begin there.

### A basket at the guru’s feet

In the English selection from Stuti Chintamani’s twenty-seventh boli, or canto, printed in Sitakant Mahapatra’s study, sins become a burden carried in a basket to the guru’s feet. The plea then widens to other devotees and finally to the suffering world. The speaker is willing to face damnation if others can be redeemed.

Read the whole selection before its final appeal. Where does the request move beyond the speaker?

The prayer belongs to Mahima Dharma. Mahapatra explains Alekha as the indescribable divine and nirakara as formless. These terms help locate the appeal within a faith, rather than treating it as a free-standing slogan.

### Find a passage you can return to

The National Book Trust’s 2026 catalogue describes Stuti Chintamani as a work of 100 cantos, each with 20 stanzas. It credits Satyananda Swain as the English translator of Gems of Soulful Prayer: Stuti Chintamani of Bhima Bhoi, published in 2024. That edition offers a longer reading route than the selections in Mahapatra’s monograph.

Keep the translator’s name with the words you read. If two English versions seem different, set one short passage beside the other and mark the choices: who is addressed, what burden is named, and whose relief is sought? A comparison becomes useful when you can point to the words that prompted it.

### A remembered life is not a settled biography

Mahapatra opens his biography with uncertainty about Bhima Bhoi’s exact birth date, birthplace and blindness. The poetry cannot settle those questions simply because its speaker describes suffering.

Johannes Beltz makes this distinction central to his study of the poet’s remembered life. During fieldwork in western Odisha in 2000–2001, he collected stories from ascetics and lay people. He reads miraculous birth stories as evidence of how people honoured Bhima Bhoi, rather than as verified accounts of his birth.

That leaves two worthwhile questions open: what can a document establish about a life, and what does a story reveal about the people who tell it? Keeping them separate lets us take a tradition seriously without converting every remembered event into a historical fact.

### Followers have read him differently

Beltz also describes a disagreement over the authority of Bhima Bhoi’s writings. In the Joranda dispute he examines, Kaupinadharis treated the texts as a code to follow; the Balkaladhari position he cites accepted them as religious books alongside others. This is a specific historical account, not a description of every follower’s position today. It shows why the same poetry can matter differently to different communities.

### Khaliapali and the life around the poems

Mahapatra places Bhima Bhoi’s later activities at Khaliapali and credits Ma Annapurna with supervising its ashram from 1877. Remembering her makes room for the work of sustaining a community around the poet.

The photograph here shows the Khaliapali memorial in 2012. It is a later place of remembrance, not a portrait of Bhima Bhoi or a reconstruction of his lifetime. The photographer, Pdhmishra, released the image under CC0.
