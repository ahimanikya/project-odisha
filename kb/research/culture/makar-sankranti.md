title: "Makar Sankranti: A Bowl of Rice for the Sun and a Friend"

When Sangram Keshari Senapati explains how to make *makara chaula* (ମକର ଚାଉଳ), he tells the cook when to stop grinding. The new rice has soaked for several hours. It should still have a little grit to it. Into that goes ripe banana, grated ginger, sesame, milk and *khai*, popped paddy. Someone who likes sugarcane can cut a little into the bowl. Two forms of the harvest meet in a spoonful: the soft grain that still offers some bite and the light grain that has already popped. <sup id="ref-1-1"><a href="#source-1" aria-label="Footnote 1">1</a></sup>

In Odisha, this is one way to prepare the offering for Makar Sankranti, the mid-January solar observance marking the sun’s passage into Makara, or Capricorn. The rice goes into the bowl uncooked. The family that makes it may set it before a household deity, carry it into a temple or offer it to the morning sun. Where the bowl goes next can matter as much as what the cook puts into it. In one Sambalpur village, sharing it gives two people a new name for each other. <sup id="ref-2-1"><a href="#source-2" aria-label="Footnote 2">2</a></sup> <sup id="ref-3-1"><a href="#source-3" aria-label="Footnote 3">3</a></sup> <sup id="ref-4-1"><a href="#source-4" aria-label="Footnote 4">4</a></sup>

## The first portion in Mayurbhanj

A report from Baripada records a short ingredient list for households in Mayurbhanj: raw rice, grated coconut, banana and sugar. Early in the morning the family offers the mixture to the sun. Then everyone in the household eats. Rice cakes are made for the festival too, but this particular offering takes no turn on the griddle. A family can make it simply and still observe the part of the preparation that a recipe might leave out: who eats first. <sup id="ref-3-2"><a href="#source-3" aria-label="Footnote 3">3</a></sup>

In Chandua village, Phulamani Mohanta described another part of the day to the same reporter. Girls of her Kudumi-Mohanta community worship Tusu Devi and sing as they take her image to a river or tank for immersion. The story she told was about Tusu. The account of the rice offered to the sun came from the reporter’s description of households across the district. Both belong in a picture of Makar in Mayurbhanj, and each has its own participants and recipient. <sup id="ref-3-3"><a href="#source-3" aria-label="Footnote 3">3</a></sup>

## A friend in Themera, a friend in Jagatsinghpur

In Themera, Sambalpur district, Bashudev Mahapatra remembered his grandparents talking about the festival. When he was eighty, he explained to a reporter that two people who shared makara chaula on Sankranti could become lifelong friends. Each was the other’s *makar*. Someone accepting a share of the new rice was also accepting a relationship that would last well beyond that year’s harvest. <sup id="ref-4-2"><a href="#source-4" aria-label="Footnote 4">4</a></sup>

Themera gives the friendship a public celebration the following day. Mahapatra remembered two chariots setting out from opposite ends of the village, one bearing Krishna and one Shiva. They came to face each other in the middle, where the village head offered worship to both. In his telling of *Hari-Hara Milan*, the gods meet as friends much as the villagers themselves have done. <sup id="ref-4-3"><a href="#source-4" aria-label="Footnote 4">4</a></sup>

On the coast, the Jagatsinghpur district gazetteer records villagers gathering at a nearby temple to exchange makara chaula as a pledge of enduring friendship. Its list of ingredients includes ghee, milk, sesame and molasses alongside rice, coconut and banana. The resemblance to Themera lies in what two people do with the offering. The ingredients and the gathering place belong to the coastal account. <sup id="ref-5-1"><a href="#source-5" aria-label="Footnote 5">5</a></sup>

## What goes into the bowl

Sujata Dehury, who comes from western Odisha, drew attention to a quieter ingredient when she spoke about the winter harvest. Along with new rice she uses split, husked mung beans. She described the preparation as *bhog*, food set aside for a deity. Other versions recorded in the same account include sugarcane, chhena or curd. In Senapati’s kitchen notes there is room to choose the fruit, the sweetener and whether to put in sugarcane at all. These are decisions a cook can make while keeping the rice and the act of offering at the centre of the dish. <sup id="ref-6-1"><a href="#source-6" aria-label="Footnote 6">6</a></sup> <sup id="ref-1-2"><a href="#source-1" aria-label="Footnote 1">1</a></sup>

At Puri’s Jagannath temple, the rice has a route of its own. An account of the temple festivals in *Orissa Review* describes the offering being carried around the temple in a procession called *Makara bedha*. Another account lists ginger and black pepper among the ingredients mixed with moistened fresh rice for the deities. A bowl that travels only from a kitchen to a household shrine and a temple offering carried in procession can both bear the name *makara chaula*. <sup id="ref-7-1"><a href="#source-7" aria-label="Footnote 7">7</a></sup> <sup id="ref-8-1"><a href="#source-8" aria-label="Footnote 8">8</a></sup>

## Three ways to share new rice

At [Nuakhai](/culture/osha/nuakhai/), families offer the first grain before anyone tastes the new rice together. At [Puspuni](/stories/puspuni/), on the full moon of Pausa, children in western Odisha call from house to house for contributions to a common meal. A farm worker hired for the agricultural year might take home paddy on that day as wages. Makar Sankranti follows the solar calendar, so its place relative to Puspuni can shift. In Themera the act of sharing has a purpose of its own. The person who receives the rice may be the person one will call *makar* for life. <sup id="ref-4-4"><a href="#source-4" aria-label="Footnote 4">4</a></sup>

The rice has already had a long journey through [The Year That Feeds Us](/stories/the-year-that-feeds-us/). At [Akhi Muthi Anukula](/stories/akhi-muthi-anukula/) someone takes seed from the house to the field. [Raja](/culture/osha/raja-parba/) gives the earth a rest. [Chitau Amavasya](/stories/chitau-amavasya/) remembers the people who stand in the flooded paddy, and [Gamha Purnima](/stories/gamha-purnima/) the bullocks that work beside them. At [Garbhana Sankranti](/stories/garbhana-sankranti/) the grain is still forming. At [Manabasa Gurubara](/culture/osha/manabasa-gurubara/) the measure for that grain has a place in Lakshmi’s worship. By Makar, someone can take a portion of it, soak it, sweeten it and carry it to a friend.
