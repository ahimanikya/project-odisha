“ଅତି ଲେମ୍ବୁ ଚିପୁଡ଼ିଲେ ପିତା ।” Squeeze a lemon too much, and it turns bitter.[^lemon]

A small sentence, an ordinary fruit, a familiar action. Yet the thought travels well beyond the kitchen. We can press a request until goodwill gives way to resentment. We can pursue one more advantage until we spoil what we already have. The saying brings those possibilities into view through something we can almost taste.

This is one of the pleasures of Odia Dhaga. A thought arrives with a picture inside it. The listener participates: lemon becomes insistence, bitterness becomes the consequence, and an everyday action becomes a way of examining our conduct. In that movement from one experience to another, we encounter the work of literature.

I have lived outside Odisha for thirty-five years. The language and the life it carries have stayed with me. My concern now is how we help that connection continue for someone else. When an expression stops passing between people, we stand to lose more than a sentence. We may lose a particular way of noticing, teasing, consoling or questioning.

Grammar helps us understand how a language is put together. Living with a language also teaches us what people can do with it: how an image changes an argument, how a pause makes a joke, how affection or anger alters the same words. Dhaga offer a particularly vivid entrance into that life.

## Poetry before we call it poetry

In *The Question That Brought Me Here*, I wrote about encountering poetry before identifying it as poetry: through a melody, a memorable voice, or a story that enters family conversation.[^question] Dhaga invite a similar encounter. A listener can enjoy the sound or recognise the situation before asking what kind of literature it is.

Consider this expression:

> ବଣ ପୋଡ଼ିଲେ ଦୁନିଆ ଜାଣେ, ମନ ପୋଡ଼ିଲେ କେହୀ ନ ଜାଣେ
>
> When the forest burns, the world knows; when the heart burns, no one knows.[^fire]

The two halves hold the same shape. ବଣ and ମନ draw forest and inner life close through sound; the repeated burning carries us from a visible blaze to pain that may remain unseen. The second half changes what we understand from the first. “No one knows” gives the feeling its force, without requiring us to believe that care or understanding is impossible.

Here is compression, parallelism and metaphor working together. A private feeling becomes imaginable through a public scene. We do not need to turn every saying into a formal poem to recognise its literary power.

Another Dhaga works like a tiny play:

> ଠାକୁର ଘରେ କିଏ ରେ? ନାଁ ମୁଁ କଦଳୀ ଖାଇନାହିଁ
>
> Who is in the shrine room? No, I haven’t eaten the bananas.[^bananas]

The question asks who is there. The reply introduces bananas that nobody mentioned. Between those two voices, a comic situation appears. The listener supplies what the speaker may wish to conceal. An unnecessary explanation becomes the centre of the joke.

The humour lies in that mismatch; a defensive reply alone would not establish anyone’s guilt. Its literary interest is how much of a scene can be suggested with so few words. Speech becomes character, and the answer gives away more than it intends.

The collection includes proverbs, rhymed sayings and idiomatic expressions. Their forms differ. What these examples offer is a way to hear the craft within familiar speech, rather than treating every entry as an interchangeable piece of advice.

## Culture lives in the occasion

The wording matters, but so does the moment in which it is spoken. The lemon saying could be a gentle reminder to ourselves. Directed sharply at someone asking for help, it could become a rebuff. Knowing the words does not settle whether their use is generous, fair or appropriate.

Learning an expression therefore involves learning a relationship: who can say it to whom, in what tone, and with what possible effect. A translation can explain the picture. A conversation can help us understand its place.

This is what I mean by culture embedded in language. The fruit, the fire and the answering voice give us more than vocabulary. They offer ways of making experience shareable. A saying lets a speaker approach a difficult thought indirectly; a listener may recognise the point, laugh at it, resist it, or turn it back on the speaker.

In *Odia Cultural Identity and Pride*, I argued that pride grows through familiarity—through names we can use, stories we understand and skills we can practise.[^identity] Dhaga give that thought a modest, practical beginning. We can learn one expression well enough to explain it, recognise a fitting occasion, and ask when its advice might fail.

## What we fear losing

I worry about these expressions fading from everyday use. That concern is a reason to listen and make room for them; it is not a survey of how all Odia families speak. Different households, generations and places will have different experiences. We should hear those differences before telling a single story of loss.

A collection helps us keep wording and sources available. Continued use asks something more of us. Who will explain the joke? Who remembers another version? Who can show a learner why a line is tender in one setting and cutting in another?

There is also a printed history to investigate carefully. A version of the lemon saying appears in the 1893 *ଢଗମାଳିକା, ପ୍ରଥମଭାଗ*, whose title page identifies Gobinda Rath as publisher and Victoria Press, Cuttack.[^history] That is evidence of the expression in print by that date. It does not tell us who first said it, and the publisher’s name should not become an invented author’s name.

Keeping such distinctions is part of caring for the inheritance. A remembered family version and a dated printed witness can both matter while telling us different things.

## Give the words somewhere to live

*A Place to Learn Together* makes room for reading, listening, questions and disagreement.[^learning] Dhaga fit naturally into that kind of gathering. Read one aloud. Explain the literal picture. Invite someone to describe an occasion when it might be used. Let another person suggest an occasion when it would be unfair or unhelpful.

A child’s objection can be a good beginning. Does more effort always spoil a result? Of course not. The lemon saying asks us to consider proportion; it does not relieve us of judging the situation. A learner who questions its use is already thinking with the language.

At home, a small word notebook could keep the expression beside the name of the person who shared it and their account of when they would say it. With permission, a recording could preserve their pronunciation and rhythm. If another person knows a different version, keep that beside it rather than correcting it away without investigation.

Older expressions also deserve judgement. Familiarity and age cannot excuse cruelty or prejudice. We can study an inherited expression while choosing not to use it against someone. Passing on culture includes showing how to question what we inherit.

Project Odisha’s [*Words We Carry* collection](https://projectodisha.org/dhaga/) brings together 159 illustrated expressions with Odia and English explanations and source notes. It offers a place to begin reading and listening. The next step can be small: choose one saying, hear its sound, consider its image, and bring it into a conversation where it belongs.

I want a learner to feel the pleasure of that discovery—the moment when familiar words reveal another meaning, or a short line holds more life than its length suggests. That pleasure gives us a reason to return. And returning gives the language another occasion to be spoken.
