# Odia lullabies and the songs of childhood

Imagine someone reaching the end of a little song, then beginning again because the child is still awake. The words need not become more elaborate. A familiar voice, returning to a familiar phrase, is enough to hold the moment. This is an invitation to picture an ordinary act of care, not an account of a family we visited.

Odia lullabies offer an intimate way into Sounds of Odisha. They lead us from the public sound of a festival towards childhood song, household memory and the books that carry some of those words beyond the people who first sang them to us.

## Songs for more than sleep

The name *nanabaya gita* reaches beyond bedtime. In his 2022 discussion of Odia oral narratives, Pratap Kumar Dash places these songs around feeding, comforting and play as well as sleep. He distinguishes inherited oral material from compositions associated with named writers. His account also notes that similar beginnings can lead into different words.[^lullabies-dash2022]

That gives us a useful question to ask of any remembered song: what was happening when it was sung? A tune recalled from being rocked to sleep may have a different place in another person's memory. Before calling one version correct, ask whose version it is. The occasion belongs beside the words.

## A book with room for a child's world

Nandakishore Bala provides a named literary connection. The Odia Virtual Academy identifies the poet with Kusupur in Cuttack and describes *Nana Baya Geeta* among his contributions to children's literature. Its account connects his writing with local speech and rural life.[^lullabies-ova-bala]

The Academy's digitised *Nanabaya Gita* has two parts. Its contents range across birds, rain, play, study and sleep. Near the end of the second part, *Nida Mausi* calls on personified sleep to care for a child named Hema, stay through dreams and leave when the crow calls. The poem makes an abstract thing—sleep—someone who can be invited close.[^lullabies-ova-nanabaya1955]

Read it as a particular literary text. A household recollection with another name, opening or sequence deserves its own record; this edition cannot stand for every family's song. Nor can a book alone tell us the tune, pace or gesture used by a particular singer.

## Keep the edition separate from the origin

The Academy catalogues its copy under 1955, with Goswami Press, Cuttack, as publishing house. That is a date attached to the catalogued book. It does not date the beginning of Odia lullabies.[^lullabies-ova-nanabaya1955]

The wider history needs several kinds of evidence: identifiable editions, the wording of particular poems, and accounts from people about where they learned a song. A familiar opening is a starting point for comparison, not enough by itself to settle authorship. The research notes retain unresolved dating and attribution questions rather than turn them into a tidy founding story.

## Writers listen to childhood too

Children's literature continues through deliberate craft as well as memory. In an interview published in *Lokaratna* in 2021, Madhabananda Panda recalled an editor encouraging him to choose simpler words. He described nature and people's lives as material for his writing, and explained his interest in birds and animals. The accompanying biography lists a collection called *Nanabaya Gita* among his works.[^lullabies-panda2021]

The shared title is a reason to check an author's name, not merge two books. Panda's interview concerns his writing for children, including older children; it should not be treated as an explanation of every infant lullaby. It does, however, leave a lovely question for the reader: how does a writer choose words a child will want to hear again?

## Make room for a remembered version

If someone in your family would like to share a childhood song, begin with the person. Ask who sang it to them, where they remember hearing it, and whether it belonged to sleep, a game or another everyday moment. Write down their wording before trying to standardise it. If a word is unfamiliar, ask what it means to them.

Keep recollection and certainty separate. “I remember my grandmother singing this” is valuable testimony. It does not establish who composed it or how old it is. A remembered fragment can remain a fragment; nobody needs to supply missing lines to make it fit a book.

Let the person decide whether the memory stays private. Ask separately before recording or sharing a voice, name or family detail, especially where children are involved. These are suggestions for a willing conversation, not evidence of interviews conducted by Project Odisha.

The next sound to notice may be very small: the point where a singer repeats a phrase, pauses, or changes a word for one child. Keep that detail with the person who explains it. It may tell a richer story than an unsupported claim that everyone has always sung the same song.
