# Binayaka Brata

Twenty-one sweets become a small map of giving: one for Ganesha, ten for a Brahmin and ten for the person observing the vow. A recovered Binayaka booklet makes this division explicit. It offers a way to explore devotion through food, while a separate government record takes the printed history of a same-titled work back to 1936.

**Narrative checkpoint: an Odia booklet and a separate historical catalogue record inspected; origin, edition dating and local preparation remain open.**

## The annual vow and its neighbours

Dash lists Binayaka Brata in Bhadrab as Ganesh worship. Mahanti places annual Ganesh Puja on Bhadrava’s bright fourth and distinguishes it from recurring bright-fortnight Binayak and dark-fortnight Sankati vows. Their similar names do not establish identical menus or fasting rules.

## A booklet with more than one calendar passage

The recovered booklet opens with Bhadraba bright-fortnight Chaturthi and a rule involving midday. Its later katha also names other months, including Srabana and Margasira. These passages need fuller textual comparison before their calendar relationships can be explained. No annual Gregorian date is calculated here.

## Yudhishthira asks how to recover what was lost

The katha frames its teaching through Suta’s narration to sages. Yudhishthira asks Krishna which deity he should worship to overcome his troubles and regain his kingdom. Krishna directs him to Ganapati and explains the vow. The text draws on a wider sacred world of exemplary devotees, then says Yudhishthira followed the teaching and recovered his kingdom. This is a devotional account of the vow’s power, not evidence that these events occurred historically or that the practice began in Odisha at that time.

## A birth story is a different narrative layer

Mahanti recounts another story: Parvati gives life to a boy made from sandalwood paste and asks him to guard her bath. A confrontation with Shiva leads to his beheading; he is revived with an elephant’s head. This attributed birth story explains Ganesha’s form. It is kept separate from the booklet’s dialogue about performing the vow.

Sources: [Fading Festivals of Odisha: A peep into the religious practices in Odisha](https://www.esamskriti.com/essays/pdf/17%20FINAL%20Fading%20Festivals%20of%20Odisha%20%281%29.pdf). Printed80–81/PDF96–97; short synopsis of this author’s version.

## Twenty-one modaka and their distribution

The booklet instructs the observer to prepare 21 modaka: ten go to a Brahmin, ten are eaten by the observer and one is offered to the deity. The later Odia directions describe the sweets as cooked with ghee. Flowers and fruit also appear in groups of 21. This is a prescription in one printed witness; it does not establish a universal household practice.

Sources: [ବିନାୟକ ବ୍ରତକଥା · Binayaka Brata Katha](https://drive.google.com/uc?export=download&id=12JXOi753dG0dGBS9HU-R0oLX_dLhwqPs). PDF6/printed4 directions; PDF9–10/printed7–8 Odia explanation, continuing over the page.

## The recipe is still missing

The inspected directions name modaka and ghee but do not give a complete ingredient list, measurements, shape or cooking sequence. A familiar modern modak recipe cannot simply fill these gaps. Neither the booklet’s name nor its allocation of sweets proves that every Odisha household makes the same preparation.

## From a household vow to a shared school meal

Mahanti describes students decorating clay images, fasting before midday worship and breaking coconuts. Teachers and students later share an evening feast after arati. He also describes household pujas and women observing Vinayaka Brata. The feast’s dishes are unnamed; it cannot be treated as the booklet’s modaka distribution or a verified public meal today.

Sources: [Fading Festivals of Odisha: A peep into the religious practices in Odisha](https://www.esamskriti.com/essays/pdf/17%20FINAL%20Fading%20Festivals%20of%20Odisha%20%281%29.pdf). Printed79–80/PDF95–96; school and household settings.

## What a 1937 record tells us

A government catalogue published on 29 October 1937 lists Binayaka Brata Katha as an 8-page work published by Gobind Sahu of Ganjam in 1936. Entry 172 describes Sanskrit worship directions and an Odia vow story. It identifies Pharmacy Press, Berhampur, names Keshab Narayan Patnaik and records 1,000 copies. This documents a printed witness by 1936; it does not date the tradition’s beginning or measure readership.

The Gazette entry and the recovered Dharmagrantha Store booklet have not been matched as editions. ## Reading the significance through food

The division of sweets makes food both an offering and a relationship between deity, ritual specialist and observer. The school feast presents a different setting of shared eating. Together they suggest questions about how devotion moves between household and institution, without assuming that one setting grew historically from the other. This is an editorial reading of the evidence.
