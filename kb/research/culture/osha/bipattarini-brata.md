# Bipattarini Brata

Bipattarini Brata brings the hope of protection into a household’s ritual life. To understand its food culture in Odisha, the first task is to identify which story and practice a family follows. The name appears in an Odisha inventory; the fuller accounts recovered here come from Bengali settings and remain comparisons.

**Research note: Bengali narrative and temple food comparisons; Odisha story, calendar and menu remain unresolved.**

## What the Odisha record establishes

Dash’s2006 inventory names Bipattarini Brata but supplies no dedicated story, date or menu. Mahanti describes a Tarini Brata on Ashadha’s bright fourth, with a fast, red thread and the Ghatagaon Tarini shrine as context. Similar names and protective themes do not establish that these are one observance. Tarini’s calendar is therefore not assigned to Bipattarini.

## A riverbank story of protection: Bengali comparison

In Amrita Bhattacharyya’s2011 account of Bengali brata stories, Narada meets women worshipping Durga beside the Kartoya river. He recalls Shiva invoking Durga while drinking the poison from the churning of the ocean, and Yashoda tying a protective armlet on Krishna. Krishna also remembers Durga in danger. This is the article’s attributed sacred narrative, not verified events or an inspected Odia household text.

Sources: [River as a metaphor and actual presence in rites and rituals of Meyeder Bratakatha](https://indiantribalheritage.org/wp-content/uploads/Folklore-and-Folkloristics-Vol.-4-No.-1-2010-National-Folklore-Support-1046-4304-1-PB.pdf). PDF16–17, Bipattarini paragraph.

## Historical evidence and its limits

The2011 article places this Bengali observance in Ashar. Its bibliography cites Rama Devi’s Meyeder Bratakatha, edited by Gopalchandra Bhattacharya, without a known year; that edition was not recovered. These modern records establish published descriptions, not the vow’s founding date, earliest textual witness or route of transmission into Odisha.

## Fruit, sweets and the number thirteen: Rajpur comparison

Rajpur’s Bipattarini Chandibari in West Bengal describes vegetarian food on the preceding day, then fruit and sweets when breaking the fast. Thirteen kinds of fruit are offered: the temple explains twelve as the year’s months and one as the worshipper. It also describes a red thread with thirteen knots. No fruit varieties, sweet names, recipe or quantities are given. This is the temple’s own account, not an established Odisha menu.

Sources: [About Bipattarini Chandibari](https://www.bipadtarinichandibari.org/about-us.php). About section: previous-day, fast-breaking, thirteen-fruit and thread paragraphs; address footer.

## How food can carry meaning

The Rajpur account distinguishes food withheld, food offered and food eaten after a fast. That sequence offers a way to ask about household memory and care: who chooses the fruit, prepares food, tells the story and shares the meal? These are research questions for Odisha participants, rather than conclusions that the same rules apply everywhere. The number thirteen in this account does not establish a historical link with the saying Bara Masa, Tera Parba.
