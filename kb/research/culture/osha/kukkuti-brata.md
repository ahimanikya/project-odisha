# Kukkuti Brata

Kukkuti Brata opens a path from Odisha’s household calendar to a wider textual tradition. The evidence is uneven: its name and dedication are locally documented, while the recovered story and food directions come from a Sanskrit comparator.

**Research note: Odisha identity and a Sanskrit comparison; local narrative, food preparation and origin remain unresolved.**

## Its place in Odisha’s calendar

Pratap Kumar Dash lists Kukkuti in Bhadrab, dedicated to Shiva and Parvati. Mahanti places it on the second day of Somnath Brata, whose start he gives as Bhadrava shukla sasthi. This suggests a connection with the next lunar day, but the relative wording alone should not be converted into a verified annual date. Neither passage supplies a separate Kukkuti menu.

Dash printed15 / PDF1; Mahanti printed48–49 / PDF64–65.

## Lalita Saptami: a textual comparison

The Shabdakalpadruma entry for Lalitasaptami names Kukkuti and specifies Bhadra shukla saptami. Its directions include Shiva–Parvati worship, an eight-knotted thread wetted with milk, and eight fruits. This is a Sanskrit textual prescription, not verification of Odisha household practice or a universal alias in every locality.

Sources: [Shabdakalpadruma: Lalitasaptami entry](https://sa.wikisource.org/wiki/शब्दकल्पद्रुमः/ल). Vol.4, displayed p.209, opening and directions before the narrative.

## Friendship, forgetting and renewal

In that entry’s story, Queen Chandramukhi and her friend Mala learn a vow from women by the Sarayu. Neglect is followed by rebirth as a monkey and a hen, then as Ishvari and Bhushana. Bereavement turns Ishvari’s friendship into jealousy; Bhushana offers to share the vow’s merit and explains its practice. The text frames this as consolation to Devaki. These are devotional narrative events, not a verified Odisha history or an explanation of real bereavement.

Sources: [Shabdakalpadruma: Lalitasaptami entry](https://sa.wikisource.org/wiki/शब्दकल्पद्रुमः/ल). Vol.4 pp.209–210, narrative attributed there to Bhavishya Purana.

## Food terms without an invented recipe

The closing directions name payasa and pishtaka and place feeding kin before one’s own meal. They do not identify ingredients, cooking techniques or an Odisha dish. Milk’s use on the thread is distinct from a drink or meal. Somnath’s ten-pitha account cannot fill the missing local evidence.

Sources: [Shabdakalpadruma: Lalitasaptami entry](https://sa.wikisource.org/wiki/शब्दकल्पद्रुमः/ल), [Fading Festivals of Odisha: A peep into the religious practices in Odisha](https://www.esamskriti.com/essays/pdf/17%20FINAL%20Fading%20Festivals%20of%20Odisha%20%281%29.pdf). Shabdakalpadruma p.209, closing food lines before p.210; Mahanti p.49.

## A local manuscript lead to investigate

A mirrored catalogue of the Orissa State Museum’s Smrti collection lists Kukkuti among four subjects in item148, a twelve-folio palm-leaf Vratakatha described as written in Oriya script. It supplies no copying date, findspot or colophon. The accession suffix is unclear in OCR and the page image could not be inspected. Twelve folia describe the combined record, not Kukkuti alone; Oriya script does not establish that the text is in the Odia language.

Sources: [Descriptive Catalogue of Palmleaf and Paper Manuscripts: Smrti collection](https://jainqq.org/booktext/Descriptive_Catalogue_of_Palmleaf_and_Paper_Manuscripts/020182). Printed85 / viewer139, item148; preface viewer9 identifies museum collection.

## Comparative witnesses outside Odisha

IGNCA’s catalogue provides four selected Kukkuti records from Saraswati Bhawan Library, Varanasi, in Sanskrit and Bangla script. One is described as incomplete and three as complete. Those labels describe catalogue assessments; the manuscripts themselves remain unread. Their titles establish research leads, not four independent versions, an Odisha origin or a proven route of transmission.

Sources: [Vedanga (Kalpa–Grihyaprayoga), Volume 2, Part VI](https://ignca.gov.in/PDF_data/Vedanga-Kalpa%28Grihyaprayoga%29-Vol-II-Part-VI.pdf). Printed108–109 / PDF126–127, records6281,6286–6288.

## What remains to understand

The story invites an editorial inquiry into friendship, grief and the transmission of ritual knowledge among women. A local study could ask how the thread is made, who tells the story, which food terms people use and how preparation is learned. The text’s supernatural rewards and punishments are not evidence about health, fertility or parental responsibility. A dated original, an identified Odia telling and locality-specific accounts are still needed.
