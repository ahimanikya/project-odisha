# Pusa Rabibara Brata

A wedding procession is still on the road and daylight is slipping away. In the Pusha Rabibara story, a bride asks the sun to wait. The request begins a tale that brings a royal household to the tulsi courtyard, where flowers, food and a Sunday vow become part of making amends.

**Narrative checkpoint: one Odia text inspected; historical dating and local food preparation remain open.**

## A seasonal Sunday vow

Dash lists Pusa Rabibara Brata in Pausa as worship of the sun. The Odia text’s directions specify Sundays in Pusha. Dash separately lists Rabinarayan in Margasira; a shared solar dedication does not make these entries interchangeable.

## The prince and the merchant’s daughter

The scanned katha opens with a king, queen and their son, who wants to marry after his companions have done so. A Brahmin intermediary finds a merchant’s daughter in Karnataka, and a wedding is arranged. These places and characters belong to the story; the text supplies no verified historical itinerary.

## The sun is asked to pause

On the return journey, the bride fears darkness will fall before the gifts and procession reach home. She asks Aditya to pause until they enter the palace. He grants the request. His mother, waiting for him, angrily curses whoever delayed his journey. When she learns why he is late, she tells him that the bride can seek relief through the Pusha Sunday vow.

Sources: [ପୁଷ ରବିବାର ବ୍ରତ କଥା · Pusha Rabibara Brata Katha](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Pusha_Rabibara_Brata_Katha.pdf). PDF5–7, request to Aditya, delayed return and the mother’s response.

## The story reaches the courtyard

The bride becomes afflicted; the royal household seeks guidance from its religious advisers. She undertakes the vow and, in the story’s ending, recovers and prospers. The narrative presents devotion as the remedy. It is a sacred healing story, not medical evidence or advice to replace treatment.

Sources: [ପୁଷ ରବିବାର ବ୍ରତ କଥା · Pusha Rabibara Brata Katha](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Pusha_Rabibara_Brata_Katha.pdf). PDF7–8, affliction, advice and concluding observance.

## Preparing a place before sunrise

The directions call for rising before sunrise on Pusha Sundays, bathing, preparing the area at the tulsi plant with cow dung, establishing a sun mandala, decorating with flowers and inviting a Brahmin for worship. These are directions in this textual witness; present-day household adaptations have not been documented here.

Sources: [ପୁଷ ରବିବାର ବ୍ରତ କଥା · Pusha Rabibara Brata Katha](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Pusha_Rabibara_Brata_Katha.pdf). PDF7, ritual directions.

## A food name carried into the market

In her essay Muan, Dr Jayashree Nanda reports that seasonal sweets are sold under names including Pusha Rabibara muan, Dhanu muan and Makara muan. This is an attributed cultural account. The shared naming context does not prove identical ingredients, and the essay does not identify a current seller for this observance.

## A wider Sunday practice, kept separate

Mahanti’s weekly Rabibara entry describes sun worship, fasting with one meal towards evening and almsgiving. It does not explicitly identify those rules as Pusa Rabibara Brata. The Pausa profile therefore leaves the actual fast-breaking time and meal open.

## History: a recovered text, an unresolved date

Project Pothi’s scan gives us a readable textual witness. The Commons page credits Prateek Pattanaik with providing and scanning it, calls the author unknown and labels it part of Bratabali. Its eighteenth-century description and 8 February 1917 publication field have not been confirmed from an original imprint. They cannot establish when this vow began in Odisha.

## How to read its significance

The story places a bride’s journey within the sun’s daily movement, then brings the consequences into household worship. Food is part of the act of devotion, alongside a prepared courtyard, flowers and spoken guidance. The seasonal market name adds a different kind of memory: how an observance may help people name a sweet. This is an editorial interpretation, not a universal account of belief.
