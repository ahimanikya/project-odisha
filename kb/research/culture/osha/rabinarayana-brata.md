# Rabinarayana Brata

Rabinarayana Brata brings the sun, the lunar calendar and everyday materials into one act of devotion. Its offerings show why food culture includes more than a list of dishes: fruit, grain and prepared sweets can have different ritual roles.

**Research note: calendar descriptions, food roles and manuscript leads; full story and local history remain unresolved.**

## A weekday meets a lunar day

Mahanti describes the observance when Ekadashi falls on Sunday and identifies the deity as Surya. His passage does not specify the fortnight or restrict the month. Dash instead lists Rabinarayan in Margasira and separately lists Pusa Rabibara in Pausa. Those entries should not be merged into one Sunday festival. The calendar descriptions remain unresolved; we have not assigned a Gregorian date.

## Materials counted in sevens

Mahanti describes women wearing a ritual thread containing seven blades of duba grass, seven barakoli leaves and seven raw rice grains, tied with seven folds of thread. After bathing they offer water to Surya and worship with seven kinds of flowers and seven kinds of fruit. The rice here is part of the thread assembly, not a documented cooked offering or meal. The fruit species, sharing practice and food eaten afterwards are unspecified.

Sources: [Fading Festivals of Odisha: A peep into the religious practices in Odisha](https://www.esamskriti.com/essays/pdf/17%20FINAL%20Fading%20Festivals%20of%20Odisha%20%281%29.pdf). Printed 45 / PDF 61, Rabinarayana entry.

## Mua and manda in an Odia account

Bishnumohan Adhikari’s 2020 account on Antaranga Kalinga specifies bright-fortnight Ekadashi on Sunday. It names seven mua and seven manda among the offerings, alongside flowers and durba, and describes a seven-knotted thread and water offering. This is an attributed cultural description; it gives no recipe, filling, grain variety or preparation method. We therefore keep its prepared-food names beside Mahanti’s fruit account without combining them into a mandatory menu.

Sources: [Rabinarayana Ekadashi — ରବିନାରାୟଣ ଏକାଦଶୀ](https://antarangakalinga.org/ରବିନାରାୟଣ-ଏକାଦଶୀ/). Calendar paragraph and final offering paragraph.

## The story has identifiable textual leads

Mahanti says the Rabinarayana brata katha is read but does not recount it. The National Mission for Manuscripts’ Odisha State Museum catalogue lists Rabinarayana Brata under ORPR-218 and ORPR-236, and a combined Rabi Narayana Brata Katha, Kanchi Kaberi record under CY-1847,1848. These are three catalogue records, not three established versions of the story. The listed extents cannot supply a date of composition, author or plot, and the combined record’s extent is not the length of the brata alone.

Sources: [Fading Festivals of Odisha: A peep into the religious practices in Odisha](https://www.esamskriti.com/essays/pdf/17%20FINAL%20Fading%20Festivals%20of%20Odisha%20%281%29.pdf), [Orissa State Museum manuscript catalogue](https://www.namami.gov.in/sites/default/files/Orissa_State_Museum_Bhubaneswar_Odisha.pdf). ## Hope, memory and the work of observance

Mahanti associates the vow with women’s hopes for children. That describes a devotional intention, not evidence of fertility treatment. As an editorial reading, repeated counting and assembling plant materials may offer a useful way to explore how practice is remembered and taught. The calendar rule also invites research into who consults an almanac and how dates are settled locally. Neither detail establishes a particular astronomical calculation, an ancient scientific theory or an explanation for the number seven.

## Keeping related solar observances distinct

The separate Rabibara paragraph in Mahanti describes Sunday fasting, an evening meal and almsgiving. Those details are not explicitly attached to Rabinarayana in its own entry. Likewise, Samba Dashami’s story and named dishes belong to that observance’s sources. Sharing a solar dedication does not establish an identical narrative, date or menu.
