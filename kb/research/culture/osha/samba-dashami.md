# Samba Dashami

An offering made in a child’s name makes a family’s hopes visible through food.

**Narrative research checkpoint**. Traditional month: Pausa. No annual visit date is confirmed.

## The legend and the observance

Dina Krishna Joshi’s 2013 essay recounts the belief that Samba, Krishna’s son, recovered from illness through devotion to Surya. It places the observance on Pausa’s bright tenth lunar day. The healing belongs to sacred narrative; it is neither clinical evidence nor a dated account of the festival’s beginnings.

Sources: [Samba Dasami in Odishan Culture](https://magazines.odisha.gov.in/Orissareview/2013/Jan/engpdf/january-2013.pdf). Printed p.55.

## A family written in food

Joshi describes offerings associated with individual family members and foods including khichdi, ghadghadia tarkari, pitha and khiri. His account moves through morning, midday and evening acts of worship and subsequent sharing. This gives us a sequence to investigate, not a universal menu or timetable.

Sources: [Samba Dasami in Odishan Culture](https://magazines.odisha.gov.in/Orissareview/2013/Jan/engpdf/january-2013.pdf). Printed p.55, Tradition.

## What gives the food its meaning

Our interpretive question is how an offering in someone’s name becomes a household memory. Which item belongs to which child? Does it remain the same after that person leaves home? Those questions could connect the historical reading with living family accounts, without claiming interviews have already taken place.


## Sunrise, midday and the later offering

Joshi places khichdi and ghadghadia tarkari at sunrise, with an individual offering in each family member’s name. His midday account brings prepared foods to the tulasi platform and includes a Samba Dashami Brata Katha reading. A later Mahakala Puja has Budha Chakuli offered to Yama. Keeping these acts separate prevents a Surya offering list from becoming a single menu for every part of the day.

Source: [Samba Dasami in Odishan Culture](https://magazines.odisha.gov.in/Orissareview/2013/Jan/engpdf/january-2013.pdf). Printed55 / PDF68, Tradition; both columns visually inspected.


## Named foods and the sharing that follows

The essay names manda, kakara and arisha pithas, Makar Chaula, Chhenaguda, khiri, rasagola, chhenagaja and sweet curd among foods prepared through the day. It describes dishes first offered to Surya and later shared with children. This is a source-specific food list, not a required household menu, tested recipe or current visitor meal. The companion preparation pages describe food forms; they do not verify this ritual’s local ingredients or quantities.

## A printed ambiguity to preserve

The midday passage prints “beetle” in its description of the turmeric-water bowl. The page image confirms that spelling. Its intended object remains unresolved: we do not silently substitute betel, a leaf or another item. An Odia katha, another edition or a situated household account is needed before using that detail as a ritual instruction.
