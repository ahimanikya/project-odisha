# Sankata Chaturthi

A rice cake shaped in the hand offers one way into Sankata Chaturthi’s food culture. The written accounts preserve both kitchen detail and uncertainty: the cake’s name and preparation vary, and descriptions of the observance do not all name the same deity or calendar rule.

**Research note: food descriptions recovered; local identity, history and complete preparation remain unresolved.**

## An Odisha account in Magha

Pratap Kumar Dash’s April 2006 survey places Sankata Chaturthi in Magha and names Shiva and Parvati as the deities worshipped. This is a published description without a named household or locality. It establishes an account to investigate, rather than a uniform rule for Odisha.

Sources: [The Perishing Tradition of Osha, Brata and Mela in Orissa: An Overview](https://magazines.odisha.gov.in/Orissareview/April2006/engpdf/The_perishing_tradition_of_osha.pdf). Printed16 / PDF2.

## Bundi in the paragraph, Bindi in the note

The April article’s food paragraph prints Bundi; its note 17 prints Bindi. The note describes a handful-sized cake of powdered fried unboiled rice, molasses and coconut scraps. The spelling difference remains visible. This rice preparation cannot be silently replaced by a familiar sweet sold under a similar name, nor transferred to the separately listed Nagala Chaturthi.

Sources: [The Perishing Tradition of Osha, Brata and Mela in Orissa: An Overview](https://magazines.odisha.gov.in/Orissareview/April2006/engpdf/The_perishing_tradition_of_osha.pdf). Printed17 / PDF3 food paragraph and printed18 / PDF4 note17; editorial identity boundary appended.

## A second kitchen description from the same author

In December 2006, Dash calls the food Bindi, also known as hundi. He describes khali, a boiled dough of sundried rice powder, with molasses, coconut, black pepper, ajowan and cardamom; ghee helps form handful-sized cakes. This account associates the observance with Shiva. It gives no quantities or complete cooking sequence, and differs from April’s fried-rice wording. The two articles share an author and are not independent corroboration.

Sources: [Uniqueness of Odiya Festivals](https://magazines.odisha.gov.in/orissareview/dec-2006/engpdf/dec.pdf). Printed48 / PDF48 byline; printed49 / PDF49 Sankata paragraph. Web-extracted text; page images unavailable..

## A recurring Ganesh vow in another account

Mahanti describes Sankati Chaturthi and Binayak Chaturthi as recurring vows on the fourth lunar day of the dark and bright fortnights respectively. Women and men fast and worship Ganesh; the Sankati account waits for the Moon in the evening. A Tuesday occurrence is called Angarki, associated devotionally with relief from danger. The inspected passage names no food. Its relation to Dash’s Magha Shiva–Parvati account remains unresolved.

Sources: [Fading Festivals of Odisha: A peep into the religious practices in Odisha](https://www.esamskriti.com/essays/pdf/17%20FINAL%20Fading%20Festivals%20of%20Odisha%20%281%29.pdf). Printed43–44 / PDF59–60, Sankati and Binayak heading.

## What the food can help us ask

The handful-sized cake brings attention to preparation as an act of care: whose hands shape it, who teaches the method, and how is it shared? Those are questions for local accounts. The sources inspected here do not establish whether this particular cake is first offered, eaten to end a fast, or served as a household meal. Those roles should be recorded separately.
