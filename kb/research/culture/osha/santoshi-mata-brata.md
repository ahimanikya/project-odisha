# Santoshi Mata Brata

Santoshi Mata Brata gives the food calendar a weekly rhythm. Its small offering, fasting discipline and concluding meal have different roles; understanding those roles matters more than assigning it an invented annual season.

**Research note: published Odisha practice and comparative narrative; local textual history and meal variations remain open.**

## A Friday vow in Odisha

Mahanti describes a comparatively recent practice in Odisha, undertaken by men and women. It may begin at any time of year and continue for sixteen Fridays or until the vow is fulfilled; he also calls it Shola Shukrabara brata. His description supplies no arrival date. Dash’s 2006 inventory likewise identifies Friday fasting and avoidance of pickle. These are published descriptions, not a survey of current households.

## History: devotion before the film

Philip Lutgendorf’s study says the Friday vow had spread in northern India for more than a decade before the 1975 film Jai Santoshi Maa. Cinema amplified an existing devotion. That history does not date its arrival in Odisha.

Sources: [A Made to Satisfaction Goddess: Jai Santoshi Maa Revisited, Part Two](https://manushi.in/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/pdfs_issues/PDF%20ISSUE%20131%283.4%29/5.%20A%20Made%20to%20Satisfaction%20Goddess.pdf). Printed24 / PDF1; film citation printed37 / PDF14; issue period July–August2002.

## A story of separation and return

In the pamphlet story summarised by Lutgendorf, a mistreated wife learns the vow from other women. Her absent husband returns, but relatives introduce sour food into the concluding feast. A repeated observance restores the household; the wife later recognises the goddess in frightening form. This is devotional narrative, not historical or causal evidence.

## Offering, abstention and completion

Mahanti identifies gur-chana as raw sugar candy with roasted chickpea. He describes fasting, avoidance of eating or touching sour or bitter things, and an udyapana in which eight children are fed and given clothes and money. He does not give that meal’s dishes. The offering is therefore not a complete menu. His sugar terminology is retained without silently replacing it with a particular modern product.

Sources: [Fading Festivals of Odisha: A peep into the religious practices in Odisha](https://www.esamskriti.com/essays/pdf/17%20FINAL%20Fading%20Festivals%20of%20Odisha%20%281%29.pdf). Printed109–110 / PDF125–126.

## A comparison that preserves differences

Lutgendorf describes one meal during the fast and an ending feast for eight boys. Mahanti says eight children. Those formulations should remain attributed until local accounts clarify practice. The comparative source does not establish an Odisha completion-meal recipe.

## What food can help us understand

An editorial reading can follow how a modest offering becomes a shared act of devotion, and how a meal marks completion of a promise. Future research should ask who obtains ingredients, prepares the meal, tells the story and teaches the rules. Roasting is a named preparation, but temperature, equipment and local technique remain undocumented here. Religious abstention does not by itself establish a nutritional or medical benefit.
