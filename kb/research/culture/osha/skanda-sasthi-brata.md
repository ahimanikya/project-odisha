# Skanda Sasthi Brata

A festival name can survive in a calendar even when its place in everyday life is uncertain. Skanda Sasthi appears in an Odisha inventory, yet another writer says it is listed in almanacs without being observed in the state. Following that discrepancy reveals why the history of a deity, a vow and a seasonal meal must each be investigated.

**Research note: Odisha practice and food unresolved; a dated South Indian comparison is kept within its own scope.**

## A name in the inventory, a question about practice

Pratap Kumar Dash lists Skanda Sasthi Brata in Chaitra. J. C. Mahanti gives Chaitra’s dark-fortnight sixth, associates it with Kartikeya and says many panjis mention it, but that it is not observed in Odisha. His broad statement is an author’s account, not a present-day statewide survey. The two sources leave actual local practice unresolved.

## The deity has an Odisha history; the vow still needs one

Kalyan Kumar Panda discusses a two-armed Karttikeya Mahasena image at the Kosalesvara temple, Baidyanath, riding a peacock and holding a vel. Panda favours a sixth-century date, while explicitly noting Vidya Dehejia’s disagreement with Charles Fabri’s dating. This is attributed art-historical context with a dating dispute. It cannot establish when Skanda Sasthi Brata began in Odisha.

## A 1921 comparison from South India

P. V. Jagadisa Ayyar’s South Indian Festivities was published in Madras in 1921. Its Skanda Sashti chapter places the festival on the bright-fortnight sixth in Arpisi, approximately October–November, and relates it to Subrahmanyam’s victory over Surapadma. That seasonal setting differs from the Chaitra entry in the Odisha accounts. It is retained as regional comparison, not used to replace their calendar.

Sources: [South Indian Festivities [Illustrated]: Skanda Sashti](https://rarebooksocietyofindia.org/book_archive/196174216674_10156536880671675.pdf). Title page PDF6; chapter XXIII printed146/PDF151.

## Six sparks and a divine child: Ayyar’s version

In Ayyar’s telling, the gods ask Shiva for relief from Surapadma. Shiva emits six sparks; Agni and Vayu temper their intensity and carry them towards the Ganges. The sparks gather among reeds and become a radiant child with six faces and twelve hands. The Krithika nurses are then linked to the name Kartikeya. This is a synopsis of a sacred narrative in a South Indian book, not a recovered Odia brata-katha or historical event.

Sources: [South Indian Festivities [Illustrated]: Skanda Sashti](https://rarebooksocietyofindia.org/book_archive/196174216674_10156536880671675.pdf). Printed147–148/PDF152–153; selected birth narrative within the chapter.

## What the comparison says about offerings

A footnote explains kavadi as a shoulder-borne pole whose ends carry pots containing milk or sugar according to the vow. The surrounding passage concerns devotion to Subrahmanyam and the Idumban story. It is not an Odisha food description, a complete Skanda Sasthi menu or a recipe. These items are therefore excluded from the Odisha seasonal-food association.

## Do not merge Skanda Sasthi with Sathi Osha

The existing Sathi Osha record concerns goddess Shashthi and Bhadraba’s bright sixth, with a separately sourced household food account. The shared sixth-day language is a reason to compare identities carefully, not evidence that the two observances share their deity, season, story or menu.

## Why this uncertainty belongs in the cultural story

This entry offers a way to ask how printed calendars, deity worship and household observance relate. It would be premature to call Skanda Sasthi either a widespread living Odisha tradition or an extinct one. The next useful evidence is a dated local panji entry paired with a willing practitioner’s account, followed by inspection of any text they identify.
