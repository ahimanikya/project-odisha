# Somnath Brata

Somnath Brata brings together a sacred thread, pitha offerings and the telling of a story. Its surviving accounts offer a way to explore how food preparation and listening carry a vow through household life.

**Narrative checkpoint: one undated Odia copy inspected; origin history, textual dating and local preparation remain open.**

## A vow across two months

Mahanti describes a period from Bhadrava shukla sasthi to Aswina shukla dashami, identified as Vijaya Dashami. Shiva and Parbati are worshipped, mainly by women seeking family well-being. Dash’s shorter inventory places Somanath in Bhadrab and lists Kukkuti separately. These are descriptions of customary timing; no annual visitor date has been verified.

## The king, the queen and the neglected vow

In the recovered Odia copy, Shiva tells Parvati about King Birabikrama and Somnath’s visiting ascetics. The king welcomes and feeds them but rejects their invitation to observe the vow, fearing ridicule. A curse brings illness and loss. His queen, later displaced from the palace, learns the vow through women in a Brahmin settlement and obtains the ritual thread with a maid’s help. Her observance precedes his recovery and their reunion. This is the story’s devotional explanation, not historical biography or evidence that observance cures illness.

## What the offerings actually say

Mahanti specifies ten types of pitha without naming them. The Odia copy instead calls for ten pithas with ten kinds of filling; ingredients and cooking method are unspecified. Its palace hospitality scene is a narrative meal, not a prescription for the ritual offering. The copy names Aswina shukla dashami for the vow. These details do not establish a standard manda recipe.

Sources: [Fading Festivals of Odisha: A peep into the religious practices in Odisha](https://www.esamskriti.com/essays/pdf/17%20FINAL%20Fading%20Festivals%20of%20Odisha%20%281%29.pdf), [Somanatha Brata Katha — ସୋମନାଥ ବ୍ରତ କଥା](https://instapdfhome.files.wordpress.com/2021/04/somanath-vrat-katha-oriya.pdf). ## Thread, gathering and sharing

Mahanti describes ten strands and ten knots, names of Shiva recited while knotting, a midday gathering around a clay linga, an elderly woman reading the katha, and participants wearing the thread and sharing bhoga. He places Kukkuti on Somnath’s second day. Its separate identity must be researched before transferring this food account to it.

Sources: [Fading Festivals of Odisha: A peep into the religious practices in Odisha](https://www.esamskriti.com/essays/pdf/17%20FINAL%20Fading%20Festivals%20of%20Odisha%20%281%29.pdf). Printed 48–49 / PDF 64–65.

## A literary history with a date still to test

Subrat Kumar Prusty’s March 2014 Odisha Review article places Somanatha Brata Katha among early Odia prose texts and assigns it to the thirteenth century. His excerpt has Shiva addressing Parvati and introduces Malaba, Patala and King Birabikrama. It cites Kunja Bihari Tripathy’s Prachina Odia Gadya Sankalan (Odisha Sahitya Akademy, 1982), p.92. This is an attributed literary dating. The cited edition and its dating argument remain unread; neither the modern copy nor this excerpt establishes when the observance began. Similar opening characters do not establish textual identity.

Sources: [Classical Language : Odia](https://magazines.odisha.gov.in/Orissareview/2014/mar/engpdf/5-14.pdf). Printed 10 / PDF 7; cited original not inspected.

## Eight catalogue leads, not eight proven versions

The National Mission for Manuscripts’ museum catalogue supplies eight selected Somanatha title records. One combines several works, and its manuscript identifier is reused for a different title in the next row. Accession, title and page location therefore remain essential. The records have not been collated or read as manuscripts; their extents, dates, authors and relationship to the recovered typed copy must not be inferred.

## Significance and practical knowledge

An editorial reading can follow the contrast between royal pride and the queen’s learning through other women. The preparations also raise questions about how people remember counts, teach knotting, vary fillings and coordinate a gathering. These are promising subjects for household research, not evidence of a particular ancient scientific theory. The numerical pattern alone does not explain why ten was chosen.
