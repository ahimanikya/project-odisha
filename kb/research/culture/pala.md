# Pala: poetry with people around it

A Pala singer brings a verse to a gathering. The story grows through explanation, music, gesture and the voice that answers back.

In the Nalconagar photograph, people sit on mats around a standing performer. His raised hand and the drum beside the listeners draw attention to a small performance space beneath a bright canopy. Poetry here has people around it: a voice to carry the story, other performers to answer, and listeners to follow its turns.

Pala brings literary and devotional storytelling into performance. To follow it, listen beyond the main narrative. A comparison can explain a difficult verse; a gesture can change the meaning of an object; an answering voice can turn an elevated thought into a sharp joke.[^pala-patra2017][^pala-mahapatra1982]

## The reader behind the voice

Jagannath Behera’s 2009 Sangeet Natak Akademi citation describes a performer who drew on literary classics in Odia, Bengali, Hindi, Sanskrit and English. A quotation in his performance could carry another writer’s words into the story being told.

The citation names Harekrushna Nath, Bhagaban Singh and Harekrushna Swain as his teachers. It offers a glimpse of an artist’s education through books and through other performers. Behera’s multilingual practice is his own recorded achievement, rather than a requirement for every Pala singer.[^pala-sna-behera2009]

There are two things to notice when a performer introduces a quotation: where it comes from, and why it belongs at that moment. The pleasure may lie in recognising the work. For an unfamiliar listener, the explanation can become the way into it. A title remembered after a performance is a reason to open a book.

## Odisha’s literary culture, heard aloud

Pala connects Odisha’s literary culture with the work of listening together. A poem becomes a voice, a musical phrase, an explanation and an answer. The link is especially clear in a 2021 profile of Gangadhar Puhan: it describes his chhanda, champu, chaupadi and Odissi singing, and his interest in Bhanja literature and Udayanath Rathsharma’s poems and plays.[^pala-os-gangadhar]

These are choices made by a named artist, not a single syllabus for the whole tradition. They show how older literary forms can enter a present gathering through a performer’s knowledge. A text need not remain on the page to have a literary life; equally, the performance can send a listener back to the book.

Read this beside Pala’s devotional histories and the Gopabandhu parody below. They reveal different cultural connections: worship and shared religious vocabulary, the study of poetry, and humour directed at public power. No single origin story can explain all those uses.

## The voice, the drum and the answer

Kalahandi district’s description of Palla names the Gayak, Bayak and Palias, alongside Mrudanga, Gini and Chamar. It places the form around Bhawanipatna, Dharamgarh and M. Rampur and describes lyrical narration of Puranic and historical events. This is a local account of an ensemble, not a fixed cast list for all Odisha.[^daskathia-kalahandi]

The lead singer does not carry every meaning alone. In Sitakant Mahapatra’s account, the Palia can answer an explanation with a pun or a contrary interpretation. The response may help an audience follow the story, but it can also interrupt its dignity. A serious claim has to survive the question or joke that follows it.[^pala-mahapatra1982]

Umesh Patra’s 2017 study distinguishes seated Baithaki Pala, standing Thia Pala and competitive Badi Pala. Badi brings two teams into a contest of wit. Knowing the form changes how one listens: a household recitation and an exchange between competing singers have different expectations.[^pala-patra2017]

## How a story acquires a body

Patra describes an invocation before the main narrative, followed by verse explanation, song, movement and changes of role. A performer can tell an episode and then give it a physical shape, drawing attention to a character’s decision or predicament.

His Harischandra example makes this visible. A chamara, or fly-whisk, stands for the king’s surrendered crown; in other passages the same object can become a bow or trident. Its material has not changed. The performer’s action asks the audience to understand it differently.[^pala-patra2017]

Watch for that change rather than looking only for elaborate scenery. What is the object at this moment? Which gesture made its meaning clear? Such questions help an unfamiliar listener follow the drama even before every literary reference is understood.

## When a familiar poem turns against power

Sitakant Mahapatra’s essay, published in 1982, describes a pointed use of Gopabandhu Das’s poetry. The original thought imagines the speaker sacrificing his own body for the country. In the Palia’s parody, a selfish leader expects the people to sacrifice themselves for him.

The reversal is small enough to recognise and large enough to change the argument. The earlier ideal remains in the listener’s mind, making the leader’s demand look worse. Mahapatra’s example shows how literary knowledge can become public criticism through an answering voice.[^pala-mahapatra1982]

This is a prose account of the example, not a transcript of a performance. When listening, notice what a joke changes: a word, the person speaking, or the person expected to bear the cost. Humour can be part of the explanation.

## Devotional history has more than one thread

Pala’s devotional history has several accounts. In his 2021 chapter, Patra examines the Satyapir narratives associated with Kabi Karna’s Shola Pala and the relationship between Satyanarayana and the Muslim pir. He reads the chapter’s cited references to Ram and Rahim, the Quran and Purana as a shared devotional language.

That is a literary interpretation of religious connection. It does not establish a single founding year or show that every community followed the same practice. Mahapatra, too, distinguishes household ritual recitation from performances that develop a story through literary comparisons.

The distinction matters when encountering Pala in different settings. Devotion, narrative and literary display can meet in a performance without being identical. Accounts of worship, the transmission of a text and the development of stage practice each leave a different historical question to answer.[^pala-patra2021][^pala-mahapatra1982]

## Named evenings, named choices

The report of the 19–21 July 2012 Pala and Daskathia Utsav at Rabindra Mandap, Bhubaneswar, gives the art particular names and stories. Santilata Sahu presented an episode about Hanuman’s meditation in Dwapara Yuga. Manorama Panda presented Naga Jyoti, and Kumudini Patel presented Sasirekha Parinaya. These women are credited as presenters with their own repertoire.

The report also connects Laxman Charan Rout’s Mahadani Ajigarta with Pandit Bhimsen Vedapathi’s Ajigarttopakhyana. A performance title can therefore lead in two directions: towards the artist’s choice of episode and back to a named literary work.

These are dated festival credits, not present-day bookings. The programme included both Pala and Daskathia; appearing at the same festival does not make them the same form. A programme tells us what was presented, while the details of a particular telling belong to the performance itself.[^pala-narthaki-utsav2012]

## What a teacher passes on

Laxmidhar Rout’s 2016 Sangeet Natak Akademi citation records training under Damodar Sarangi and Baishnaba Charan Pradhan. It credits him with teaching younger students and work through Pala Pratishthan, Shree Jagannath Pala Research Foundation and a Nilachala Pala parishad in Kendrapara. It also names his book Udaharana Sataka. Here preservation has identifiable work behind it: teaching, performance and writing.[^pala-sna-rout2016]

Laxmidhar Rout is distinct from Laxman Charan Rout, the Bhadrak performer credited in the 2012 festival report. That government programme also names Santilata Sahu, Manorama Panda and Kumudini Patel as Pala performers. Remembering the art means keeping individual credits, women’s repertoire and the work of accompanying performers in view.[^pala-culture-report2012]

A recording creates another route into this world. UKS TV’s catalogue credits Sachidananda Dash and team with Guru Dakhina, and Nrusingha Hota and team with Rochak Ramayan. Such recordings can introduce a visitor to the pace of a performance. A publisher’s upload does not make its footage free to reuse, and a recording cannot replace an apprenticeship.[^pala-sachidash-uks20261019]

## What changes when time is shortened

Patra records Prasant Nath Sharma’s concern, in an interview dated 14 June 2014, that shorter programmes were squeezing out music, comedy and other material. This is a performer’s dated account, not a measured trend across Odisha.[^pala-patra2017]

It points to something a listener can ask about before attending: how much time has been allowed? Following a long comparison or a comic exchange requires a different pace from hearing a short extract. The duration helps explain what kind of encounter the programme can offer.

## Literature in public life

The Chief Minister’s office report of 28 December 2025 credits singers and Bayaks with bringing Odia poetry to the public at the 48th state-level Pala Sammilan in Kendujhar. The gathering is a dated example of Pala’s place in public cultural life; its report does not supply artist earnings or a current programme.[^pala-cm-sammilan2025]

An award citation, a festival repertoire and an account of a performer’s training reveal different parts of the work. Together they give a listener more to notice: the text selected, the teacher remembered, the accompanying artist and the way an explanation is made clear.

## Why Pala deserves a future

The case for preserving Pala begins with the skills it joins together. A book can preserve the words of a poem; a recording can preserve one telling. Neither, by itself, keeps the ability to explain the poem, shape its music, answer a fellow performer and hold a gathering’s attention. Those abilities need people learning from one another, with time and opportunities to perform.

Losing that practice would narrow the ways Odisha can encounter its own literature. It would also weaken a form in which devotion can share space with wit, a listener can hear an unfamiliar text explained, and a Palia can question the dignity of the main speaker. The argument is for keeping those possibilities available to future performers and audiences.

There are reasons to take the conditions of the work seriously. Sharma’s 2014 concern about compressed programmes describes pressure on performance time. A 2021 report records artists seeking help during pandemic disruption. Neither source measures today’s statewide condition, but both direct attention towards rehearsal, paid work and the time an ensemble needs—not only towards awards and publicity.[^pala-patra2017][^pala-nie-pandemic2021]

Preservation should leave room for artists to change their practice. UNESCO’s 2003 safeguarding framework treats transmission, community participation and continuing recreation as central. Applied here as a principle, that means supporting teachers and ensembles, documenting work with their agreement, and letting practitioners shape what is shared. It is not a claim that Pala has a UNESCO listing.[^pala-unesco-safeguarding2003]

## Finding a performance during your visit

Start with a dated programme that explicitly names Pala or Palla. The 2012 Rabindra Mandap festival in Bhubaneswar and the 2025 Kendujhar Sammilan are historical examples of public presentation. They do not establish the next edition, a weekly show or admission on your travel dates. No forthcoming public performance is confirmed in the sources checked for this guide on 19 October 2026.[^pala-culture-report2012][^pala-cm-sammilan2025]

For enquiries, the Odisha Culture Department’s CTMS register offers district and art-category filters. Its Music/Pala entries include troupes in Cuttack, Bhadrak and Kendujhar. Treat a listing as an introduction to ask about an actual programme, not as a recommendation, an open rehearsal or a confirmed booking. Check the category: a Pala-sounding troupe name alone is insufficient.[^pala-ctms20261019]

If staying in Bhubaneswar or Cuttack, ask your host or guide to find an organiser’s current notice and confirm it directly before you travel. Ask whether visitors are welcome and whether the occasion is a public festival, a commissioned presentation or a private observance. A household or devotional gathering needs an invitation; its cultural interest does not make it a tourist attraction.

Ask which form will be presented: seated recitation, standing Pala or Badi Pala between teams. Confirm the starting time, expected duration, language and whether a brief introduction to the episode is possible. Short extracts can offer an entrance; a fuller programme gives more room for the literary and comic exchanges. Agree on what is being offered rather than assuming every performance has the same format.[^pala-patra2017]

## Enjoy the evening, even if Odia is new to you

- A question: which poem, episode or text will we hear?
- An introduction: who is the Gayak, who plays the drum, and who are the answering performers?
- A little patience: follow a complete exchange before reaching for a translation or your phone.

Before going, ask for the episode’s title and a short account of its characters. If you need translation, ask whether an Odia-speaking companion can explain the premise before the performance and discuss it afterwards. English interpretation, subtitles and translated programmes should not be assumed.

Let the performance establish its rhythm. Watch how a performer changes a prop’s meaning and listen for the point where an answering voice alters the main explanation. You need not catch every quotation to notice a change of character, the drummer’s response or the audience’s recognition. Ask your questions during a suitable pause or afterwards rather than interrupting the exchange.

Confirm the occasion, duration and access with the host. Ask about seating, step-free access, toilets and the return journey, particularly if a programme may finish late. These are questions to settle with the organiser; facilities at one venue cannot be assumed at another.

## Be a guest who supports the work

Ask before taking photographs or recording sound or video; respect a refusal, and ask separately about posting an extract. Follow the host’s arrangements for seating, dress and devotional observances. Keep your phone silent and avoid blocking other listeners or performers.

If commissioning a presentation, agree with the troupe beforehand on the fee, rehearsal or teaching time, travel, duration and recording permissions. Ask what payment or contribution arrangement applies at a public event; do not assume entry is free or that a performer receives the whole ticket price. Requesting a shorter visitor introduction should be a choice made with the artists, not a demand to cut the programme down.

Take away the names of the artists and the work, not only an image of a costume. Credit the whole ensemble when the names are available. Follow an artist-authorised recording, attend another programme, or ask about an agreed teaching session. Visitor interest can create an opportunity; keeping Pala alive still depends on the people who practise it and the communities that give it a place.

# Pala and its culture

Pala belongs at the meeting point of performed poetry, musical storytelling and community life. A 2012 festival account connects these ballad traditions with literature, rhythm, humour and epic narratives. [Pala and Daskathia Utsav](https://www.narthaki.com/info/rev12/rev1245.html).

## Forms and history: preserve the source’s vocabulary
