title: "Pana Sankranti: A Drink for the Tulasi"
description: "In the first heat of the Odia year, a pot drips over the courtyard tulasi, bael ripens for pana, and families make enough for strangers."
---

A week before Pana Sankranti, the children in cookbook author Sweta Biswal’s family had a job. They scouted the neighbourhood for ripening *bela*, the hard-shelled bael fruit. Once the children reported back, their mothers or grandmothers got in touch with the neighbour who owned the tree. Someone climbed up to pick fruit that would finish ripening at home. By the time the Odia new year arrived in mid-April, the family had what it needed for the day’s drink. <sup id="ref-1-1"><a href="#source-1" aria-label="Footnote 1">1</a></sup>

In Cuttack, Parbati Sahoo described a different piece of preparation. A small clay pot must have a hole in its base, just large enough to let water escape a drop at a time. Suspended above the *tulasi*, the household’s sacred basil, it will go on watering the plant while the family offers prayers and shares *pana* with relatives, friends and anyone else who comes by. Sahoo also knew the custom of hanging the pot over a sacred stone. <sup id="ref-2-1"><a href="#source-2" aria-label="Footnote 2">2</a></sup>

## What goes in the pot

The raised courtyard shrine where the basil grows is the *tulasi chaura*. In his book *Fading Festivals of Odisha*, J. C. Mahanti describes a leafy canopy built above it for shade. Beneath it hangs the *Basundhara theki*, the little earthen pitcher. Mahanti records water or pana in the pitcher and a fresh filling each day for the month. Sahoo speaks of plain water in the pot. For a family that keeps the observance through Baisakha, hanging it on New Year’s Day is only the beginning. Someone has to come back tomorrow to fill it again. <sup id="ref-3-1"><a href="#source-3" aria-label="Footnote 3">3</a></sup> <sup id="ref-2-2"><a href="#source-2" aria-label="Footnote 2">2</a></sup>

Mahanti explains that the household honours the tulasi as Maa Brundabati and offers her relief from summer heat. In Garh Manitri in Khordha, anthropologist Akio Tanabe recorded what that offering meant in the village farming calendar. At Pana Sankranti, people gave a cooling drink to Mother Earth herself, grown hot and dry. The same village calendar has a later entry for [Akhi Muthi Anukula](/stories/akhi-muthi-anukula/), when the cultivator worships the earth and puts the first paddy seed into the ground. Before asking the soil to receive seed, this village gave it something to drink. <sup id="ref-3-2"><a href="#source-3" aria-label="Footnote 3">3</a></sup> <sup id="ref-4-1"><a href="#source-4" aria-label="Footnote 4">4</a></sup>

## What goes in the glass

Biswal cracks the shell of a ripe bela, loosens the flesh in water with her fingers and strains out the fibres and seeds. That gives her the liquid base. Banana and *chhena*, fresh cheese curds, give her version body. A little black pepper sharpens the sweetness. The festival takes its name from a whole family of drinks. Food writer Sujata Dehury describes a *guda pana* that needs only jaggery and water. Both have a place on a hot day. <sup id="ref-1-2"><a href="#source-1" aria-label="Footnote 1">1</a></sup> <sup id="ref-5-1"><a href="#source-5" aria-label="Footnote 5">5</a></sup>

Who gets the first serving depends on where one asks. In Bhubaneswar’s Nayapalli, a temple priest, Anshuman Mishra, described devotees bringing pana to offer to the deities and drinking it after the offering. At the Tarini shrine in Ghatagaon, Keonjhar, visitors on Pana Sankranti in 2024 queued to see the goddess dressed in gold. The servitors gave the visitors pana made with fruit, curd, grated fresh cheese and black pepper. <sup id="ref-6-1"><a href="#source-6" aria-label="Footnote 6">6</a></sup> <sup id="ref-7-1"><a href="#source-7" aria-label="Footnote 7">7</a></sup>

A family’s guests can extend well beyond its door. Manisha Dhir recalled her family holding a *homa*, a fire offering, at home and hosting neighbours for a meal at a nearby Durga temple. Her father and uncles took pana out to people on the street. Rabindra Sahu in Ganjam likewise described giving out pana as his way of observing the auspicious day. In Sahoo’s Cuttack household, there was food to give away alongside the drink, including bananas, coconut, jaggery and *chhatua*, roasted flour. <sup id="ref-8-1"><a href="#source-8" aria-label="Footnote 8">8</a></sup> <sup id="ref-9-1"><a href="#source-9" aria-label="Footnote 9">9</a></sup> <sup id="ref-2-3"><a href="#source-2" aria-label="Footnote 2">2</a></sup>

## The year the village has already heard

At [Dola Purnima](/stories/dola-purnima/), weeks earlier, villagers in Garh Manitri heard the new *panjika*, or almanac, read aloud before the deities. Dola fell on the full moon of Phalguna. Pana Sankranti (ପଣା ସଂକ୍ରାନ୍ତି), also called Maha Bishuba Sankranti, follows the sun. It opens the solar month of Baisakha and the Odia solar year. Tikina Panda, a priest of the Lingaraj temple in Bhubaneswar, identified this as the day the new *panji* comes into effect. A village could hear the year to come at Dola and still have weeks to wait for the first day of the solar year. <sup id="ref-4-2"><a href="#source-4" aria-label="Footnote 4">4</a></sup> <sup id="ref-8-2"><a href="#source-8" aria-label="Footnote 8">8</a></sup>

There is a way to reckon those weeks in the kitchen, too. Food researcher Swetak Abhisek Mohapatra describes homes offering the first raw mangoes to the gods at Dola, sometimes as *amba pana*. By the solar new year, the children in Biswal’s recollection had found ripe bela. These two accounts give [The Year That Feeds Us](/stories/the-year-that-feeds-us/) a measure of summer small enough to hold in one hand. A mango picked green. A bael left to ripen. Fresh water for the tulasi when the pot runs dry. <sup id="ref-5-2"><a href="#source-5" aria-label="Footnote 5">5</a></sup> <sup id="ref-1-3"><a href="#source-1" aria-label="Footnote 1">1</a></sup>
