title: "Puspuni: Who Gets a Share of the Harvest?"
description: "In western Odisha, children sing for rice, farmhands settle a year’s wages, and a village cook saves something special for the full moon of Pausa."
---

In Sambalpur, on a January morning in 2017, a photographer caught a group of children collecting food from households. Their call was *Chher Chhera*. One house could give rice, another vegetables, another a little money. The children could tease the older women who answered the door and ask for their blessings in the same visit. Between them, the households supplied what the children needed for a feast. <sup id="ref-1-1"><a href="#source-1" aria-label="Footnote 1">1</a></sup>

The occasion was Puspuni (ପୁଷପୁନି), the full moon of Pausa, when farming families in western Odisha celebrate after the monsoon paddy harvest. Earlier in the season, at [Nuakhai](/culture/osha/nuakhai/), a household sets aside some of the first new rice as an offering before the family eats. By Puspuni, the main crop has come in. A child can stand at the door of a house that has harvested paddy and ask for a share. So can a worker to whom some of that grain is owed. <sup id="ref-1-2"><a href="#source-1" aria-label="Footnote 1">1</a></sup> <sup id="ref-2-1"><a href="#source-2" aria-label="Footnote 2">2</a></sup>

## What a year’s work was worth

Writing in 2014, Sureswari Bagh described an arrangement under which a *halia*, a farm worker hired for an agricultural year, received wages largely in paddy. The agreement was oral. Bagh records the unit in which the paddy was reckoned, the *puti*, and the smaller measure, the *mann*, used to count it out. At Chher Chhera the parties settled the year’s account. A worker could agree to stay on or take up work with another farmer, sometimes at a better rate. <sup id="ref-2-2"><a href="#source-2" aria-label="Footnote 2">2</a></sup>

For the children making their rounds, paddy was a gift. For the halia bringing it home, it was pay. A family could have worked through the whole growing season and depend on that settlement for its own store of rice. Bagh was already describing changes to annual hiring, including workers leaving western Odisha for jobs farther south. The full moon is easier to mark on a calendar than the changing terms on which a household earns its living. <sup id="ref-2-3"><a href="#source-2" aria-label="Footnote 2">2</a></sup>

## A festival meal in Baigapada

Raj Kumar wrote about Puspuni in an account of his own village, Baigapada, in today’s Nuapada district. Rice was the staple. In a lean season, when both stored paddy and paid work ran short, a household might get by on *pakhala*, rice in water, with whatever salt, chilli or onion it had. On Puspuni, he remembered, people made a point of having at least a little non-vegetarian food. In a village where the season could determine how much accompanied the rice, that detail says something about the effort behind a festival meal. <sup id="ref-3-1"><a href="#source-3" aria-label="Footnote 3">3</a></sup>

Baigapada also gives us a way to read the two harvest festivals together. Kumar describes families offering the first rice at Nuakhai to village deities, the earth and ancestors. He names three days in its observance there, including *basi*, the day after Nuakhai. Both *basi* and Puspuni were occasions for that special meal. Someone remembering the taste might link them as closely as someone remembering the work would distinguish them. <sup id="ref-3-2"><a href="#source-3" aria-label="Footnote 3">3</a></sup>

Alok Chandra Barik, reporting for Akashvani Sambalpur, names other things a family might share with neighbours, among them *arisha* and *kakara* pitha. He also explains what happens to the grain the children collect. They pool it and cook a meal together. Barik hears in the custom an obligation towards households without land of their own. A child needs no field from which to bring a contribution. Joining the rounds is a way to help make the meal. <sup id="ref-4-1"><a href="#source-4" aria-label="Footnote 4">4</a></sup>

## When Bargarh keeps its feast

In Bargarh town another event helps set the date. During Dhanu Yatra the town stages the story of King Kansa. The Sambalpur report that photographed children making their rounds in 2017 also recorded that Bargarh town kept Puspuni a day later, after Kansa’s death in the play. A family following the town’s calendar could finish watching the drama before sitting down to its harvest feast. <sup id="ref-1-3"><a href="#source-1" aria-label="Footnote 1">1</a></sup>

In the Rairakhol area of Sambalpur district, villagers speaking to *OrissaPOST* remembered a change in where the grain itself went. With machines coming to harvest the paddy, landholders could sell it on the spot. The same villagers described groups going around for paddy or sweets, sometimes before the full moon. A crop sold at the field edge takes a different route from one brought home and measured out at the door. Their recollection gives a practical reason why a custom could grow quieter even in a place where people still knew the call. <sup id="ref-5-1"><a href="#source-5" aria-label="Footnote 5">5</a></sup>

## From one handful to another

At [Akhi Muthi Anukula](/stories/akhi-muthi-anukula/), a farmer takes a handful of seed out of the house to begin sowing. Between that handful and the rice the children bring home come the pause in working the earth at [Raja](/culture/osha/raja-parba/), the prayers for feet in flooded fields at [Chitau Amavasya](/stories/chitau-amavasya/) and the meal for the bullocks at [Gamha Purnima](/stories/gamha-purnima/). [Garbhana Sankranti](/stories/garbhana-sankranti/) attends to the grain still forming on the plant. At [Manabasa Gurubara](/culture/osha/manabasa-gurubara/), the grain measure itself has a place in Lakshmi’s worship.

Puspuni leaves us with that familiar measure put to work. Someone counts out what a worker has earned. Somewhere else children arrive with enough rice to put a pot on the fire. Those are two answers farming families have given to a question that lasts beyond the harvest. Once the rice is home, who gets to eat it?
