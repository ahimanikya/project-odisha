# Sankha ଶଙ୍ଖ and the sound of arrival

*Sounds of Odisha*

Imagine a courtyard pausing for one long breath. A conch rises to someone's lips. Conversation gives way to a ringing note; then the bright ripple of huluhuli answers it. Before anyone announces a welcome, the welcome has already been heard.

Sankha, the conch, connects several Odishas: the wedding threshold, the remembered maritime journey, Puri's sacred geography and the performing traditions of Ganjam. Following its sound means following people as much as an object.[^sankha-ezcc-2012][^sankha-jena-2023][^sankha-mishra-2009][^huluhuli-thurston-1906]

## A shell becomes an instrument

A shell is already a remarkable form: a winding interior that opens into a broad mouth. Making a trumpet from it changes how air enters that interior. In the Metropolitan Museum of Art’s Immaterial discussion, curator Bradley Strauchen-Scherer describes cutting off the tip to create a small blowing opening. That modification gives a player access to the shell’s natural passage. It describes an end-blown shell trumpet; it is not a documented workshop recipe from Ganjam.

The player does more than breathe through a hole. The lips vibrate, repeatedly admitting small pulses of air. The air inside the shell resonates and acts back on the lips, helping sustain a note. This interaction between a vibrating player and a shaped air passage is why museums classify the instrument as a lip-vibrated aerophone. The shell is neither a whistle that sounds from any passing breeze nor a loudspeaker for an ordinary spoken voice.

Acoustic work by Miriam A. Kolar and colleagues makes the interaction measurable. Their study at Chavín de Huántar in Peru tested nineteen excavated shell horns with player-blown sounds and separate impulse measurements. The instruments produced a range of pitches, and the player’s contribution affected the result. These are measurements of particular Peruvian shells, not a frequency chart for Odisha. They explain why the object’s shape and the musician’s technique must be considered together.

Shell playing can also involve the hand. In the Met conversation, musician Steve Turre describes changing notes by moving a hand at the shell’s opening and using different shells for a wider musical range. That is a named musician’s practice. It helps us hear possibilities without assigning his method or repertoire to every Sankha player.

The Met’s late nineteenth-century instrument from Vanuatu offers another comparison. A similar way of making sound can occur in different cultural worlds. Neither that object nor the Peruvian experiments establish a migration route to Odisha, an inventor of Sankha, or a date when Odia people began playing it.[^sankha-met-shells][^sankha-met-vanuatu][^sankha-kolar-2012]

## A welcome at the threshold

A description published in Edgar Thurston's Ethnographic Notes in Southern India in 1906 places conch shells beside women's huluhuli and recited verses when a groom arrives at the bride's house. The passage concerns Badhoyis and other communities in Ganjam.

It is a historical glimpse of sounds working together. The conch gives the arrival weight; the voices make it communal. This is an interpretation of that scene, not a fixed musical rule. The account records a practice by 1906, without telling us when it began or how every community celebrated a wedding.

Read it alongside Huluhuli: one sound comes through a shell, the other through the human voice. Their meeting is where the scene becomes more than an inventory of instruments.[^huluhuli-thurston-1906]

## Puri carries the conch in its name

Puri is known as Sankha Kshetra, among its several sacred names. Pramodini Jena's account in Odisha Review places that name alongside Nilachala, Srikshetra and Purusottam Kshetra.

Here the conch becomes a way of imagining place. Its significance reaches beyond the moment someone blows it: it belongs to the city's devotional identity. Sacred geography offers a cultural reading of a landscape; it should not be mistaken for a modern cadastral boundary or proof that a city was literally built inside a shell.

For a visitor, this gives Puri another layer. Listen to the city, then notice how a familiar sound also lives in a name. The sea and the sacred city become connected in the imagination, without needing an invented origin story.[^sankha-jena-2023]

## A blessing for the remembered voyage

Parikhit Mishra's 2009 essay describes a Kartika full-moon commemoration in which a decorated vessel makes a short journey and women blow conches to wish the participants a safe voyage and return.

The small journey carries a larger memory. The sound can be heard as a blessing across the distance between departure and homecoming. That emotional reading helps explain why maritime remembrance is more than a list of goods and ports.

The essay describes a commemorative practice. It does not supply a contemporary record of ancient sailors' departure ceremonies. Keep the remembered past and the documented past in conversation, without making them interchangeable.[^sankha-mishra-2009]

## Ganjam turns breath into performance

Jodi Sankha brings conch playing and movement together. An Eastern Zonal Cultural Centre description in the official 2012 Republic Day programme connects it with Ganjam’s Thakurani Jatra processions. It describes coordinated conch modulation and bodily movement, accompanied by dhol, changu and mahuri.

The Odisha Reference Annual of 2017 calls Jodi Sankha “Double Conch” and describes badya and nrutya—instrumental playing and dance—combined. This changes what deserves attention. The task is not simply to hold a long note while standing still: sounding and moving form one performance. Watch the relation between a phrase, a change of posture and the accompanying musicians. Those relationships are part of the work, even when a photograph makes the conches seem like the whole event.

The same annual names Gangeswar Jugal Sankha Badya Lokanrutya Kala Anusthan at Narendrapur in Ganjam as an institution performing Jodi Sankha and Ranapa. A named place and organisation bring the account closer to the people who sustain it. The 2017 record documents that association; it does not establish the institution’s founding date or its present programme.

An AIR Cuttack roster supplies individual names. Its Jodi Sankha entries list Sarat Ch. Mohapatra both alone and with a party, with an audition date of 27 October 2011. Rajendra Prasad Patra and party appear under Sankha Badya with an audition date of 28 May 2012. These are three roster entries, not three independent biographies or proof of particular broadcasts. They show how a regional practice also enters the categories and audition systems of a broadcasting institution.

Ranapa needs its own identity in this account. The 2017 annual describes it as dancing on stilts; the shared institution performs both forms. Odisha’s Culture Department likewise groups Jodi Sankha / Ranapa in an administrative category. Sharing a listing or a troupe does not make conch playing and stilt dancing the same art. Nor should an origin account attached to one be transferred to the other.[^sankha-ezcc-2012][^sankha-ora-2017][^sankha-air-cuttack][^sankha-ctms]

## A sound can carry home overseas

A Michigan chapter report in the Odia community magazine Utkarsa in 2013 describes sankha and hulahuli at a Ganesh Puja gathering.

A familiar pairing has crossed an ocean. For people who recognise it, that sound may summon a courtyard or a celebration far away. This is one documented diaspora gathering; the memories it evokes will differ from listener to listener.[^huluhuli-osa-2013]

## Meet the people behind the sound

If you encounter a public Jodi Sankha performance, learn the troupe’s name and where its members learned. Ask how a player selects a shell, how the blowing opening is prepared and how breath is coordinated with movement. Those are practical questions this desk research cannot settle for a particular Ganjam performer. Listen for the supporting instruments as well as the conch.

For a ritual, follow the hosts' lead. Ask before recording people or entering a private celebration. A recording belongs in a living-heritage collection only with the performer's permission, a clear description of its setting and agreed credit.

The most revealing question may be simple: Who taught you to make that sound? It opens a story of practice, family, teachers and places. The shell is memorable. The person who makes it speak is the reason to stay.
