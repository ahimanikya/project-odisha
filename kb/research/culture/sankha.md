# Sankha ଶଙ୍ଖ and the sound of arrival

*Sounds of Odisha*

Imagine a courtyard pausing for one long breath. A conch rises to someone's lips. Conversation gives way to a ringing note; then the bright ripple of huluhuli answers it. Before anyone announces a welcome, the welcome has already been heard.

**Sankha, the conch, connects several Odishas:** the wedding threshold, the remembered maritime journey, Puri's sacred geography and the performing traditions of Ganjam. Following its sound means following people as much as an object.

## A shell becomes an instrument

A conch trumpet begins with a shell, but the player brings it to life. The Metropolitan Museum of Art classifies one such instrument as a lip-vibrated aerophone: sound begins with the player's lips, rather than a string or drumhead. Its discussion with curator Bradley Strauchen-Scherer describes lips and hand shaping the note.[^sankha-met-vanuatu][^sankha-met-shells]

That makes the breath part of the story. A shell displayed on a shelf cannot tell us how a musician starts a note, sustains it or brings it to a close. Those details belong to a person who has learned to play.

The instrument also travels across cultures. The Met's late nineteenth-century example comes from Vanuatu. It gives us a useful comparison, rather than evidence of a route from Odisha to the Pacific. Similar instruments do not, on their own, establish shared ancestry.[^sankha-met-vanuatu]

## A welcome at the threshold

A description published in Edgar Thurston's *Ethnographic Notes in Southern India* in 1906 places conch shells beside women's huluhuli and recited verses when a groom arrives at the bride's house. The passage concerns Badhoyis and other communities in Ganjam.[^huluhuli-thurston-1906]

It is a historical glimpse of sounds working together. The conch gives the arrival weight; the voices make it communal. This is an interpretation of that scene, not a fixed musical rule. The account records a practice by 1906, without telling us when it began or how every community celebrated a wedding.

Read it alongside [Huluhuli](huluhuli.md): one sound comes through a shell, the other through the human voice. Their meeting is where the scene becomes more than an inventory of instruments.

## Puri carries the conch in its name

Puri is known as **Sankha Kshetra**, among its several sacred names. Pramodini Jena's account in *Odisha Review* places that name alongside Nilachala, Srikshetra and Purusottam Kshetra.[^sankha-jena-2023]

Here the conch becomes a way of imagining place. Its significance reaches beyond the moment someone blows it: it belongs to the city's devotional identity. Sacred geography offers a cultural reading of a landscape; it should not be mistaken for a modern cadastral boundary or proof that a city was literally built inside a shell.

For a visitor, this gives Puri another layer. Listen to the city, then notice how a familiar sound also lives in a name. The sea and the sacred city become connected in the imagination, without needing an invented origin story.

## A blessing for the remembered voyage

Parikhit Mishra's 2009 essay describes a Kartika full-moon commemoration in which a decorated vessel makes a short journey and women blow conches to wish the participants a safe voyage and return.[^sankha-mishra-2009]

The small journey carries a larger memory. The sound can be heard as a blessing across the distance between departure and homecoming. That emotional reading helps explain why maritime remembrance is more than a list of goods and ports.

The essay describes a commemorative practice. It does not supply a contemporary record of ancient sailors' departure ceremonies. Keep the remembered past and the documented past in conversation, without making them interchangeable.

## Ganjam turns breath into performance

**Jodi Sankha** brings conch playing and movement together. An Eastern Zonal Cultural Centre description in the official 2012 Republic Day programme connects it with Ganjam's Thakurani Jatra processions. It describes coordinated conch modulation and bodily movement, accompanied by dhol, changu and mahuri.[^sankha-ezcc-2012]

This is a compelling reason to watch as well as listen. Follow the phrase and the body together: how does a movement meet a note, and how does the ensemble carry the next moment? A performance makes the skill visible.

Recognition appears in institutional records too. Odisha's Culture Department includes **Jodi Sankha / Ranapa** in its folk-dance categories, while an AIR Cuttack roster lists **Jodi Sankha / Sankha Badya**, with audition entries dated 2011 and 2012.[^sankha-ctms][^sankha-air-cuttack] These records establish named performance categories, not the number of practitioners working today. Ranapa and Jodi Sankha also remain distinct forms, even when an administrative category groups them together.

## A sound can carry home overseas

A Michigan chapter report in the Odia community magazine *Utkarsa* in 2013 describes sankha and hulahuli at a Ganesh Puja gathering.[^huluhuli-osa-2013]

A familiar pairing has crossed an ocean. For people who recognise it, that sound may summon a courtyard or a celebration far away. This is one documented diaspora gathering; the memories it evokes will differ from listener to listener.

## Meet the people behind the sound

If you encounter a public Jodi Sankha performance, take time to learn the troupe's name and where its members learned. Ask what the occasion means to them. Listen for the supporting instruments rather than treating the conch as the whole ensemble.

For a ritual, follow the hosts' lead. Ask before recording people or entering a private celebration. A recording belongs in a living-heritage collection only with the performer's permission, a clear description of its setting and agreed credit.

The most revealing question may be simple: **Who taught you to make that sound?** It opens a story of practice, family, teachers and places. The shell is memorable. The person who makes it speak is the reason to stay.
