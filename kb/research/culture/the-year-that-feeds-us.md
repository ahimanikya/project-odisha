---
title: The Year That Feeds Us
description: Follow the rice through Raja, Nuakhai and Manabasa Gurubara, from the resting earth to the first mouthful and the household grain measure.
---

At Raja, a piece of pitha may leave the house where it was cooked and arrive as a gift in another. At Nuakhai, the rice a family shares comes from the new crop. On a Thursday in Margasira, a household sets out a measure of grain for Lakshmi. Follow the rice through these three Odia observances and the question changes each time. Who did the work that makes a holiday possible? Who tastes the harvest first? Who is welcome to eat?

## When the earth rests

As the rains approach, Basumati, the earth goddess, is understood to menstruate. In an account of Raja concerned especially with Cuttack and its surroundings, cultivation pauses for three days. The pause reaches into the kitchen. Grinding and cooking take time, so families prepare food ahead. Poda pitha keeps its place in the celebration even while the people who made it can put down their tools. Some of the cakes travel between relatives and neighbours. A portion arriving at the door tells you something a photograph of a Raja swing might miss. Someone began the festival’s work before the holiday began. <sup id="ref-1"><a href="#source-1" aria-label="Footnote 1">1</a></sup>

There is another moment in Odisha’s rice year when people speak of a field as they might speak of a body. In his study of rice rituals in the Odisha plains, Misra describes Garbhana Sankranti, when the developing paddy was understood through pregnancy. A branch used in household worship was carried out to the field. The family’s prayer went with it, at a time when there was still no grain to carry home. Gujarat’s Garba offers a visual parallel. Dancers circle a perforated earthen vessel sheltering a lamp, or an image of Amba. What the vessel holds and what the stalk holds belong to different devotional traditions. Placing them side by side lets us see why growth itself can deserve an occasion. <sup id="ref-2"><a href="#source-2" aria-label="Footnote 2">2</a></sup> <sup id="ref-3"><a href="#source-3" aria-label="Footnote 3">3</a></sup>

## The first mouthful

In western Odisha, Nuakhai marks the point at which people may eat the new grain. In Kesavan’s account from a village in Bargarh, the village priest distributes grain from the new crop. Ground and mixed with jaggery, it is offered to deities and ancestors. Family members then take their share from a leaf bowl. The amount can be small. What matters here is the order of giving and eating. The ancestors have a place in the meal before anyone tastes the season’s rice. Other accounts name other preparations. The first grain can be ready while work continues in the fields. <sup id="ref-4"><a href="#source-4" aria-label="Footnote 4">4</a></sup>

In Tamil Nadu, a harvest pot gives thanks a different form. At Pongal, rice cooks until the pot overflows, a sign of abundance. Worship honours the Sun, and Mattu Pongal honours the cattle whose labour helped bring the crop in. Where the Bargarh account draws our attention to the first portion, Pongal asks us to look back across the field at what made a potful possible. <sup id="ref-5"><a href="#source-5" aria-label="Footnote 5">5</a></sup>

## A measure in the house

On the Thursdays of Margasira, the *mana*, a household grain measure, has a place in the worship of Lakshmi. The accompanying *Lakshmi Purana*, associated with Balaram Das, asks a harder question of a household with food to give. In Balabhadra Ghadai’s retelling, Lakshmi honours Sriya, whose caste makes the visit objectionable to Balabhadra. Jagannath shuts Lakshmi out. Later, hungry and unable to feed themselves, the brothers reach her door. They finally accept the food she has cooked. Read alongside the grain measure, that reversal gives prosperity a demanding meaning. A house may have rice enough to measure and still have to answer for whom it excludes. <sup id="ref-6"><a href="#source-6" aria-label="Footnote 6">6</a></sup>

These observances come from different parts of Odisha. A family in Bargarh need not keep the same calendar as one in Cuttack for their stories to illuminate one another. Read them together and even an ordinary serving of rice invites a second look. Before anyone could offer it, the earth had to bear it. Before anyone could eat it, someone had to bring it in and cook it. And when it was ready, someone decided whose bowl to fill.
