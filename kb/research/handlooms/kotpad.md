# Kotpad textiles

**A colour can lead you back to the hands that made it.**

Begin with a red thread. Follow it into a pattern, then ask what happened before the cloth existed. Kotpad invites that slower journey: from a finished surface to dye preparation, weaving and the names behind the work.

## Follow the colour towards Koraput

*Sustainability in the Handloom Traditions of India*, hosted by the Development Commissioner for Handlooms, associates Kotpad’s weaving with the Mirgan community in Koraput district. Its account names cotton yarn, tussar silk and aul roots among the materials, and describes red, maroon, brown and black in the textile palette.[^kotpad-textiles]

The report traces preparation through washing, drying and dyeing before yarn reaches the loom. That sequence gives colour a history. It also gives a visitor a better question: who prepared and dyed the yarn in this particular piece?

Its description is an introduction to a documented tradition. Fibre, dye ingredients and finishing still belong to the individual cloth; natural-dye language alone cannot establish organic certification or measured environmental benefit.

## A small motif, a particular maker

Dastkari Haat Samiti’s 2017 documentation offers another way in. It identifies crabs, fish, vessels and axes among the woven motifs, naming an axe as *tangi* and a fish as *machari*. The story credits text to **Jaya Jaitly**, photographs to **Chirodeep Chaudhuri**, and artisan work to **Gobardhan Panika and the community**.[^kotpad-motifs]

Open the documented images and choose one figure. What does its shape make you notice? Let its maker’s explanation guide any meaning you attach to it. A familiar-looking object can have a story that your first glance misses.

Panika’s name also appears in the Ministry of Home Affairs announcement dated **25 January 2018**, under Padma Shri, with the field Art-Weaving and state Odisha. That document spells his name **Gobaradhan Panika**. The earlier research below retains the spelling variants and a directory’s conflicting 2017 award year; the ministry announcement supports 2018.[^handloom-padma2018]

A credited work and an award record help us name a weaving life. They do not identify the maker of every Kotpad textile you encounter.

## Give the moving story some time

PSBT’s producer catalogue lists **Biswanath Rath’s** *Kotpad Weaving: The Story of a Race Against Time* as a **2018**, **29-minute** film in **Odia and English**, concerned with the textile tradition and natural dyeing.[^kotpad-film]

Follow the producer’s page for viewing information. This profile draws on its catalogue entry; the complete film has not been reviewed here, and current screening availability remains unconfirmed. A documentary can be a companion to learning about the place before you travel.

## Bring home a detail and its credit

If a maker welcomes a conversation, ask about one motif, the fibre and the way this cloth should be cared for. Keep the maker’s documented name with the piece. For a demonstration or workshop visit, agree the host, time, fee and photography permission in advance.

A good encounter leaves room for an answer you did not expect. Write down what was explained, what you noticed yourself, and what remains a question. That is a more useful souvenir than a story you cannot trace.
