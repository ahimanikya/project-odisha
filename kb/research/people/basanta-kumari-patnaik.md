# Basanta Kumari Patnaik: Writing, Publishing and the Screen

By Ahimanikya Satapathy

In Basanta Kumari Patnaik’s story *Velvet Mites*, Nanda has sold her house to pay for two years of study abroad. Around her, the prospect of leaving takes a tender, ordinary form: Dheera’s mother worries about her meals and presses more food on her. Rohan Kar’s English translation lets ambition and affection share the scene.[^velvet]

A wish to leave can sit beside the comfort of being cared for. That closeness is a way into Basanta’s fiction: a large decision taking shape among small gestures. Her work belongs in a cinema reading journey through ଅମଡ଼ା ବାଟ (Amada Bata), but the screen offers only one entrance. There are other stories, other characters and the practical work she undertook to bring books into circulation.

## The life within ordinary words

Jatindra K. Nayak, who translated *In Bondage*, draws attention to Basanta’s irony, understatement and domestic settings. He describes the difficulty of carrying her idiomatic Odia into English.[^nayak] His account invites a reader to notice how much can depend on the shape of an expression.

In the passage from *In Bondage* reproduced with Nayak’s commentary, the narrator describes household work filling day and night. Even free time becomes knitting, leaving a pile of pullovers she wonders what to do with.[^nayak] Here, domestic life closes around a woman’s time.

His translation appears in *Spark of Light* (2016), edited by Valerie Henitiuk and Supriya Kar.[^spark] The anthology offers a route into her short fiction in English.

## Making books as well as writing them

Basanta earned an MA in economics at Ravenshaw College, Cuttack. With her brother she established Shanti Nibas Bani Mandira, a publishing firm that operated from 1959 to 1962. Her novels include *Amada Bata*, *Chorabali* and *Alibha Chita*.[^spark]

The publishing venture gives her literary life another dimension. Writing a book and helping books reach readers involve different kinds of responsibility. The recorded partnership places her within both worlds. It offers a concrete starting point for remembering women’s work in the making and circulation of Odia literature, alongside their presence as authors.

## A novel meets the screen

Black Eagle Books identifies *Amada Bata* with the 1964 film starring Jharana Das.[^novel] The film database credits Basanta’s story, Gopal Chhotray’s screenplay, Amar Ganguly’s direction and Babulal Doshi’s production.[^film]

The [literature-and-cinema chapter](../culture/cinema-series/literature-and-screen.md) follows a later publishing connection through Manas Ranjan Mahapatra’s recollection. Here, that path leads back to the woman who wrote the novel and had herself worked in publishing. Her place in the story extends beyond the moment an adaptation reached the screen.

## Finding the writer again

Odisha’s official award table records Basanta Kumari Patnaik’s Atibadi Jagannath Das award for 2001.[^award] The recognition sits beside something readers can still do: seek out a work and spend time with its language.

Begin with one of the available translations, keeping its translator’s name in view. A story can lead towards another story, an Odia edition, or a conversation about what changes between languages. For publishers and reading groups, making such connections easier to follow is a modest, useful possibility: a clear edition, a credited translation and a route to the writer’s wider work.
