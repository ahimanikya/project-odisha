## Selected reading introduction · 20 October 2026

A knot in a rope can count a year of work. It cannot decide how much debt remains. In Paraja, Gopinath Mohanty makes unequal power visible through such small, ordinary things.

### What a knot can count

In a passage from Paraja reproduced in an IGNOU study unit, a goti, or debt-bound labourer, marks each completed year of service with a knot in a rope. The same study traces how a fine leads Sukru Jani to borrow from the moneylender, the Sahukar. Working for him keeps Sukru and his sons from ploughing their own land.

The rope records time faithfully, but the lender controls the terms on which time becomes repayment. Read the image beside the account of the family’s work: losing the use of one’s land is also losing the time needed to live from it.

This reading begins with selected English passages reproduced in teaching material. They give us something precise to return to; they do not stand in for the whole novel or its Odia text.

### Who speaks, and in whose language?

Another teaching unit places transliterated Odia beside the English translation. In a chapter-two encounter, the Forest Guard addresses Jili while she washes leaves for cooking. The unit points to changes between local speech, colloquial Odia and the narrator’s standard Odia. In English, the reader is told that he speaks her dialect, but cannot hear those shifts in the same way.

That is a useful question to carry into a translation: when does a voice sound different from the narrator’s, and how is that difference conveyed? A note about dialect, a retained word and a change of sentence rhythm do different kinds of work.

### A landscape that measures loss

The unit also shows a field mouse whose lifetime of gathering can be scattered by one gust. Here, an animal’s small store gives the reader a scale for human vulnerability: the long effort of accumulation against the speed of loss.

Return to the rope and the mouse together. One measures years; the other makes destruction almost instantaneous. What happens to the familiar picture of a rural landscape when we notice labour, time and the possibility of dispossession within it?

### The writer’s position

Born in 1914 at Nagabali in Cuttack, Mohanty entered administrative service in 1938. His memorial biography places his early postings in Koraput and connects them with his interest in local languages and lives. It also records his translations of Tolstoy’s War and Peace and Gorky’s My Universities: his literary world extended beyond the landscapes for which he is often remembered.

An administrator can encounter both everyday life and the institutions that govern it. That position is worth keeping in mind as we read. A novelist’s choices of scene, voice and sympathy remain choices; fictional characters cannot speak for every member of a community today.
