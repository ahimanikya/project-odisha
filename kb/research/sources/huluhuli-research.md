## Historical and cultural conclusions

1. Named practice documented in a Ganjam wedding by1906. Earlier references remain possible;1906 is a retrieved attestation, not an invention date.
2. Auspicious worship and women's participation are documented within particular sources. Sacred meaning is a cultural belief, not demonstrated acoustic or medical efficacy.
3. Gadaba Dhemsa, household worship and diaspora examples are distinct settings; no tribal-to-Vedic origin hierarchy is inferred.
4. Regional and global similarities support comparison, not exclusive ownership or a proved transmission route.
5. Gendered participation merits recognition; no claim that every Odia woman can or must perform, or that only women anywhere can physically ululate.
6. Ranpur service history complicates an exclusively celebratory account, with source attribution and underlying-record gap explicit.

## Literary and language leads still open

Samal's displayed Khudurukuni stanza remains a lead requiring original book, edition, author attribution and page. No Bhagabata passage/date supplied. Research wedding songs, osha texts and named modern recordings by edition and performer before quoting or embedding. Do not use a shared title as proof of a canonical famous song.

## Next useful field research

Record testimony as testimony with locality and date, not origin proof. No outreach has occurred. Seek permission for a short unprocessed audio demonstration, spoken context and reuse terms; no rights cleared or recording obtained yet.

A Sounds of Odisha collection could connect huluhuli, conch, mahuri, mardala and work songs, each with its own sourced identity and locality.
