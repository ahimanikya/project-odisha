# Koraput, Sambalpur and Bargarh: people and work baselines

**The newer nutrition survey shows a mixed picture.** Stunting estimates fell in all three districts, while wasting and underweight estimates rose in Koraput and Bargarh. NFHS-6 figures are provisional and come from Odisha fieldwork in July–November 2024; they do not describe 2026 prevalence. The older Census education and work baselines remain below for historical context.

| Bargarh | 74.62% | 83.68% | 65.38% | 762,092 | 51.45% |

Sources: the respective 2011 District Census Handbooks, published in 2014. “Important Statistics” appears on PDF pages 23 (Koraput), 29 (Sambalpur) and 26 (Bargarh). Literacy denominator explanations appear on PDF pages 27, 33 and 31 respectively. See the original documents in this page’s source metadata and the [observation ledger](../references/data/statistics-atlas.json).

Workers include both main and marginal workers. The percentage uses the entire district population, including children, as its denominator. It is not an adult employment rate, formal-job count, labour-force participation rate or evidence of a workforce presently available for recruitment. The geography is each district at Census 2011, not a city or the older undivided Sambalpur region.

|---|---:|---:|---:|

| Sambalpur | 40.7% | 25.5% | 36.3% |
| Bargarh | 38.9% | 18.0% | 30.0% |

The Ministry of Women and Child Development reproduced these NFHS-5 estimates in the Annexure to [Lok Sabha question 1060, answered 22 July 2022](https://sansad.in/getFile/loksabhaquestions/annex/179/AU1060.pdf?source=pqals), PDF page 3. They are adverse historical findings worth retaining alongside economic stories.

The annexure supplies the national survey-round label, not each district’s exact fieldwork dates, valid-measurement sample sizes or confidence intervals. Exact district dates, child measurement denominators and confidence intervals remain unknown; the later methods update below supplies overall survey sample sizes and statewide fieldwork windows. These percentages must not be added together: the indicators can overlap. Do not multiply them by Census 2011 population to estimate affected children, present them as current prevalence, or treat small differences as statistically established rankings.

These three measures use height relative to age, weight relative to height, and weight relative to age respectively; each threshold is below two standard deviations of the WHO standard. ## What the sample sizes do—and do not—mean

| District | NFHS-5 households / women / men | NFHS-6 households / women / men |
|---|---:|---:|
| Koraput | 885 / 982 / 140 | 985 / 1,007 / 145 |
| Sambalpur | 890 / 876 / 133 | 975 / 926 / 116 |
| Bargarh | 896 / 891 / 145 | 999 / 948 / 153 |

These are overall survey sample totals, **not denominators for child nutrition percentages**. NFHS-5 district introductions provide the older totals; the NFHS-6 appendix provides the newer ones. Exact eligible child samples and confidence intervals remain unavailable in the inspected fact sheets.

The stated Odisha fieldwork windows were 19 January–21 March2020 and 30 November2020–31 March2021 for NFHS-5, and 18 July–28 November2024 for NFHS-6. These statewide windows do not establish each district’s dates. NFHS-6 retains a two-stage design but changes the urban first-stage frame to Urban Frame Survey blocks; it explicitly does not support district rural/urban estimates. Exact geographic comparability with Census2011 remains unresolved.

The fact sheets mark small samples with parentheses and suppress estimates with an asterisk. None of the nine adopted NFHS-6 nutrition estimates carries either mark, but that does not supply a precise sample denominator. Preserve these limits when developing stories, comparing places or considering local business needs.
