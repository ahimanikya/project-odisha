In [A Week in Bhubaneswar: Conversations and New Beginnings](https://projectodisha.org/stories/bhubaneswar-week/), I wrote about the people I had been meeting and the unexpected directions those conversations were taking me. An old poem had led me back towards music. Writers, filmmakers and heritage storyteller Shakti Badjena had given me more to think about, and more reasons to explore Odisha.

Today, that exploration took me to Parsurameswara Temple and then to an evening of Odissi music beside Bindu Sagar. I have visited many temples in Bhubaneswar, but I had never visited this one.

I joined Ekamra Walks for *Bhubaneswar by Night*. Prateek was our guide for the temple visit; Omm Prakash and team performed for us during the music session.[^1] We had already written about heritage walks in Project Odisha. This time, I could add my own account of taking part.

There was plenty to photograph and plenty to listen to. At the temple, Prateek explained the architecture, pointed out sculptures and told us the stories associated with them. Later, the musicians sat before us on striped mats, with notebooks open and the lights around Bindu Sagar behind them.

![Omm Prakash and team seated on striped mats beside Bindu Sagar, with notebooks open before them](https://projectodisha.org/assets/ekamra-evening/odissi-baithaki.jpg)

*Omm Prakash and team at the waterside baithaki during Bhubaneswar by Night.*

## At Parsurameswara with Prateek

The tower of [Parsurameswara Temple](https://maps.app.goo.gl/oYQXgNp6bzWaNTEr5?g_st=iw) was lit against a deep blue sky. Light picked out the projecting stonework, with shadows between the carved surfaces. My photographs show the building from several angles, as well as close views of its figures and patterns.

Prateek began with the structure. He described the sanctuary tower as a *rekha deula*, a form associated with Kalinga architecture. Beside it was the lower *jagamohana*, or entrance hall. He drew our attention to its rectangular form and how it differed from the pyramidal halls of many later Odishan temples.

I could follow the explanation by looking from the tower to the hall. These were architectural terms attached to something directly in front of us. Parsurameswara is generally dated to the seventh century and belongs to the early development of Odisha’s temple architecture.[^2]

![The illuminated tower and low entrance hall of Parsurameswara Temple](https://projectodisha.org/assets/ekamra-evening/parsurameswara-temple.jpg)

*The sanctuary tower and the low hall beside it—the starting point for Prateek’s architectural explanation.*

He also discussed stone construction and the use of iron clamps to secure building elements. His account brought the making of the temple into the conversation: how the stones were joined, as well as what was carved on them.

When he spoke about the Shailodbhava dynasty, he included a legend about its origins near Mahendragiri. In the version we heard, a figure emerged from stone and became the founder of a ruling line. The visit included both historical explanations and stories passed down about the place; the legend was part of that storytelling.

There was a great deal to see on the walls. Above one doorway sat Ganesha, surrounded by attendant figures and decorative carving. Higher up, an inscription ran across a stone beam. Faces appeared within curling frames, and small patterned screens broke up the solid stonework.

The wide photographs show how densely the temple is carved. The close-ups let me return to individual details: a face, an ornament, a line of lettering.

![Ganesha and attendant figures above a temple doorway, with an inscription on the beam above](https://projectodisha.org/assets/ekamra-evening/ganesha-doorway.jpg)

*Ganesha above the doorway, with an inscription on the stone beam overhead.*

Prateek introduced the row of mother goddesses as the Saptamatrikas. He explained their associations with divine powers and their place in mythological battles. Along the panel, the seated figures have distinct ornaments and attributes. Flowers lie beneath them, and red marks of worship touch the stone.

Those details were present throughout the visit. Flowers had been placed on sculptures; in another photograph, a priest stands at the doorway. We were touring a temple where worship continues. The carvings we had come to examine were also being cared for as sacred images.

![The carved mother-goddess panel, with flowers beneath the figures and red marks of worship](https://projectodisha.org/assets/ekamra-evening/mother-goddesses.jpg)

*The mother-goddess panel, with flowers and marks of worship.*

The Sahasra Linga was another object Prateek asked us to notice. He explained it as a linga bearing a thousand miniature lingas. In the photograph, rows of small carved forms cover its lower surface. Flowers rest on top, and a small earthen lamp sits near the base.

Prateek also spoke about Lakulisa and the Pashupata tradition. These names joined the architectural terms and sculptural details in my notes. There was more here than I could take in during one visit, but I now had particular things to read about and look for again.

![The Sahasra Linga, with miniature lingas carved around its surface and flowers on top](https://projectodisha.org/assets/ekamra-evening/sahasra-linga.jpg)

*The Sahasra Linga and its repeated miniature carvings.*

## The stories Prateek told us

From the temple, Prateek’s account widened to Ekamra Kshetra and the great mango tree remembered in its mythology. In the story he narrated, a self-manifested linga stood beneath the tree. Parvati came to this place beloved by Shiva and took the form of a cowherd woman, Gopalini.

We heard about the two asuras, Kirti and Vasu, who encountered her. In Prateek’s telling, she asked them to carry her, then became so heavy that they were crushed beneath her weight. He connected this episode with a place remembered for the goddess’s footstep.

The story of Bindu Sagar followed. Prateek described Shiva bringing together waters from sacred rivers to quench Parvati’s thirst. He presented it as an expression of love. Someone is thirsty; someone who loves her responds. The scale belongs to mythology, but the need at the centre of the story is familiar.

He also narrated Ravana’s attempt to lift Kailash: the mountain shaking, Parvati’s alarm and Shiva pressing down with his toe, trapping Ravana beneath it. The account continued through Ravana’s suffering and devotion. Prateek pointed out a sculptural representation associated with the episode and explained that it could be difficult to see clearly, even in daylight.

These stories gave us something specific to look for in the carvings. They also connected the temple visit with the place where we would listen to music later that evening.

At Bindu Sagar, Lingaraja’s illuminated tower stood beyond the trees and buildings. Reflections crossed the water. My photographs include both the temple in the distance and the coloured lights of the fountains nearer the shore. This was the setting for the second part of our evening.

![Lingaraja’s illuminated tower beyond the trees and lights around Bindu Sagar](https://projectodisha.org/assets/ekamra-evening/bindu-sagar-lingaraja.jpg)

*Lingaraja seen across Bindu Sagar at night.*

## Listening to Omm Prakash and team

Omm Prakash and his team performed for the participants beside the water. I was among the listeners.

The musicians sat together on striped mats. A large barrel-shaped drum rested before the percussionist. Notebooks and loose pages lay open in front of the performers. In the photographs, a musician wears a bright yellow kurta, and a woman in a patterned sari sits beside him. The arrangement was simple, with the city lights visible behind them.

The Odissi session was an amazing experience for me. The singers were also teaching us as they performed, explaining the music, its poetry and the ideas behind it. I could listen and learn in the same sitting. That combination made the evening special.

Listening to Odissi music with that spectacular view of Lingaraj Temple was simply blissful.

![Another moment from the Odissi baithaki, with the three musicians seated together beside the water](https://projectodisha.org/assets/ekamra-evening/omm-prakash-and-team.jpg)

*Singing and teaching together: Omm Prakash and team introduced us to the music as they performed.*

Jayadeva’s *Gita Govinda* was an important part of the discussion. We heard about its place in Odisha’s musical and devotional traditions. The twelfth-century Sanskrit work, centred on Radha and Krishna, has influenced music, dance and other arts across India.[^3] During the session, its poetry was discussed as material for singing and interpretation.

This connected with something I had written about in my earlier Bhubaneswar essay. Turning my teenage poem, “I Am Free to Dream,” into music had been one beginning of my renewed involvement with poetry. Now I was listening to practitioners discuss poetry within a musical tradition with a much longer history. The relationship between a written line, a performer and a listener was a thread I could follow.

The discussion also addressed Odissi music’s classical character: its notation, rhythmic cycles and compositional structures. The speakers argued for a fuller understanding and recognition of the tradition. Guru Gopal Chandra Panda’s work came up in the account of teaching, composition and preservation.

There were philosophical questions as well. The conversation touched on an ultimate reality beyond visible form, on surrender and on the longing expressed in devotional poetry. The notes I brought back include references to poet-saints, songs addressed to the divine and the idea of devotion without worldly demands.

After hearing Prateek explain stories of Shiva and Parvati, I was now listening to a discussion that approached devotion through poetry and music. Both parts of the evening brought people’s explanations to subjects I had been reading and writing about. The names and references in my notes give me somewhere to begin further reading.

In my earlier Bhubaneswar essay, I had written about conversations opening new paths. This evening added Prateek, Omm Prakash and his team to that account.

This is a fantastic effort by the Bhubaneswar Development Authority (BDA) and Team Ekamra Walks. Thank you for bringing the temple tour and the music together, and for giving us the chance to learn from the people sharing them. I came away having visited a temple that was new to me and experienced Odissi music in a setting I will remember.
