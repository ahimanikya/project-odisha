# Dhemsa and the movement of a shared rhythm

By Ahimanikya Satapathy

Dhemsa makes the group itself a moving form. Dancers link with their neighbours, bend their knees and carry a repeated rhythm through a line or semicircle. The interest lies in coordinated changes of direction and weight: each dancer helps sustain a pattern larger than an individual display.

The *Odisha Reference Annual* describes a Gadaba form in which linked dancers move forwards and backwards, divide into groups and come together again. Its account names dhol, tamak and mahuri among the accompanying instruments and places dancing at occasions including Dussehra, Pausa Purnima and Chaitra Parba. This is a description of a particular community’s practice, rather than a fixed choreography for every performance called Dhemsa. [^1]

The wider tradition crosses community boundaries in the former undivided Koraput region. Folklore scholar Anand Mahanand identifies Paraja, Gadaba, Didai and Bonda participation in a 2020 study. He describes the mahuri player establishing the tune, with other instruments joining, and records several named forms. Chait Parab and Pus Parab are among the occasions he discusses, along with celebrations connected to birth and marriage. [^2]

That range helps explain why Dhemsa should be understood through occasions as well as steps. A dance within a marriage celebration belongs to the relationships gathered there. A staged presentation has a different arrangement of participants and spectators, even when recognisable movements travel with it.

Mahanand also identifies a practical consequence of the move to commercial stages: recorded accompaniment can replace live instruments. His concern extends beyond the sound of a performance to the knowledge of playing and making those instruments. A dance tradition includes work that may remain outside the camera frame. [^3]

Odisha’s Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes Research and Training Institute records dance and music within its Gadaba community profile, alongside language, livelihoods and social organisation. That setting is useful: Dhemsa belongs to people with a wider contemporary life, not to an anonymous category of “tribal entertainment”. [^4]

The mahuri’s opening tune and the linked dancers’ changes of direction belong to one coordinated performance. Presenting the dance with its community and musicians named acknowledges the people who sustain both its movement and its sound.
