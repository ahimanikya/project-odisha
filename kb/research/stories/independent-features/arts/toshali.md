# Toshali’s temporary market for continuing craft

By Ahimanikya Satapathy

A craft fair compresses a dispersed working world into one place. At Toshali in Bhubaneswar, weavers and artisans bring objects made elsewhere into direct contact with buyers. The exchange can include an explanation of materials and technique as well as a price.

The nineteenth Toshali National Crafts Mela ran at Janata Maidan from 19 to 31 March 2025. SIDAC’s activity report records 736 participating artisans and weavers from 23 states and three Union Territories. The scale gave visitors access to many regional traditions within one temporary marketplace. [^1]

The fair’s working arrangements are less conspicuous than its displays. SIDAC lists free stalls for artisans and weavers and free accommodation for women exhibitors among the facilities. Those provisions address costs that can otherwise make travelling to sell difficult. They also help explain why an organised fair offers something different from simply inviting makers to a commercial exhibition. [^2]

Demonstrations add an educational role. SIDAC’s 2024–25 activity report records participation by trainees from its institutional craft disciplines. Showing a process allows an audience to see stages of work that the finished object conceals. For a trainee, explaining those stages is itself useful practice: the person at the stall must translate workshop knowledge into a conversation with a potential buyer. [^3]

The national range is visible in official exhibitor records too. The Development Commissioner for Handlooms’ 2026 allotment draw lists products including Chanderi sarees from Madhya Pradesh and pashmina and kani shawls from Jammu and Kashmir. These records identify selected participants; they are not a sales tally. They nevertheless make the fair’s interregional character concrete. [^4]

For the buyer, this concentration of work makes comparison possible. A question about fibre, weaving, dyeing or care can reveal differences that are difficult to judge from a display alone. The maker’s explanation gives a purchase context and can help a customer understand why apparently similar objects carry different prices.

Toshali ends on a particular date; the work represented there continues. Its economic value ultimately rests with exhibitors’ returns and subsequent orders, rather than attendance alone. The fair creates a brief opportunity for that longer relationship to begin, across the narrow distance between a maker and a person choosing what to take home.
