# Lower Indra and the two promises of a reservoir

By Ahimanikya Satapathy

Lower Indra was built to carry water beyond a river valley into farmland in Nuapada and Balangir. It also required households to leave land and homes. Irrigation and resettlement are therefore inseparable parts of the project’s history, even though they are measured in very different ways.

The dam stands on the Indra, a tributary of the Tel in the Mahanadi basin, downstream of its meeting with the Sundar. A Water Resources Department project note gives a designed cultivable command area of 29,900 hectares across 198 villages. Of that area, 13,191 hectares lie in Nuapada and 16,709 in Balangir. These are the intended reach of the system, not a measurement of how much land receives water in every season. [^1]

Distribution is a major part of that undertaking. The scheme combines canals with underground pipelines, using gravity to move water through the network. The note also provides for micro-irrigation on 2,990 hectares of upland intended for non-paddy crops. A reservoir’s agricultural value emerges through this chain of delivery: storage must become water at the appropriate field and time.

The project was inaugurated on 10 March 2021. Immediately before that event, displaced residents were pressing for compensation and basic services at relocation sites. A contemporary *OrissaPOST* report described a meeting with the district administration at which residents asked for their demands to be settled before inauguration. The collector urged them to allow more time for resolution. [^2]

Later administrative records show that rehabilitation remained part of the project’s work. The department’s 2024–25 activity report records housing-assistance guidelines for eligible Lower Indra beneficiaries and approval of ₹26.40 crore in ex-gratia assistance for 2,640 displaced families. An approval is a documented commitment; it does not by itself establish each household’s receipt or the quality of its resettlement. [^3]

These two promises require different kinds of follow-through. Irrigation depends on functioning distribution and dependable access. Resettlement depends on homes, services and the ability to rebuild everyday livelihoods. A completed dam can be photographed at once; those outcomes unfold over years.

The next field receiving water and the next displaced household receiving assistance belong to the same project history. Both determine what the completed reservoir means in people’s lives.
