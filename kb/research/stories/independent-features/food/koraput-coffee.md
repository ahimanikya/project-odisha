# The work behind a cup of Koraput coffee

By Ahimanikya Satapathy

Koraput coffee has become a regional name, but its commercial future depends on a sequence of less visible tasks: tending shade, picking ripe fruit, drying or pulping it, and finding a buyer who pays for the resulting quality. The crop’s story is as much about that organisation of work as the hills in which it grows.

Coffee research and extension in Koraput long predate the present brand. The Coffee Board records its local Technology Evaluation Centre as established in 1978. Its activities include demonstration and training, part of the support needed to establish a crop beyond India’s older commercial coffee regions. [^1]

The landscape imposes practical demands. The Board’s cultivation guidance identifies erosion during the monsoon and inadequate moisture afterwards as problems on sloping coffee land. It recommends measures such as contour planting, suitable shade and moisture conservation. These explain why planting more bushes alone cannot secure a harvest: the soil and the canopy require management too. [^2]

After picking, processing creates another division of labour. In the wet method, the fruit is pulped and the coffee prepared as parchment; in the dry method, the fruit is dried as cherry coffee. Care over ripe picking and drying affects the material that subsequently reaches the roaster. These are different production routes, not interchangeable labels for the same handling of the harvest. [^3]

A major marketing development came in 2019 with the launch of the Koraput Coffee brand by the Tribal Development Co-operative Corporation of Odisha, or TDCCOL. Reporting in March 2026, *The New Indian Express* described the corporation’s procurement, processing and café activities. Branch manager Rakesh Ranjan Biswal said approximately 360 tribal families benefited directly and that 71 women received payments into their own bank accounts. Farmer Jinu Majhi, from near Dumuriput, described coffee as bringing work back into villages. These are attributed accounts of the programme, rather than an independent district-wide income survey. [^4]

That reporting makes the significance of a local brand more concrete. Recognition can help bring a crop to market, but growers benefit through the terms of purchase, the timing of payment and the work retained locally. The name on a packet is the final public expression of those arrangements.

A cup carries the results of decisions made long before roasting: which shade to retain, when to pick and how to process the fruit. The regional brand brings that work to public attention; dependable purchasing and payment determine what it returns to growers.
