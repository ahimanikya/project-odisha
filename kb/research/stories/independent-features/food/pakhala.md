# Pakhala and the many ways to build a meal

By Ahimanikya Satapathy

Rice and water provide pakhala with a clear centre. Almost everything around that centre can vary: whether the rice is fresh or fermented, whether curd is added, which seasonings are used and what arrives beside the bowl. The meal’s identity is stable enough to recognise and flexible enough to belong to a particular household.

The names of its versions offer a useful introduction. Saja pakhala is prepared with fresh cooked rice and water; basi pakhala is the fermented version. Dahi pakhala includes curd, while tempered preparations add another layer of flavour. In a *Mint Lounge* account, food writer and photographer Alka Jena describes chunka pakhala seasoned with ingredients such as curry leaves, ginger, green chilli and cumin. [^1]

Jena’s recollection of a neighbour from Ganjam makes the variation more personal: fresh rice was accompanied by baby radish and fragrant citrus leaves. It is a small example of how a recognisable dish carries the preferences of a cook and a locality. There is no need for every pakhala meal to have the same sourness, seasoning or accompaniments.

What surrounds the rice matters just as much. Pritisha Das’s Odisha Tourism essay describes badi chura, potato preparations, fried brinjal and fish among the possible accompaniments. Crisp, fried or mashed dishes give the eater different textures to move between. A vegetarian arrangement and one including fish can both be complete meals built around the same bowl. [^2]

Chef Nisar Ahmed’s account adds the name torani for the rice water and discusses yoghurt, roasted cumin and ginger, along with greens, badi and coconut in the accompanying dishes. These are useful culinary details because they explain how flavour is distributed across the meal. The bowl does not have to carry everything by itself. [^3]

Pakhala also has a public calendar. OTDC documented a Pakhala Divas event at Panthanivas Bhubaneswar on 20 March 2025. Such events bring a familiar household food into restaurants and public celebration, often presenting several accompaniments together. [^4]

An elaborate platter is one expression of the dish, not a requirement for enjoying it. Pakhala’s adaptability is part of its cultural strength. A few choices—fresh or fermented, curd or tempering, greens or fish—can make a simple combination of rice and water taste unmistakably like someone’s own meal.
