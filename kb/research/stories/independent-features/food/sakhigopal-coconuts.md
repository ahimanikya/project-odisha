# Sakhigopal’s coconut trade after the storm

By Ahimanikya Satapathy

A coconut market depends on a landscape that takes years to grow. At Sakhigopal in Puri district, the link between palms, traders and roadside sellers became painfully visible after Cyclone Fani in 2019. Reopening a shop could not replace the mature trees on which its supply depended.

The town had an established marketing role long before the cyclone. A National Institute of Agricultural Marketing study records the regulated market’s establishment in 1961 and describes a 25-acre site beside the highway. During its survey, staff supervised open auctions on cemented platforms, while substantial trade also passed through private buyers outside the yard. The study identified missing amenities, including storage and rest facilities, showing that a market could be active while still leaving practical needs unmet. [^1]

Fani disrupted the growing end of that network. In May 2019, *The New Indian Express* reported appeals for assistance from coconut farmers in Sakhigopal and neighbouring areas after the storm devastated plantations. The losses involved an income-producing asset as well as a single harvest. [^2]

Reporting published by CarbonCopy in May 2026 found sellers back in business, but interviewed traders described a reduced flow of produce. Sakhigopal trader Subhas Kumar Bhanja said truck arrivals had fallen and that fewer traders remained in the business. Sellers also reported changes in fruit quality and problems with mite damage. These observations describe their experience; they do not isolate the effects of the cyclone from pests, replanting choices and other growing conditions. [^3]

The cultivation economy has another, slower component: replacement planting. A government agricultural-site proposal records coconut plantations and nursery activity at Sakhigopal. A nursery concerns the supply of future trees, while the market handles produce available now. Recovery has to bridge that difference in timescale. [^4]

Seen together, the market study and the later reporting describe a system with several points of vulnerability. Growers need productive palms; traders need reliable volumes; sellers need fruit that customers will buy. A disruption at one stage travels through the others.

A reopened stall is an early, visible sign of recovery. Rebuilding the supply behind it takes longer: productive palms, traders able to move the crop and sellers confident of what will arrive next.
