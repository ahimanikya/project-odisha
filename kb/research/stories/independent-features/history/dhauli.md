# What Ashoka’s inscriptions at Dhauli ask of government

By Ahimanikya Satapathy

Dhauli’s white peace pagoda is its most conspicuous monument, but the older voice on the hill is cut into rock. Ashoka’s inscriptions address the conduct of officials and the treatment of people under their authority. Their concern with justice gives the site a more practical political meaning than a general appeal to peace.

The inscriptions date to the third century BCE. They use the Prakrit language in Brahmi script and occupy a prepared surface on the rock. Above them stands a carved elephant, partially emerging from the stone. The Archaeological Survey of India identifies the special edicts as addressed to officers at Tosali, the regional administrative centre associated with the site. [^1]

Dhauli’s selection of texts is significant. The major rock edict describing the Kalinga war, numbered XIII, is absent, along with XI and XII. Two separate Kalinga edicts take their place. The account of conquest familiar from discussions of Ashoka should therefore not be mistaken for a passage a visitor will find on this particular rock. [^2]

One of the separate edicts presents the ruler’s subjects as his children and instructs judicial officers to deal impartially with them. It warns against anger, haste, harshness and indolence, and discusses imprisonment and suffering caused by the administration of justice. Inspections by senior officials are part of the proposed remedy. This is moral language directed at specific administrative behaviour: an official’s temperament and diligence have consequences for people in custody. [^3]

The contrast with the pagoda is a matter of historical distance. Bhubaneswar’s official visitor account dates the Shanti Stupa to 1972 and credits the Japan Buddha Sangha and Kalinga Nippon Buddha Sangha. The modern structure gives monumental form to Buddhist commemoration more than two millennia after the inscriptions were made. [^4]

Both monuments belong to the experience of Dhauli, but they ask for different kinds of attention. The pagoda can be apprehended as a form on the skyline. The rock edicts reward reading, translation and an interest in the machinery of rule. Their survival lets a visitor move from the familiar image of an emperor transformed by war to the instructions through which he wanted authority exercised.

At the foot of the larger story of Ashoka lies a strikingly ordinary demand: officials should avoid cruelty, take care over decisions and remain answerable for their work. Dhauli makes that demand durable in stone.
