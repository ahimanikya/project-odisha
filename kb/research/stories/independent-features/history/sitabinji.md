# A royal procession survives beneath Sitabinji’s rock

By Ahimanikya Satapathy

An elephant carrying a royal figure moves through the surviving painting at Sitabinji in Kendujhar. Horsemen, footmen and attendants complete the procession. The scene occupies the underside of a great rock shelter known as Ravana Chhaya: a courtly image preserved in a setting shaped by stone rather than palace walls.

Archaeologist Paul Yule’s account in *Early Historic Sites in Orissa* records a painted area about 3.70 metres wide and 1.90 metres high. He describes details of clothing, the elephant’s saddle blanket and the angular legs of the figures moving ahead of the ruler. Such observations bring the composition into focus as a carefully arranged procession, even where parts of the painting have deteriorated. [^1]

The ruler’s identification comes from more than the elephant. A painted caption was read as naming Maharaja Sri Disabhanja. Yule notes that this caption was no longer visible in the condition he documented. Earlier records thus preserve information that a present-day view may not reveal. Their value lies partly in allowing the damaged work to be studied across time.

The Archaeological Survey of India describes a palette of white, buff, brown, red and yellow ochre, and places the mural in the fifth–sixth centuries CE on palaeographic grounds. Older scholarship proposed other dates within the early historic period, so the painting is better understood through a scholarly date range than a falsely exact year. [^2], [^3]

The surrounding remains also deserve attention. ASI records shell-script inscriptions on nearby boulders and a five-faced Shiva linga made of chlorite. Sitabinji consequently brings several kinds of artistic work together: painting, writing cut into rock and sculpture. They are related by place, even where their interpretation requires separate study. [^4]

Conservation has addressed the shelter as well as the painted surface. Yule records a channel cut to divert water away from the mural. It is a small but telling intervention: the protection offered by a rock overhang is incomplete when water can still reach the image.

Sitabinji’s power lies in the survival of a human arrangement within that enormous natural enclosure. The procession remains legible enough to suggest rank, movement and display, while its losses make the labour of documentation tangible. Looking at the painting means encountering both the ancient artist’s composition and the accumulated efforts to keep it readable.
