# Cyclone Dana and the layers of coastal protection

By Ahimanikya Satapathy

Cyclone Dana crossed Odisha’s coast early on 25 October 2024, near Habalikhati in Bhitarkanika and Dhamara. The India Meteorological Department places the centre’s crossing between about 1.30 and 3.30 a.m. IST, with sustained winds of 100–110 kilometres an hour and gusts to 120. The full landfall process lasted longer than the passage of the centre. [^1]

Protection had begun before those hours. Kendrapara’s 2025 district disaster-management plan records evacuations starting on 23 October and reports that 121,116 people had been moved to 646 safe shelters by the following evening. Dry and cooked food were provided from 23 October. These details show what an evacuation count contains: places to receive people, movement before the storm and arrangements to sustain them after arrival. [^2]

Statewide figures grew as information was consolidated. The Union Home Ministry’s situation report dated 27 October recorded 810,896 evacuees and 6,210 relief centres in Odisha. Those figures belong to that report’s date; smaller totals in earlier bulletins reflected earlier stages of reporting. [^3]

Dana’s landfall near Bhitarkanika also renewed attention to mangroves. There is independent evidence that mangrove belts can reduce cyclone losses, but the best-known Odisha study examined a different event. Saudamini Das and Jeffrey Vincent analysed deaths in 409 villages after the 1999 super cyclone, accounting for factors including storm surge and distance from the coast. They found a protective association with mangrove width. They also concluded that early warning had saved more lives than mangroves. [^4]

That evidence supports a layered understanding of protection. Forests can contribute to reducing exposure, while forecasts, evacuation, shelters and emergency services address risks through different means. The 1999 findings do not quantify how much damage mangroves prevented during Dana; that would require an analysis of Dana’s own conditions and outcomes.

The practical lesson is visible in the evacuation record. A forecast became action through local transport, identified buildings, staff and food. Those arrangements did not depend on a single barrier holding back every hazard.

Dana is therefore most accurately remembered through the combination of its meteorology and the work undertaken before landfall. Conserving coastal ecosystems and maintaining preparedness are complementary tasks. Neither makes the other dispensable, and neither should disappear behind a claim that one intervention alone protected the coast.
