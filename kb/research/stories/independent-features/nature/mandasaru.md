# Mandasaru and the smaller lives of a forest gorge

By Ahimanikya Satapathy

Mandasaru’s conservation story reaches well below the treetops. The Odisha Biodiversity Board’s inventory of this Eastern Ghats gorge includes 130 kinds of fungi, 72 lichens and 70 bryophytes, alongside hundreds of flowering plants. These are organisms easily missed in a photograph of the valley, yet they help explain why the forest received special recognition. [^1]

The gorge lies in Kandhamal’s Raikia area, within a landscape the district administration describes as moist deciduous forest. Its slopes and wooded enclosure give Mandasaru a setting quite different from an open hilltop viewpoint. The forest itself, rather than a single summit or waterfall, is the destination. [^2]

The Board lists 772 flowering-plant species and 59 pteridophytes, the group that includes ferns. Its animal records include fishing cat, small-clawed otter and golden gecko. Such an inventory describes the variety documented in a habitat; it is not a promise that those animals will appear during a visit. It also broadens the usual wildlife story: a gorge can be important for reptiles, fungi and small plants as well as conspicuous mammals. [^3]

Mandasaru became Odisha’s first Biodiversity Heritage Site through a process involving the surrounding communities. The government notification records proposals from the biodiversity management committees of Gumamaha and Sugadabadi, forest officials and the Kandhamal organisation PAHAD. It also records Gram Sabha approval on 2 May 2018. Local institutions were therefore part of the designation’s documented beginning. [^4]

That designation recognises a living landscape. The notification’s criteria include distinctive ecosystems, threatened species and places with cultural as well as biological importance. Mandasaru’s protection is consequently concerned with the relationships between a forest and the people who know and use it, as well as the organisms counted by specialists.

For a visitor, this offers another way to look. A distant view gives the shape of the gorge; closer attention reveals the variety contained within the word “green”. Ferns, bark, leaf forms and the forest floor are subjects in their own right. Recognising that variety does not require collecting specimens or expecting an encounter with a rare animal. Mandasaru rewards an interest in the forest at several scales, from the enclosing hills to the small lives beneath them.
