# Patrapada’s garden of named plants

By Ahimanikya Satapathy

At the Medicinal Plants Knowledge Centre in Patrapada, the organisation of the garden is part of its purpose. Individual species occupy designated plots, turning a planted landscape into a collection that can be compared and studied. A visitor can connect a familiar name with a living plant, then notice the differences between it and its neighbours.

Odisha’s 2026 forestry report records 260 species in 260 plots and dates the centre’s development to 2008–09. It describes conservation outside natural habitats and public education as central functions. The garden is therefore both a place to grow plants and a means of making botanical knowledge accessible. [^1]

Early reporting shows how that idea was put into practice. *The New Indian Express* described a demonstration area named after Charaka, with signs identifying plants, and separate cultivation of tulsi, aloe vera, ashwagandha, gudmari, kalmegh, guluchi and cinnamon. It also recorded a bamboo collection. These names give the centre a more tangible identity than the general phrase “medicinal garden”: it brings commonly recognised plants into the same educational setting as less familiar ones. [^2]

The layout attends to the land beneath the collection too. The Forest Department’s 2020–21 report describes contour bunds around plots intended to retain runoff. Growing a collection requires management of soil and water, not simply the assembly of labels. [^3]

Research at the centre extends beyond plants. A study published in *Frontiers in Microbiology* describes maintaining Indian honeybee colonies there and sampling workers in April 2024 to investigate how supplementary sugar feeding affected their gut microbial communities. The experiment provides a specific example of the site’s use as a research setting, alongside its public teaching role. [^4]

The 2026 forestry report also records a recently established processing unit for non-timber forest produce from locally sourced plants and seeds. This introduces another connection: the living collection sits beside practical work with plant materials. [^5]

The word “medicinal” describes an important field of knowledge, but a garden label is not a treatment recommendation. Patrapada’s immediate value is more foundational. It lets plant identity, cultivation and recorded uses be examined together. Learning what a plant is—and how it grows—is a substantial beginning in a city where greenery can otherwise pass unnoticed as decoration.
