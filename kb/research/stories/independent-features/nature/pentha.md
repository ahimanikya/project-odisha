# Pentha and the unfinished work of coastal protection

By Ahimanikya Satapathy

At Pentha in Kendrapara, coastal erosion has made engineering part of the shore’s history. The village’s geotube defence was intended to resist the sea using large fabric containers filled with sand. Its subsequent difficulties show how much depends on maintenance, changing beach conditions and the design of the whole protective structure.

The idea was already attracting scrutiny in 2009. Reporting in *Down To Earth* described the proposed geotubes and competing assessments of their suitability. The central question was practical: how would this intervention behave on a coast exposed to waves and erosion? An apparently simple barrier concealed a more demanding problem of matching a structure to a moving shoreline. [^1]

The Comptroller and Auditor General later documented a substantial change in the project. The planned protective length was reduced from 675 metres to 505 metres after Cyclone Phailin in 2013 altered conditions at the beach. The audit discussed delays and incomplete work, placing the intervention within a record of changing designs and implementation difficulties. Those figures describe that phase of the project, rather than the present length or condition of the defence. [^2]

By January 2026, maintenance was again in the news. *The New Indian Express* reported damage to stone-filled gabion boxes after a fire. An assistant engineer said deliberate ignition was suspected; the report did not establish who was responsible. Officials also described wave damage to portions of the protective wall. The incident exposed two different vulnerabilities: deterioration under coastal forces and damage to the materials protecting the installation. [^3]

Pentha’s chronology resists a tidy verdict of engineering success or failure. The original proposal, the post-Phailin revision and the later damage concern different moments in the life of the same coastal defence. Together they show why protection cannot be judged only on the day a structure is completed.

For communities beside an eroding shore, the useful measure is whether protection continues to function through successive seasons. That requires inspection, repair and a realistic understanding of the beach around it. Pentha’s story is therefore as much about sustaining public infrastructure as installing it: the sea continues to act on a structure long after the construction contract has ended.
