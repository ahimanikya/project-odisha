# Learning and belonging in Niyamgiri

By Ahimanikya Satapathy

Mukteswar Wadaka taught children in Khambesi without charging for tuition during the pandemic lockdown. In Abhijit Mohanty’s January 2021 report, the Dongria Kondh student also spoke of wanting to enter the civil service and challenge assumptions about the work Adivasi people could do.[^1]

Mukteswar brought teaching close to home. For students who left Niyamgiri to study, education also raised questions about language, family and belonging.

## Ambition and example

Mohanty’s report followed students associated with the Kalinga Institute of Social Sciences in Bhubaneswar. Phulaki Wadaka, who had earned a master’s degree in psychology, described wanting to encourage friends to continue their studies. Ravindra Wadaka recalled taking up weightlifting after encouragement from coach Shubakanta Malya. At his first competition he felt nervous among so many people, despite months of training.[^1]

## Understanding the lesson

In September 2012, journalist Manipadma Jena reported from Sikhsya Niketan, a residential school for Dongria Kondh girls in the Chatikona area. Teacher Jayanti Sabar described students helping translate lessons into Kuvi for classmates who struggled with the language of instruction. Understanding a lesson depended partly on classmates who could explain it in familiar words.[^2]

The same report introduced Rina Wadaka of Khambesi, then a schoolgirl who wanted to teach. Jena traced her inspiration to Kasturi Melaka, who had completed school.[^2] Another student’s example helped Rina imagine teaching others; the report does not tell us what career she later pursued.

## Readers, primers and workbooks

One resource for Kuvi readers is *Pilanka Katha (Kuvi), Part I*, published by Odisha’s Academy of Tribal Languages and Culture. Its preparation credits name a Kuvi Resource Group: Trinath Wadaka, Tripati Hikaka and Surya Narayan Padhy, with Paramananda Patel as coordinator and Priyaranjan Baral as illustrator. One story page places Kuvi text above an illustration and an Odia passage below it, using Odia-based writing.[^3]

The reader’s edition date remains unconfirmed. A separate resource, NCERT’s *Kuwi Primer*, was launched in August 2023; a 2025 CIET newsletter records revised Kuwi and Desia primers submitted for printing.[^4]

By March 2026, the Ministry of Education also reported the development of *Shishu Vatika* workbooks in Odia script for languages including Kuvi.[^5] Readers, primers and workbooks offer different resources. These publication records do not tell us which edition reached a particular Dongria classroom or how a teacher used it.

## Staying connected to home

A July 2016 Kalpavriksh study recorded parents’ interest in literacy and education alongside concerns about sending children away. Participants wanted schools within Niyamgiri, teaching that related to local life, and teachers who understood the community. Their concerns included children’s separation from parents and from the knowledge associated with livelihoods in the hills.[^6]

The study drew on short visits and translated discussions, with limited participation by women and young people.[^6] The parents’ concerns and students’ aspirations raise connected questions: where to study, which language to learn through and how to remain close to family.

## Returning to education

SPREAD’s 2024–25 annual report describes Dasami Wadaka, an embroiderer from Khajur and member of its Rang Kerang collective, returning to senior-secondary study through the National Institute of Open Schooling. According to the account, she stayed at SPREAD’s Koraput office while attending her examinations. Her friend Jyoti Huika, whom the report identifies as Dongria Kondh, accompanied her and also decided to resume her education.[^7]

The account leaves the examination dates and results unstated. It describes a return to studying, not a completed qualification.

## Teaching close to home

Mukteswar’s village tuition, Sabar’s pupils explaining lessons and the two women returning to study show different forms of help between learners. Qualifications and careers matter in these accounts, but so do familiar words, someone willing to explain, and company when a person begins again.
