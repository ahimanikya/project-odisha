# Jayadeva and the places that remember him

*Odisha Roots · Story 4*

**A poem can find many homes. Finding its poet’s birthplace is a different journey.**

A manuscript has reached its last lines. After the poetry comes a small piece of writing about the text and its transmission: a colophon. Centuries later, those few lines will enter an argument about where a poet belonged.

In his Sahitya Akademi monograph, first published in 1973, Suniti Kumar Chatterji discusses a manuscript colophon dated **1746** that locates Jayadeva’s home at Kendubilva in Birbhum. It is an arresting piece of evidence, with a limitation built into its date. A record made centuries after a poet’s lifetime tells us about the history of a tradition. Its age alone cannot turn it into a contemporary witness to his birth.

That distance between a life and its remembrance runs through this story.

## More than two places

The familiar contest sets Odisha’s Kenduli against Bengal’s Jaydeb Kenduli. Chatterji’s survey makes room for a third claimant: **Mithila**. He weighs Odisha’s case, including Kedarnath Mahapatra’s arguments, while favouring the Bengal identification. His judgement is an argument to examine, rather than an institutional verdict that closes the enquiry.

The additional claimant changes the question. Before deciding which modern state wins, we need to ask what each piece of evidence actually locates: a birthplace, a court, a manuscript, a shrine or a later memory.

Those places need not all be the same.

## The poet among poets

Scholar Heidi Pauwels places the late-twelfth-century *Gita Govinda* within a literary world associated with Lakshmana Sena’s court. Her account points to fellow poets named in the work and to an early-thirteenth-century anthology containing relevant compositions.

These connections help reconstruct a literary milieu. Employment, patronage and intellectual company can take a writer far from home. A court association deserves its place in a biography; using it as a birthplace certificate requires another step of proof.

Odisha’s archaeological claim needs the same careful attention. An Odisha State Museum account reports tenth- to twelfth-century pottery and structural remains at Kenduli and presents them as confirmation of Jayadeva’s Odisha birthplace. The published description establishes the museum’s position. It does not show, in the material inspected for this story, how those remains identify this particular person. The excavation report and the reasoning connecting site to poet remain essential reading.

## A poem given a place in worship

Puri offers a different kind of connection.

Pauwels discusses a **1499 inscription** at the Jagannath temple prescribing the *Gita Govinda* in song and dance. Here the evidence concerns the poem’s ritual life: a dated record linking the work to an institution and a practice.

This deserves attention on its own terms. Odisha’s relationship with Jayadeva includes the history of what people did with his poetry—how they received it, preserved it and made it part of worship. That relationship does not become trivial because the poet’s birthplace remains disputed.

It also gives us a more useful itinerary for curiosity. Read a passage of the poem. Find a credited performance. Notice what a singer or dancer brings forward. Then return to the historical sources and ask which part of that experience they can explain.

## Let each connection keep its name

A responsible introduction can say where a manuscript was copied, which court a scholar associates with the poet, and where a temple record places his work. It can describe a birthplace claim as a claim, including the evidence its supporters offer.

Doing that leaves us with a fuller Jayadeva: a poet whose biography needs investigation and whose poetry has a history beyond the biography.

The next time a place introduces itself as his birthplace, stay curious. Ask what its earliest surviving evidence says—and what it leaves unsaid.
