# Mayurbhanj Chhau and the question of recognition

*Odisha Roots · Story 6*

**Follow an unmasked dance from its regional name to a parliamentary demand—and discover what recognition needs to provide.**

High kicks, jumps, turns and a torso trained to carry expression: these are among the movements described in Battery Dance’s programme for a Mayurbhanj Chhau presentation in New York on **15 August 2024**. The billing names Rajesh Sai Babu and The Chhau Samagam, with music credited to Saroj Mohanti.

Before the performance begins, the programme has already done something valuable. It has given the dance a regional name and named people who make it possible.

That sounds simple. A parliamentary record shows why it matters.

## Three names worth remembering

UNESCO’s account of Chhau identifies **Mayurbhanj, Purulia and Seraikella** as three regional styles. Purulia and Seraikella use masks; Mayurbhanj does not. Its description connects the dance with martial practices and movements drawn from everyday life, alongside epic and other subjects.

For a newcomer, the mask is an accessible first distinction. It should lead to closer attention: the body’s training, the repertoire, the teachers and the place in which a particular form developed.

Chhau entered UNESCO’s Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in **2010**, under India. The entry’s regional distinctions are part of that recognition. They should remain visible when the dance travels under a shorter label.

## A demand from Mayurbhanj

On **21 May 2012**, Mayurbhanj MP Laxman Tudu raised a specific objection in the Lok Sabha. He said a proposed national Chhau centre was to be located at Jamshedpur in Jharkhand, overlooking Mayurbhanj, and asked for it to be established in **Baripada**. He suggested strengthening the existing Chhau Nrutya Pratisthan.

His statement also asserted Mayurbhanj’s claim to Chhau’s origin. The parliamentary record proves that he made that argument; it does not independently settle the origin of every regional style.

The location demand is significant in its own right. A national centre is a place where teaching, institutional attention and opportunities may gather. The speech reveals a contest over where recognition would take material form.

That is more precise than a general claim that one state took another’s dance. It gives us a decision, an objection and a proposed alternative to investigate.

## What a later answer reveals

A Rajya Sabha answer dated **6 February 2025** describes a Chhau Kendra established by the Sangeet Natak Akademi at **Chandankiyari, Bokaro, in 2018**. Its annexure lists training associated with several regional traditions, including Mayurbhanj, and names Odisha gurus such as Laltu Mohanta and Sachindra Dhara.

This is a later institution in a different Jharkhand location. The records inspected do not establish its precise administrative relationship to the Jamshedpur proposal mentioned in 2012.

The answer contains another useful distinction. Asked about classical status, the ministry said the Akademi does not declare any dance classical and referred to the UNESCO inscription. Those forms of recognition should not be casually substituted for each other in a promotional caption.

## Bring the name back to the dancer

In Baripada, Project Chhauni describes a district-administration initiative launched in **2016**, with a proposal prepared by dancer Subhasree Mukherjee. Its stated work connects research, training, performance and artists’ livelihoods. These are the organisation’s aims; its website alone cannot establish how much income or opportunity each dancer has gained.

Together, these records suggest a practical way to tell Odisha’s story. Name Mayurbhanj. Credit the guru, performers, choreographer and musicians. When arranging an experience, ask who teaches it, who is paid and how the performers want their tradition introduced.

There is a richer encounter waiting beyond a photograph of a costume. A visitor can learn what a movement asks of the body and how a teacher passes it on. Any invitation to attend training or meet a troupe should begin with an arrangement made with the practitioners themselves.

Keep the three regional names in mind. Then choose one performance and learn the names behind it. Recognition becomes much more useful when it reaches a person.
