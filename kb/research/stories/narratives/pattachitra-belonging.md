# Pattachitra and the names a painting carries

*Odisha Roots · Story 3*

**Follow a painted line into Odisha’s living craft—and discover why a painting deserves a more careful introduction than a choice between two states.**

A hand holds a fine brush close to the painting. Around it, small figures stand inside arches; blue, red and dark patterned borders divide the surface into scenes. There is so much to look at that it is easy to miss the simplest question.

Who made this?

The photograph, taken by Sumit Surai in Raghurajpur in 2013, preserves the hand and its patient work. Its source does not supply the artist’s name. That missing name is a useful place to begin a story about belonging.

## Before the first painted line

In Odisha’s traditional Pattachitra process, the surface itself takes work. Layers of cotton cloth are joined with tamarind-seed glue, coated with chalk and polished before the picture develops. The painting begins before the first figure appears.

The Development Commissioner (Handicrafts) connects this tradition to the Chitrakara community and the ritual world of Jagannath. Its account describes mythological subjects, floral borders and carefully worked detail. These are features of a documented practice; the materials and methods of an individual painting still belong to its maker to explain.

Raghurajpur, near Puri, gives that practice a place on the map. Odisha Tourism identifies the village with Pattachitra and its artists. For a visitor, the name can become the beginning of an encounter: a person, a working surface, a story being painted.

“Odisha Pattachitra” carries those connections. An accurate caption should help them travel with the picture.

## A picture that has a voice

Across the state border, another tradition asks us to listen as well as look.

Banglanatak’s account of the Patuas of Naya, in West Bengal’s Paschim Medinipur district, describes stories painted on long scrolls and narrated through song. The sequence of images and the performer’s voice belong together. A still photograph can show the painted surface while leaving that performance unheard.

Bengal’s painting histories also need their own distinctions. The Victoria and Albert Museum describes Kalighat painting as a genre that developed in nineteenth-century Calcutta, where artists adapted to an urban market. Kalighat painting and the rural scroll-and-song tradition should not simply be treated as interchangeable labels.

There is room for curiosity here. How does a story change when it is arranged within a painted border, unfolded in sequence, or carried by a singing voice? A comparison becomes richer when we keep the particulars.

## An exhibition before the argument

One small archival discovery interrupts the familiar contest over who can claim the name.

For its Festival of Ramkatha, held in New Delhi from **12 to 20 March 2008**, the Indira Gandhi National Centre for the Arts listed paintings from several Indian traditions. Among them were Patachitra from **West Bengal and Orissa**, the state name used in the programme.

Both appeared in the same institutional account. This does not settle their earliest origins or every historical relationship between them. It does establish something useful: recognition of Bengal’s tradition did not begin with its later GI certificate.

The archive gives us a better starting point than a contest between two captions.

## What the certificates tell us

India’s GI register contains two distinct entries: **Orissa Pattachitra**, with a certificate dated **10 July 2008**, and **Bengal Patachitra**, with a certificate dated **28 March 2018**.

A geographical indication links a product’s identity and qualities or reputation to a place. Those certificate dates record registration milestones. They do not, by themselves, establish when people first painted in either tradition or decide which tradition came first.

Odisha’s earlier certificate matters as part of its protection and recognition. Odisha’s cultural history deserves to be understood through the artists, practices and historical evidence as well.

## Give the painting its address

Attribution has a practical test. If a work made by an Odisha artist is presented as a Bengal work, the correction should identify that painting, its maker and the evidence. The same care applies in the other direction. The existence of a neighbouring tradition does not establish that a particular artwork has been mislabelled.

For our Odisha story, the task is wonderfully specific: keep the artist’s name, the place, the medium and the subject together. Ask how the maker wants the work described. When sharing a photograph, include the photographer’s credit too. If something is unknown, leave space to learn it.

Return to the brush in the opening photograph. We can admire the fine line. We can name Raghurajpur. We can credit the photographer. We should also notice the artist whose name is still missing.

The next time a painting makes you stop, begin there. Ask who made it—and let that name travel home with you.
