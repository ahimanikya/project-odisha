# Sambalpur and the language of public life

*Odisha Roots · Story 7*

**A struggle over the language of official work helped turn a question of words into a question of borders.**

Think of a petition: a name, a grievance and a request that someone with authority must read. Now consider what happens when the language accepted by that authority changes.

This is an invitation to imagine an administrative problem, rather than a reconstructed encounter with a particular petitioner. In Sambalpur, the problem has a documented history.

Historian Pritipuspa Mishra describes **1895 proposals to replace Odia with Hindi** in Sambalpur’s administration under the colonial Central Provinces. Opposition connected local concerns with a wider Odia political movement.

A language can be spoken at home and still lose ground in public institutions. The question was what standing it would have when people addressed the state.

## The newspaper enters the story

Later accounts in *Odisha Review* give the movement people and places. They connect the newspaper **Sambalpur Hiteisini** and its editor **Nilamani Vidyaratna** with opposition to the language change. The paper belongs in the story because public argument needs somewhere to be made, repeated and carried to others.

The magazine’s April 2021 account also describes a **1901 delegation** to Chief Commissioner Andrew Fraser at Nagpur, naming Madan Mohan Mishra, Balabhadra Supkar, Mahant Bihari Das and Brajamohan Patnaik.

These names restore some of the human work behind the familiar phrase “language movement.” A grievance had to be formulated. Representatives had to carry it. A newspaper could make an administrative decision a matter for public discussion.

The surviving newspaper issues are the next important witnesses. No individual *Hiteisini* editorial was inspected for this draft, so its language and arguments are not recreated here. The account of its role comes from the named later histories.

## When language became a boundary question

Mishra’s larger argument helps explain the movement’s reach. Odia-speaking populations were spread across different colonial administrations. Campaigns for language recognition became connected with proposals to bring these areas together. The formation of the **Utkal Sammillani in 1903** gave the wider unification movement an organisation.

Sambalpur’s campaign therefore asked more than which language an office would use. It contributed to an argument about which administration the district should belong to.

Later historical accounts place the restoration of Odia in official use in **1903** and Sambalpur’s transfer to the **Orissa Division in 1905**. These were distinct developments: changing administrative language and changing administrative boundaries.

The sequence is clearer than every exact date. Sources encountered in this research differ in how they date the initial decision, implementation and restoration. The original notifications were not obtained, so this story does not pretend that every day and order number has been reconciled.

## Whose voice survives?

There is another question worth keeping beside a story of successful mobilisation. Petitions, newspapers and official correspondence make some participants easier to hear than others. An editor’s name may survive where an ordinary speaker’s experience does not.

Nor should a movement described through Odia language politics make every local voice seem identical. Mishra’s study examines the complications of territorial claims that also encompassed Adivasi populations. That warning against a simple, uniform map matters when reading the history of a linguistically varied region.

This investigation concerns colonial policy and the people who challenged it. It supplies no reason to assign inherited blame to today’s Hindi-speaking communities, or to make historical terminology decide present questions about Sambalpuri language and identity.

## Read the small print

For a visitor interested in Sambalpur, the story opens a route into its print culture and political memory. A newspaper title, a petition or an official notice can be as revealing as a monument—provided we know when it was made and whose voice it carries.

Begin with one document. Look for the language of the request, the official expected to answer it and the people who signed. Then ask whose names are absent.

Behind the changing map lies that very ordinary wish: to have words recognised when they reach the desk where a decision is made.
