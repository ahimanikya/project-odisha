# Reading Kalinga Beyond Odisha’s Borders

By Ahimanikya Satapathy

A stone inscription near Vizianagaram offers a useful starting point for reading Odisha’s history across a modern state boundary. In March 2024, a report described Deepak Kumar Nayak and Bikram Kumar Nayak’s exploration around Dharmapuri, assisted by local heritage enthusiasts D Santosh and Bijaya Kumar. The report pointed readers back to Satyanarayana Rajaguru’s *Inscriptions of Orissa*.[^nayak-dharmapuri2024]

The reference connects fieldwork with the published record. An inscription can be known in a book while its physical setting still needs attention. Returning to it brings the published record into conversation with a place, its surviving remains and the people who know it. Nayak’s documentation of Kalinga-related heritage in North Andhra belongs in this continuing work between the library and the field.

## The older inscription record

A closely matching earlier entry appears in Rajaguru’s 1961 *Inscriptions of Orissa*, volume III, part II, under “Cholagangesvara temple.” Modern photographs and site-location evidence still need comparison before identifying it securely with the stone in the 2024 report. Entry 232 identifies Telugu language and script. It locates the slab on a mound called Gudipadu-dibba at Dvarapureddipalem, in the then Vijayanagram taluk, and cites *South Indian Inscriptions*, volume X, No. 702.[^nayak-rajaguru1961]

Rajaguru’s date note gives Saka 1072, equivalent to 1150 CE. He connects Chodagangesvara with a temple at Dharmapura, treating construction by Chodaganga as probable. That proposed attribution should remain distinguishable from an explicit statement on the stone.[^nayak-rajaguru1961]

The earlier South-Indian Inscriptions edition, published in 1948, describes a land gift supporting a perpetual lamp at Cholagangisvara in Dharmavoramu. Entry 702 prints Saka 1071; Rajaguru’s later note corrects the year to Saka 1072. These are distinct editorial readings of the date, and the recorded donation does not establish when the temple was built.[^nayak-sii1948][^nayak-rajaguru1961]

The 2024 photograph’s single oblique view cannot establish a line-by-line match with the older edition’s two-face transcription. Clear views of both faces and a documented location are still needed to connect the modern object securely with that record.[^nayak-report2024-print][^nayak-sii1948]

The older place description gives fieldworkers a specific point of comparison. A return to a recorded site can establish what remains there, how its surroundings have changed and what needs closer examination.

## A survey becomes a book

Nayak’s wider undertaking took published form in *Relics of Kalinga in South India, Part-I*. INTACH’s chapter newsletter records its launch in Cuttack on 27 April 2025, identifies him as its author, and credits Sanjib Chandra Hota and Asoka Kumar Rath as editors. B.K. Publications published the volume. Its geographical focus is five North Andhra districts: Srikakulam, Vizianagaram, Parvatipuram Manyam, Visakhapatnam and Anakapalle.[^nayak-intach2025]

The launch report names Nayak as project coordinator and Bishnu Mohan Adhikari as epigrapher. It credits surveyors Bikram Kumar Nayak, Suman Prakash Swain, Bikash Prasad Das and Chiranjib Rout, together with contributor Satya Chandra Saikumar Kethineedi.[^nayak-launch2025]

Those credits help explain the nature of the undertaking. Finding a place, recording an object, interpreting writing and preparing a publication are distinct contributions. Keeping them visible gives readers a clearer understanding of how the resulting knowledge was assembled.

The survey’s scope also suggests a way to read regional history. A present-day state border is useful for administration; it cannot, by itself, decide which records belong in a historical inquiry. Each proposed connection needs its own evidence. A temple name, a donor, a language or a royal title can open a question without establishing a single, unchanging boundary for Kalinga.

## Two places in the survey

The book’s Pidi Mandasa entry considers Baidyanath Temple through an account of local construction tradition and a possible thirteenth-century date. The tradition is part of the place’s history of remembrance; it does not, on its own, establish a construction chronology.[^nayak-book-preview]

At Siddheswar Temple in Ronanki, Nayak distinguishes the rebuilt temple from earlier remains. He reports, from conversations with local people, that an inscribed slab lies beneath the renovated floor. That account raises a question about access to the record; it does not establish that the slab has been permanently lost.[^nayak-book-preview]

## What an inscription can tell us

Epigraphy is the study of inscriptions: writing preserved on materials such as stone and metal. The Archaeological Survey of India explains that temple inscriptions can record donors, rulers, builders and circumstances of construction. Such records can bring the history of religious institutions, administration and everyday economic arrangements into view alongside dynastic history.[^nayak-asi-epigraphy]

Reading them requires more than recognising a few names. A record needs a location and a legible copy; a proposed reading needs explanation and comparison. ASI’s account of estampages describes paper impressions that allow inscriptions to be studied away from the original object.[^nayak-asi-estampages] Photographs, copies and published editions make it possible for another reader to examine an argument.

A related example from Nayak’s work in Odisha shows why the wording of a claim matters. An October 2024 report about Chitreswari credits a joint investigation by Satyajit Swain, Bikram Kumar Nayak, Mihir Bahinipati, Bishnu Mohan Adhikari and Nayak. It attributes to Adhikari a reading naming the sculptor Sreepati. The proposed early nineteenth-century date rests on the script: the report says the inscription gives no construction date.[^nayak-chitreswari2024]

This distinction helps a reader understand what is being proposed. A name read in an inscription and a date inferred from letter forms are different kinds of conclusion. Both can be valuable when their basis is clear.

## Keeping the record connected to the place

The Dharmapuri report’s local collaborators deserve a place in this history too. D Santosh and Bijaya Kumar appear alongside the visiting researchers, reminding us that a field account is shaped by more people than those named on a book cover.[^nayak-dharmapuri2024]

Documentation can give a place a more durable presence in public knowledge. It can supply a reference for a future visit, a comparison for a later photograph or a question for an epigrapher. Conservation asks for further work: attention and documentation alone do not establish that a structure has been repaired or an inscription protected.

Nayak’s North Andhra project brings publications, places and collaborators into a continuing conversation. The connections become stronger when a later reader can follow the evidence back to a particular record and a particular place.
