# What remains after growing the rice?

**A harvest has a value. The work behind it has a cost.**

A field of rice can invite admiration long before anyone asks about its accounts. Yet a bigger harvest alone cannot tell us what a grower keeps. Follow one historical regional study through its definitions, and the difference becomes visible.

## One hectare, three figures

P. P. Sahoo, K. K. Sarangi and S. N. Mishra’s study, published in **March 2023**, reports this paddy case for Odisha’s **North Eastern Ghats agro-climatic zone**. Its paddy survey and valuation year remain unresolved; these are **not current statewide averages**.

| Reported measure | Rupees per hectare |
| --- | ---: |
| Gross value, including the by-product | ₹79,050.00 |
| C2 cost under the study’s definitions | ₹64,908.43 |
| Reported net income above C2 | ₹14,141.58 |

The gross value combines ₹72,750 for the main product with ₹6,300 for the by-product. Calling the whole sum a grain-sale payment would change what it measures.

## The work that has no separate wage slip

The study’s **C2** accounts for valued family labour, land and owned-capital costs as well as other cultivation costs. “Valued” matters: work can enter an economic account even when it does not produce a separate wage payment. The table assigns family labour ₹9,000 per hectare in this case.

A smaller cost boundary gives a larger remainder. The paper also reports ₹38,036.90 per hectare as *farm business income* after **A2**. That is a different measure from the ₹14,141.58 after C2. A2 itself includes items such as depreciation and owned inputs; it should not be retold as a cash-only budget.

The two remainders are not competing answers to one question. Read the cost definition before deciding what an income figure tells you.

## Carry a better question into the next story

For future reporting, begin with one crop and an agreed period. Ask what was received, which costs were paid, how family work and land were valued, and what records support the account. These are proposed questions, not interviews Project Odisha has conducted. A household’s total income would need a wider account than this crop calculation.

The [procurement-payment story](rice-procurement-payments.md) asks a related question at a different scale. Departmental payments and a regional per-hectare cost study cannot be combined into a present-day farm-profit estimate.

At the next meal, the rice on the plate can prompt more than a question about taste. It can also prompt interest in the work that made the harvest possible—and in how carefully we listen when someone explains what remained.
