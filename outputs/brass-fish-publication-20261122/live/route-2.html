Silver, Metal, Clay, Stone and Appliqué

Materials, tools and the people who give them form

By Ahimanikya Satapathy

All chaptersFootnotes & Sources

The open spaces in Cuttack filigree are part of its design. Silver wire encloses small curls and loops, allowing light to pass through an object that is still strong enough to hold its form. Looking closely changes the question from what the object represents to how its parts were made and joined.

This chapter follows that question through silver, cast and hammered metal, clay, stone and the stitched surfaces of Pipili. Each material asks something different of the maker: control of a fine wire, a mould prepared for molten metal, a hollow body shaped in clay, a measured cut or a carefully placed seam. Their finished forms belong to homes, personal adornment, worship and public display.

In the NID documentation of tarakasi, thicker wire provides a frame and finer wire fills it. Tweezers help roll, fold and twist the infill; soldering joins the parts, followed by filing and polishing. The account names Ramesh Behera as a senior Cuttack craftsman. Jewellery, ritual objects and decorative pieces share this patient construction from small components.1

Metal takes more than one path

Dhokra casting begins before there is a metal object to see. The Dhenkanal process recorded by NID starts with a clay core. Wax threads cover it to establish the design, and further clay layers enclose the wax. Openings provide a route for the metal and a way for air to escape. Heating removes the wax; molten metal occupies the space it has left. Once the casting has cooled, the mould is removed and the surface cleaned and finished.2

A raised line on the finished object therefore preserves a decision first made in a softer material. The pattern has passed through wax and clay before becoming metal. The documentation identifies Sansari Gadatia as its senior craftsman contact in Dhenkanal, giving this description of technique a named practitioner.3

Brass and bell-metal vessels involve another organisation of work. Impart’s account of Kansari ware describes a collaborative sequence: several artisans hammer an ingot while another turns it, with repeated heating keeping the metal workable. The coordinated blows spread the material and help maintain an even thickness. A vessel takes shape through timing and shared attention as well as individual skill.4

These processes deserve separate names. A cast figure, a hammered vessel and a filigree ornament may all appear in a display of metal craft, but their surfaces record different operations. Following those operations restores some of the workshop to the finished object. The preparation of a mould, the drawing of wire and the turning of heated metal are all labour, even when a polished surface makes that labour difficult to see.

Clay learns a shape

A small fish toy offers a precise lesson in clay work. NID’s documentation describes a hollow body formed on the potter’s wheel and cut free with wire. After partial drying, fins, tail, eyes and mouth are added. A wooden modelling tool helps with details; a pen cap makes impressions for the gills. Familiar tools become instruments for giving the animal character.5

The same documentation introduces Jayadev Rana of Barpali, Bargarh, as a maker of terracotta jewellery and crafts. Its title uses Sambalpur; the biography gives the more specific locality. Naming both preserves the record without losing the maker’s place.6

The museum photograph below shows another expressive use of clay: a figure with an open mouth, prominent eyes and repeated raised details. Its maker is not identified in the image record. It is reproduced as a separate example, not attributed to Rana or presented as the fish toy described above.

Working into stone

Carving requires a different kind of foresight. The NID account of stone work in Bhubaneswar begins with selecting and checking the material, including looking for cracks. Cutting establishes the broad form; hammer and chisel remove smaller pieces. Finer tools develop the details, and polishing finishes the surface. The character of the stone affects the treatment it can bear.7

The order matters. A face, hand or ornament must be anticipated while it is still surrounded by stone. As material comes away, the maker moves from mass towards detail. A small projection needs enough substance to survive the next cut. The finished figure can conceal the sequence of decisions that made it possible.

Sudarshan Sahoo’s work also draws attention to the institutions through which craft is shared. The Ministry of Tourism’s account credits him with establishing the Sudarshan Craft Museum in Puri in 1977. Its collection brings stone sculpture into company with other media, including wood carving, painting and filigree.8 A named artist and a museum offer a different point of entry from an anonymous category such as “stone craft”.

The tools in these accounts also complicate a simple division between hand and machine. NID records a cutting machine alongside chiselling in stone work, just as its filigree account records wire-drawing equipment. Skill includes choosing how a tool will be used and judging what remains to be done. The presence of a machine does not tell us who conceived the form or controlled its finer details.17

Pipili joins colour to ceremony

In appliqué, a cut piece of cloth becomes part of a larger surface. NID’s Pipili account describes preparing a background, cutting motifs and joining them through stitching. For lampshades, templates help produce repeated shapes; cut pieces can be bundled for later use. The account includes machine stitching and wooden or iron rings that give the shade its form. Pattern depends on preparation as much as the visible seam.9

The Puri district administration connects Pipili’s cloth work with the decoration of Ratha Yatra chariots. It also lists umbrellas, bags, purses and wall hangings among the town’s products. Ceremonial use and domestic use sit within the same craft landscape, though each object has its own scale and purpose.10

The display photographed here makes repetition visible: circular centres, surrounding motifs and gathered borders return in different arrangements. A finished object rewards attention to these choices. Across the materials in this chapter, close looking leads back to makers, tools and sequences of work. Keeping those details in the record allows the skill to remain visible after the object has left the workshop.
