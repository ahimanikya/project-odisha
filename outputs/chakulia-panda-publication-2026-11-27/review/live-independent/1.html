Sounds of Odisha · Pala

Poetry with
people around it.

By Ahimanikya Satapathy · Last reviewed 19 October 2026

A Pala singer brings a verse to a gathering. The story grows through explanation, music, gesture and the voice that answers back.

In the Nalconagar photograph, people sit on mats around a standing performer. His raised hand and the drum beside the listeners draw attention to a small performance space beneath a bright canopy. Poetry here has people around it: a voice to carry the story, other performers to answer, and listeners to follow its turns.

Pala brings literary and devotional storytelling into performance. To follow it, listen beyond the main narrative. A comparison can explain a difficult verse; a gesture can change the meaning of an object; an answering voice can turn an elevated thought into a sharp joke.54

Odisha’s literary culture, heard aloud

Pala connects Odisha’s literary culture with the work of listening together. A poem becomes a voice, a musical phrase, an explanation and an answer. A 2021 profile of Gangadhar Puhan describes his chhanda, champu, chaupadi and Odissi singing, and his interest in Bhanja literature and Udayanath Rathsharma’s poems and plays. These are one artist’s choices, showing how older literary forms can enter a present gathering.9

A performance can also send a listener back to a book. When a singer introduces a quotation, listen for where it comes from and why it belongs at that moment. Recognising the work is one pleasure; hearing an unfamiliar text explained is another. Pala’s devotional histories and its public humour open other paths into Odisha’s cultural life.

The voice, the drum and the answer

Kalahandi district’s description of Palla names the Gayak, Bayak and Palias, alongside Mrudanga, Gini and Chamar. The Gayak leads the telling; the surrounding voices and percussion make it an ensemble performance. The district account places Palla around Bhawanipatna, Dharamgarh and M. Rampur and describes lyrical narration of Puranic and historical events. This is a local description, not a fixed cast list for all Odisha.34

The lead singer does not carry every meaning alone. In Sitakant Mahapatra’s account, the Palia can answer an explanation with a pun or a contrary interpretation. The response may help an audience follow the story, but it can also interrupt its dignity. A serious claim has to survive the question or joke that follows it.4

Umesh Patra’s 2017 study distinguishes seated Baithaki Pala, standing Thia Pala and competitive Badi Pala. Badi brings two teams into a contest of wit. Knowing the form changes how one listens: a household recitation and an exchange between competing singers have different expectations.5

How a story acquires a body

Umesh Patra describes an invocation before the main narrative, followed by verse explanation, song, movement and changes of role. In his Harischandra example, a chamara, or fly-whisk, stands for the king’s surrendered crown; in other passages the same object can become a bow or trident. Its material has not changed. The performer’s action asks the audience to understand it differently.5

Watch for that change rather than looking only for elaborate scenery. What is the object at this moment? Which gesture made its meaning clear? Such questions help an unfamiliar listener follow the drama even before every literary reference is understood.

When a familiar poem turns against power

Sitakant Mahapatra’s essay, published in 1982, describes a pointed use of Gopabandhu Das’s poetry. The original thought imagines the speaker sacrificing his own body for the country. In the Palia’s parody, a selfish leader expects the people to sacrifice themselves for him.4

The reversal is small enough to recognise and large enough to change the argument. The earlier ideal remains in the listener’s mind, making the leader’s demand look worse. Mahapatra’s example shows how literary knowledge can become public criticism through an answering voice. Listen for what the joke changes: the words, the speaker or the person expected to bear the cost.

Devotional history has more than one thread

Pala’s devotional history has several accounts. In his 2021 chapter, Patra examines the Satyapir narratives associated with Kabi Karna’s Shola Pala and the relationship between Satyanarayana and the Muslim pir. He reads the chapter’s cited references to Ram and Rahim, the Quran and Purana as a shared devotional language.6

That is a literary interpretation of religious connection, not an established founding year or evidence that every community followed the same practice. Mahapatra distinguishes household ritual recitation from performances that develop a story through literary comparisons. Devotion, narrative and literary display can meet without being identical; the transmission of a text and the development of stage practice leave different historical questions.4

Six artists and their work

Jagannath Behera drew on literary classics in Odia, Bengali, Hindi, Sanskrit and English. His 2009 Sangeet Natak Akademi citation names Harekrushna Nath, Bhagaban Singh and Harekrushna Swain as his teachers. His career brings the work behind a quotation into view: reading widely, learning from other performers and finding a place for another writer’s words in a telling.1

Laxmidhar Rout’s 2016 Sangeet Natak Akademi citation records training under Damodar Sarangi and Baishnaba Charan Pradhan, teaching younger students, and work through Pala Pratishthan, Shree Jagannath Pala Research Foundation and a Nilachala Pala parishad in Kendrapara. It also names his book Udaharana Sataka. Performance, teaching and writing each gave him a way to pass the art on.8

Raghunath Nathsharma’s singing joined Odissi, chhanda and champu with Sanskrit learning. A September 2020 profile reports that, after he retired from the stage, other singers still came to his home to learn. A performing career could continue through what he passed on.15

Santilata Sahu presented an episode about Hanuman’s meditation in Dwapara Yuga at the 19–21 July 2012 Pala and Daskathia Utsav at Rabindra Mandap, Bhubaneswar. The Culture Department roster identifies her as a Pala performer; the festival report gives her choice of story. Her name belongs beside that repertoire.107

Manorama Panda presented Naga Jyoti at the same festival. The report names Jagannath Barik as her guru. Keeping her name, teacher and work together makes women’s contribution visible through a specific performance credit, while leaving room for a fuller account of her career.7

Kumudini Patel presented Sasirekha Parinaya. Alongside Sahu and Panda, she appears in the government’s Pala roster and the festival account with her own repertoire. Remembering these women as individual artists gives future research a starting point for fuller biographies, teaching histories and recordings.107

Literature in public life

The Chief Minister’s office report of 28 December 2025 credits singers and Bayaks with bringing Odia poetry to the public at the 48th state-level Pala Sammilan in Kendujhar. The gathering places Pala in a continuing public conversation about literature. It is evidence of that event and its stated purpose, rather than a measure of artist earnings.2

Why Pala deserves a future

The case for preserving Pala begins with the skills it joins together: explaining a poem, shaping its music, answering a fellow performer and holding a gathering’s attention. Books and recordings matter, but keeping those abilities in practice needs people learning from one another, with time and opportunities to perform.

Losing that practice would narrow the ways Odisha can encounter its own literature. It would weaken a form in which devotion can share space with wit, an unfamiliar text can be explained aloud, and a Palia can question the dignity of the main speaker. Those possibilities deserve a place in the lives of future performers and listeners.

The conditions of the work matter. In an interview dated 14 June 2014, Prasant Nath Sharma told Patra that shorter programmes were squeezing out music, comedy and other material. A 2021 report records artists seeking help during pandemic disruption. Neither measures today’s statewide condition, but both make performance time and paid work part of the preservation question.513

Support can take practical forms: paid apprenticeships, recurring performance opportunities, and recordings that name the ensemble and its repertoire. Teachers and performers should help decide what is documented and how it is shared. UNESCO’s 2003 safeguarding framework offers a useful principle here: transmission, community participation and room for a tradition to change. Applying that principle does not imply a UNESCO listing for Pala.14

Before you go

Look for a dated programme that explicitly names Pala or Palla, and confirm it with the organiser before travelling. No forthcoming public performance was confirmed in the sources checked for this guide on 19 October 2026. The historical festivals described above and a troupe’s registration are enquiry leads, not bookings.

The Odisha Culture Department’s CTMS register lets you search for troupes by district and lists their art categories. Its Music/Pala entries include troupes in Cuttack, Bhadrak and Kendujhar. Check the category as well as the name, then ask your host or guide to help establish whether there is a public programme or an invited visit. A household or devotional gathering requires the host’s invitation.12

Ask about the form, starting time, duration and language. Will it be seated recitation, standing Pala or a Badi contest between teams? Request the episode’s title and a brief introduction; if you need help with Odia, arrange a companion who can explain the premise and discuss it afterwards. Confirm admission or fees, seating, step-free access, toilets and the return journey. English interpretation and particular facilities should not be assumed.

What to listen for

Follow a complete exchange. Notice the main singer’s explanation, the drum and the answering voices, then watch for a change of character or a prop taking on a new meaning. The Gopabandhu parody offers one question to carry with you: what has the reply changed? Ask about unfamiliar quotations during a suitable pause or afterwards.

To listen before travelling, UKS TV’s catalogue credits Sachidananda Dash and team with Guru Dakhina, and Nrusingha Hota and team with Rochak Ramayan. The links below offer a way to become familiar with the pace of a recorded performance. The publisher’s upload does not grant permission to reuse its footage.11

Supporting the artists

Ask before photographing or recording, and ask separately about posting an extract. Respect a refusal, keep your phone silent and follow the host’s arrangements for seating, dress and devotional observances. Credit the whole ensemble and the work when the names are available.

If commissioning a presentation or learning session, agree with the troupe on payment, preparation or teaching time, travel, duration and recording permissions. Let any shorter visitor introduction be a format agreed with the artists. Attend another programme, follow an artist-authorised recording or support an agreed teaching session: practical interest gives the work another occasion to continue.
