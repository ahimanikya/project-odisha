# The Question That Brought Me Here

By Ahimanikya Satapathy

A line of poetry can travel far from the page.

I began noticing it in the folds of a sari, in stone shaped by patient hands, in a song that outlived its singer. Sometimes it returned through a familiar *Dhaga*—a few words spoken in an everyday conversation, carrying more experience than their length could hold.

Looking at Odisha, I found myself following these journeys. How did an idea become something we wore, something we remembered, something we passed on? And what happened to the person whose imagination gave it life?

These questions felt close to me. I am an artist and a poet at heart, though much of my creative life has unfolded before a code editor. Building software, I have known the pleasure of giving an idea a form another person could use. A canvas offers me that same invitation. The materials change; my desire to make something that reaches another human being remains.

People who have worked closely with me know this about me. I see art in a product’s form, in its behaviour, and in the care with which it meets a human need. Technology, art and poetry have always spoken to one another in my mind. Moving between them has helped me approach questions differently.

For years, I had been absorbed in building my own world. Now my attention was turning towards the language and culture that had helped shape it.

The more I looked, the more I wanted to know about the people behind what I loved: the writer searching for a reader, the artisan seeking a fair return, the storyteller hoping someone would listen.

Admiration brought me closer to their work. A question about livelihood kept me there.

**How could I help put money in writers’ pockets?**

During the pandemic, I had helped farmers sell fruit in large quantities—around 500 tonnes each season—with the aim of getting as much of the money as possible into their hands. When I turned towards Odia literature, that experience came with me.

Farming and writing have different needs. Yet I recognised a question about the distance between making something and earning from it. Who helps a work reach the people who might value it? How much of that value returns to its maker?

I wanted writers to have a fair chance to keep creating. I worried about the person who spent years writing a book, paid to have it printed, and then had to carry the burden of finding readers alone.

Helping with publication could be useful. But I began to wonder whether I was thinking too narrowly about where a writer’s work could live.

Art and poetry should have a place in ordinary life. They should be available to the person who enjoys a song without studying its metre, the child who remembers a character, and the audience drawn into a performance. Scholarship helps us understand and preserve these works. Their life also depends on people finding pleasure, recognition and companionship in them.

Odisha gave me many paths to explore. Pala, nataka and dance brought me towards the meeting of language, music, movement and an audience. Researching my own name led me towards Ahimanikya and Sashisena, and towards the relationship between a story, a place and its remembered life. Textiles made me think about how imagination enters things we wear and use.

I began to see poetry as something that could be encountered before it was identified as poetry.

A child might first love a melody. Someone else might remember a line because a performer gave it an unforgettable voice. A story could become part of a family’s conversation long before anyone went looking for its written source.

That possibility mattered to me. Selling a poetry collection could be one way for a writer to earn. There could also be songs, performances, adaptations, illustrated stories and other forms through which the work reached people.

The question of who sustains art is an old one. Royal patronage belongs to that history. In our own time, who is willing to commission a work, pay for its use, support its development or buy a ticket?

There may be many answers: audiences, publishers, producers, institutions, businesses and public support. I wanted to understand how those relationships could give creative people both dignity and room to work. Appreciation needed a practical expression.

Meanwhile, a poem from my own life was finding an unexpected form.

During a weekend on a trip to the United States, I began turning *“I Am Free to Dream,”* which I had written as a teenager, into music. Words from my younger self returned through a voice I had not imagined for them.

That experiment grew into Kabita Without Borders and led me to build Poet Studio, an application for poets to publish and manage their work. Sri Pradeep Biswal and Sri Paresh Patnaik, whom I had known since my teenage years, encouraged my renewed involvement in Odia literature. I also registered the Pravakar Satapathy Foundation with the intention of helping writers publish through grants.

These were beginnings. I was learning through them, and each brought me back to the question of an audience.

For children, that question became especially immediate. At a birthday party, the need for relatable Odia cartoon characters gave it a concrete shape.

I wanted children to encounter their language through something they were excited to return to. Could affection for a character lead towards a story, and then towards a book? Could we offer that experience with enough imagination and care that choosing it felt natural?

It was easy to discuss what children should read or what a community should preserve. I wanted to help make things they would enjoy having in their lives.

This also shaped an essay I wrote, *“Rise, Children of Utkal.”* I had heard the familiar expression “ଆମର ଏଠି କିଛି ହେବନି”—nothing will happen here. I understood that disappointment could sit behind it. Still, I wanted to leave the conversation open.[^utkal]

What could we begin with what we already knew? Who might work with us? Could we buy a book, support a workshop, make a useful introduction, or give a small idea enough time to discover whether it helped?

I was asking those questions of myself.

Cinema began to gather many of these thoughts together. Here was a form in which writing, music, performance, visual imagination and technology could meet. It could carry an Odia story into a shared experience, and perhaps towards people who would never have encountered it on a bookshelf.

During a week in Bhubaneswar, I met *DAMaN* directors Vishal Mourya and Debi Prasad Lenka, and producer Deependra Samal. We met twice. Their energy and willingness to build something stayed with me. Conversations with writers, filmmakers and heritage storyteller Shakti Badjena gave me further reasons to listen and explore.[^week]

I began reading more closely about the work behind the films.

A screen gathers attention around actors and stories, but many people make that encounter possible. Writers, producers, technicians, musicians, performers, distributors and exhibitors contribute different kinds of skill and take different kinds of risk. If I wanted to understand how cinema could support literature, I also needed to understand the livelihoods and relationships that sustained cinema itself.

Curiosity became research. That research is now taking shape as a book and a series of articles.

I did not set out to write a history of Odia cinema. I turned to its past to understand the people, choices and creative possibilities that might help us move forward. The history in these pages serves that exploration; it does not attempt to record every film, every contributor or every period.

The question guiding me is what we can build from here: an Odia-language cinema nourished by the lives, landscapes and cultures of Odisha, reaching wider audiences and sustaining the people whose imagination and labour bring it to life.

I came to cinema’s history looking for people and possibilities. I wanted to know what someone had imagined, how others had helped bring it into being, and what allowed it to reach an audience. Those experiences could inform what we tried next.

Cinema would not solve every difficulty facing literature. A film would not automatically create readers or guarantee a writer a livelihood. Yet a thoughtful adaptation could offer a work another life. A good film could make someone curious about its source. A fair agreement could provide income and recognition to the person who wrote it.

The successes we already have make these possibilities worth examining. People have created films that moved audiences, traveled beyond Odisha and earned recognition. What helped them do it? What could others learn? How could one success make the next beginning easier?

Those are the questions I want to carry through these pages.

I want to explore how Odia cinema can develop its quality, reach wider audiences, and sustain the people who make it. I want to understand how literature and cinema might nourish each other, and how our language can hold both the worlds we remember and the lives we are living now.

There is much I am still learning. These stories leave room for that learning—and for people whose experience may deepen or challenge what I have understood.

Through it all, a line from my teenage poem has returned:

*“I am free to dream and allowed to get lost.”*

I hear it differently now. The journeys through poetry, software, farming, literature and cinema are beginning to meet. Each has taught me to look for the person on the other side of what we create.

I began by asking how to put money in a writer’s pocket. I am still following that question.

Somewhere ahead, a child may meet a character they love. A reader may discover a book through a film. A writer may receive an opportunity that makes the next work possible.

I want to understand what we can build to help those encounters happen.
