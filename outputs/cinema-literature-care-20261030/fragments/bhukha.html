A tradition can be admired long after the people who practise it have begun to struggle. The drum remains a symbol; the drummer still needs to earn a living.

In ଭୂଖା (Bhukha), Sabyasachi Mohapatra’s 1989 Sambalpuri film, folk performers live within a changing society. Film critic Ashok Palit discusses it as an encounter between inherited ways of life and modernity.1

Behind the sound, a livelihood

The film grew from a play by Manglu Charan Biswal. A later report about Mohapatra’s work describes its concern with bajnia folk drummers whose livelihoods were threatened by changing musical tastes.2

For a working musician, cultural change can arrive as the loss of the next engagement. The film’s subject brings an economic question into the celebration of inherited art: what allows a performer to continue?

Ramesh Mahanand composed the film’s music. Himanshu Shekhar Guru’s 2015 account in OrissaPOST identifies a Nachnia song and a Sajani geet in its musical repertoire.3

Finding an audience at home

In a 2022 interview, Mohapatra recalled that Bhukha did well in western Odisha and also reached audiences in Bhubaneswar, Cuttack and Puri. Looking back on the broader difficulties of distributing his films, he described the separate expense of posters, banners and promotion. Completing a film was only one part of bringing it to viewers.4

In his recollection, the film travelled between western Odisha and the coastal cities. Its local audiences belong to its history alongside the festival record.

A regional voice reaching another audience

The 70th National Film Awards catalogue records an International Jury Award at Gijón for Bhukha, but gives no prize year.5

Amrit Amlan Pattanaik’s study places Mohapatra’s Sambalpuri films within western Odisha’s literary and cultural worlds.6

Following the filmmaker forward

Mohapatra’s later work gives this story another chapter. ଆଦିମ ବିଚାର (Aadim Vichar) received the Best Odiya Film award in the 62nd National Film Awards, announced in March 2015 for films of 2014.7

The drum in this story belongs to someone’s working life. Listening to the music while following that livelihood keeps art and its practitioners together. Mohapatra’s account of reaching viewers adds the next step: a story about performers also needs people willing and able to see it.
