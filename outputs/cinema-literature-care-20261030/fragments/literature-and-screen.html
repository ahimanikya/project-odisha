Sometimes a story comes home as a song, long before anyone asks for the book.

Manas Ranjan Mahapatra remembers watching ଅମଡ଼ା ବାଟ (Amada Bata) with his mother. Years later, working with the National Book Trust, he sought permission to publish Basanta Kumari Pattanayak’s novel. In his account, Dr Narendra Prasad Das and his wife helped him reach the author; Pattanayak asked that the royalties go to the Krishnamurthy Foundation.1

A film seen with a parent, a novel sought out later, an author deciding where its royalties should go: the recollection gives a human shape to the passage between literature and cinema. It is one person’s journey, with a writer and a book at its centre. It also leaves us with a hopeful question. How might more films help their audiences find the writing behind them—and how might writers share in the work and opportunity that follow?

Writers who give a film its first world

A page can hold a voice before a camera finds a face for it. Basanta Kumari Pattanayak’s Amada Bata became a 1964 film directed by Amar Ganguly, with Gopal Chhotray credited for the screenplay.2 The novel supplied a world; Chhotray had to find its life in scenes, speech and silences.

There are other routes through this shared history. Kalindi Charan Panigrahi’s ମାଟିର ମଣିଷ (Matira Manisha) became Mrinal Sen’s 1966 Odia film. Sen’s filmography credits Panigrahi with the story and Sen with screenplay and direction.3 In କା (Kaa), Kanhucharan Mohanty’s novel meets the screenplay and dialogue of Sarada Naik; the Gour–Parbati archive names the directing collective Siddharth as Ram Chandra Thakur, Gour Ghose and Parbati Ghose.4

A novelist may give us access to a character’s thoughts over many pages. A screenwriter must decide what can be seen and heard, what can be left to an actor, and where one scene should end. Dialogue has to live in a performer’s mouth. The source author remains present even when the film finds a different rhythm.

Odia cinema’s literary relationships also include collaboration across languages and filmmaking traditions. Panigrahi and Sen belong in the same account. Their collaboration makes room for a story rooted in Odisha and a filmmaker bringing another experience of cinema to it.

What a story asks us to notice

In the chapter “Bhagia and Saria” of ଛମାଣ ଆଠଗୁଣ୍ଠ (Chha Mana Atha Guntha), Fakir Mohan Senapati’s narrator turns towards educated readers who know distant societies better than their neighbours. The question is deliberately ironic:

“ଆପଣାର ବା ପ୍ରତିବାସୀ ଜାତି ଓ ସମାଜର କଥା ଜାଣିବାର ଦରକାର କ'ଣ ?”

“What need is there to know about our own community and society, or our neighbours’?”

The narrator’s apparent dismissal is a rebuke to that indifference.5

The question reaches beyond any one adaptation. What do we learn to notice when we look closely at a place? A history of cinema can follow famous careers and prizes while attending to the labour, speech and unequal relationships that stories make visible. A familiar neighbourhood may contain lives we have scarcely learned to see.

Parbati Ghose’s chapter follows the novel’s adaptation and its credited makers. Read a passage beside a scene and a more specific conversation becomes possible. What can an actor convey without the narrator’s explanation? Where might a pause, a gesture or a cut carry the work of a sentence? The comparison need not end with a verdict about which form is better. It can deepen our attention to both.

Writing between the stage, radio and cinema

Chhotray wrote across theatre, radio and cinema.6 The family archive for ମା (Maa) credits Chhotray and Biswajit Das jointly for dialogue, while Nitai Palit supplied screenplay and direction.7

A writer’s movement into cinema can also involve learning a new craft. In a 2019 interview, Bijay Mishra recalled learning screenplay writing from Shyamal Mukherjee while working on ଯାଯାବର (Jajabara).8 Such recollections bring the working relationship into view: a literary or theatrical life can continue through conversations, revisions and decisions made for the screen.

Songs open another path. A lyric has to find room within a tune and a voice, yet its phrasing can carry a writer’s wit and feeling. The Akshaya Mohanty chapter follows some of those exchanges through named singers and lyricists. A remembered voice can lead us back to the person who found the words.

In the opening household passage of ରେବତୀ (Rebati), Fakir Mohan describes a child beside her father as he reads and sings:

“ରେବତୀ ପାଖରେ ବସି ଶୁଣୁଥାଏ।”

“Rebati would sit beside him and listen.”9

Rebati’s listening gives this literary household its quiet centre. It offers a parallel to explore alongside AIR Cuttack: a voice gathering listeners beside a family member, around a radio, inside a cinema. Each setting changes who can hear and how a story reaches them.

The connection continues

Pratibha Ray’s official bibliography identifies her novel ଅପରିଚିତା (Aparichita) as adapted into a film and the title story of ମୋକ୍ଷ (Moksha) as adapted into a feature.10 A novel’s sustained companionship and a short story’s concentrated moment offer different possibilities to the screen.

Other paths lead through theatre and contemporary short fiction. ଭୂଖା (Bhukha) grew from Manglu Charan Biswal’s play, bringing folk performers’ livelihoods into a Sambalpuri screen story.11 ପ୍ରତୀକ୍ଷା (Pratikshya) draws on a short story by Gourahari Das.12 BookMyShow’s listing for ଅନନ୍ତା (Ananta) identifies Fakir Mohan’s Randi Pua Ananta as its literary source.13

These examples keep different forms and languages in the picture. A play, a novel and a short story give filmmakers different starting points. Sambalpuri speech and performance carry particular local relationships. Each adaptation also enters the circumstances of its own production and audience.

When a film brings a book into view

Mahapatra’s remembered visit to Amada Bata did not end at the cinema door. Years later, it became part of his search for its author and a new edition. That is one documented journey, not a promise that every adaptation creates readers. It is enough to make us wonder what happens after a film stays with someone.

A character may send us looking for a novel. A line may make us curious about the language in which it was first written. The book need not become an obligation after the pleasure of the film. It can be another pleasure waiting nearby.

There is a practical kindness in keeping that next encounter possible: an obtainable edition, the author’s name, a translation that welcomes another reader. The closing essay returns to the paid work and partnerships that could make more such encounters possible. Here, we can begin with the stories themselves.

Basanta’s novel, Panigrahi’s brothers, Fakir Mohan’s neighbours and Rebati listening beside her father belong to different works. Together they remind us what literature offers the screen: people whose lives deserve our attention, and language through which we can begin to know them.
