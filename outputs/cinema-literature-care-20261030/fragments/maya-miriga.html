In January 2025, an Odia film made four decades earlier appeared on the programme of New York’s Museum of Modern Art. ମାୟା ମିରିଗ (Maya Miriga) was being shown in its restored form, as part of the museum’s film-preservation festival.1

There are two journeys here. One belongs to a film that found audiences far from the place where it was made. The other belongs to the physical material that had to survive for those audiences to keep finding it.

A household large enough to hold a world

Nirad Mohapatra’s 1984 film follows an extended middle-class family in a small Odisha town. MoMA’s account describes a household pulled between obligations and individual ambitions, brought to life by a nonprofessional cast and Bhaskar Chandavarkar’s music.1

Mohapatra’s own note, reproduced in the Bologna programme, describes married sons balancing competing responsibilities and daughters-in-law seeking freedom within the household. His interest held affection and frustration together: family could offer warmth while also constraining the people who lived within it.2

The household was shaped by several kinds of work. The Bologna programme credits Nirad Mohapatra and Bibhuti Patnaik with the story, Rajgopal Mishra with cinematography and Bibekananda Satpathy with editing.2

A house made ready for a family

Mohapatra recalled arriving in Puri months in advance with a small crew and nonprofessional performers. They prepared an abandoned house and its courtyard as the film’s only set. In his director’s note, he described improvising within a written story.3 The household on screen had required another kind of shared work before filming: making a place ready for it.

Different doors into the world

The film was selected for Cannes Critics’ Week in 1984. Mannheim’s 1984 archive records it in the Grand Prize of the Third World competition, shared with ଲେଇଲା ଏ ଲେ ଲୁ (Leila et les loups); that category was distinct from the festival’s overall Grand Prize.45

At home, it received the Second Best Feature Film recognition in the 31st National Film Awards, covering films of 1983 and presented in 1984.6

The work of bringing a film back

The negative had been found abandoned in a warehouse in poor condition, according to Film Heritage Foundation’s Shivendra Singh Dungarpur in the Bologna programme.2

The restored version was assembled from a 16mm original camera negative and a 35mm print. MoMA credits Film Heritage Foundation’s 4K restoration at L’Immagine Ritrovata in Bologna, using material preserved by the foundation and NFDC–National Film Archive of India.1

Bologna’s programme also credits the director’s family, Digital Film Restore for digital restoration, and Prasad Corporation for scanning.2

The restoration was screened at Il Cinema Ritrovato in Bologna on 27 June 2024.2

A new encounter with the family

Prabha, the eldest son Tuku’s wife, is played by Manaswini Mangaraj.27

Writing after that screening, film historian Antti Alanen placed her at the film’s emotional centre and traced the family’s tensions through gender and generation. He admired its atmosphere and music, while also finding moments when it lacked intensity.7

A restoration returns more than an image: it gives another viewer the chance to notice a character, hear the music and disagree with an earlier response. Alanen’s account is one such encounter. The family on screen can enter a new conversation because people kept the film available.
