On 27 June 2024, ମାୟା ମିରିଗ (Maya Miriga) met an audience again in Bologna. Its restored images carried an Odia household story into another generation of viewing.1

Nirad Mohapatra’s film asks what family gives its members—and what it withholds. His programme note holds warmth alongside daughters-in-law’s desire for freedom.1 Bringing that story back to the screen required people to work with a badly damaged negative. The screening was an achievement in preservation as well as an encounter with a film.

A writer’s words find actors; a production finds its audience. Between them lie rehearsals, borrowed spaces, recording sessions and the work of keeping a finished film available. This collection follows those connections through particular films and careers.

Before the camera: Natak, Rasa and Gitinatya

Sita Bibah had a life before the camera. A later report by Minati Singha identifies a play by Kamapal Mishra as the basis of Sita Bibah.2 Piyush Roy’s research describes Goswami’s touring rasalila group and his work training performers. In an interview recorded by Roy, film historian Surya Deo characterises the first film as primarily gitinatya, or musical drama.3

Goswami brought experience of performing, singing and organising a troupe to filmmaking. A familiar dramatic story could now be carried by recorded voices and moving images.

Gitinatya joins dramatic storytelling with music and verse. Theatre scholar Alok Baral places Kalicharan Pattanaik among its practitioners.4 Kalicharan also wrote lyrics for Lalita, according to Bhuyan’s retrospective.5 His work connects musical theatre with the second Odia feature through an identifiable contribution.

The wider performance world deserves its own attention. Our guides to Prahallad Nataka, Pala and Daskathia explore different ways of singing, narrating and enacting a story. They introduce cinema’s neighbours, while Goswami’s own Rasa and theatre background supplies the documented connection to this first production.

The first Odia film: Sita Bibah, 1936

Odia cinema’s first feature was ସୀତା ବିବାହ (Sita Bibah), made by Mohan Sundar Deb Goswami. Film writer Kapilas Bhuyan’s IFFI retrospective records its formal opening in Puri on 28 April 1936, after a Calcutta screening the previous month. The next Odia feature, ଲଳିତା (Lalita), arrived in 1949.5

Thirteen years is a striking interval. A first film establishes possibility; it does not by itself establish a continuing industry. The familiar anniversary date deserves to be remembered alongside the difficulty of making the next film.

Music was present from the beginning. Bhuyan records fourteen songs in Sita Bibah, sung by its actors, and a move to background singing in Lalita. Later films drew on Odia literature, including ଅମଡ଼ା ବାଟ (Amada Bata), ମଲାଜହ୍ନ (Malajanha) and ମାଟିର ମଣିଷ (Matira Manisha).5

The screen offered another life to words and voices already circulating through the culture. Writers and composers helped shape that life alongside directors and performers.

Radio drama: another home for the storyteller

Radio enters this account later. AIR Cuttack began in 1948, twelve years after the first Odia feature. The Odia Bibhaba archive records Gopal Chhotray’s association with the station from its beginning.6 Its writers joined a film culture already underway.

Chhotray is a concrete link between the media. The Library of Congress documents his work in Gitinatya, his long association with AIR Cuttack, and his writing of plays, operas and screenplays.7 The Gour–Parbati archive credits him specifically with dialogue for ଶ୍ରୀଜଗନ୍ନାଥ (Sri Jagannath) (1950), while assigning direction and screenplay to C.R. Mitra.8

Chhotray worked between the stage, the microphone and film production. Dialogue was part of what he carried between them.

The people who built more than a screen career

Parbati Ghose belongs near the centre of this history. The Gour–Parbati filmography records her as a co-producer with Gour Prasad Ghose on ଲକ୍ଷ୍ମୀ (Laxmi), କା (Kaa) and ସ୍ତ୍ରୀ (Stree). It also credits her within the directing partnership used on Kaa and Stree. Her work crossed the boundaries between performing, organising a production and deciding what would reach the screen.9

The archive dates her ଛ’ ମାଣ ଆଠ ଗୁଣ୍ଠ (Chha Mana Atha Guntha) adaptation to 1986. Its credits name Nagen Barik for sound and Asim Basu and Sarat Sahoo for art, and acknowledge Olosingh’s villagers and host Kalyani Patnaik.10 The Parbati Ghose companion follows her producing, writing, directing and acting work.

The places and teams that sustain production

Institutions mattered too. The Odisha Film Development Corporation was incorporated on 22 April 1976. Its stated remit included support for film production, cinema houses, studio services and film culture. These are the less visible foundations: access to equipment, places to finish a film, and places where audiences can watch it.11

The Chha Mana Atha Guntha archive names Kalinga Studio and Prasad Film Laboratory among its credits, while stating that the shoot took place entirely in Olosingh. The credits do not specify which services those facilities supplied.10

Private facilities form another part of this infrastructure. India Cine Hub lists Mute Entertainment’s Mute Studio in Bhubaneswar under post-production companies, advertising editing, visual effects and chroma services.12

Technology also appears in performers’ memories. Uttam Mohanty contrasted reflector-lit shoots earlier in his career with later low-light work, in a 2018 interview.13

Public infrastructure has its own chain of responsibility. Naveen Patnaik laid the rebuilding foundation stone for Kalinga Studio on 25 March 2021; the Industries Department assigned implementation to IDCO and existing-building renovation to the R&B Department.14 Mohan Charan Majhi inaugurated the redeveloped studio and its cinema archive and museum on 5 October 2026. The recorded events concern redevelopment; they do not establish its commercial outcomes.15

A film culture needs places to learn and people willing to organise a screening. Nirad Mohapatra’s memorial biography records his teaching at FTII and his work with Cinexstasy, a Bhubaneswar film society active from 1974 to 1983. Screenings and film-appreciation courses were another part of his contribution, beyond directing Maya Miriga.16

Maya Miriga and other films in circulation

Maya Miriga received Second Best Feature Film at the 31st National Film Awards, for films of 1983; festival records commonly date it to 1984.17 In 1984 it was selected for Cannes Critics’ Week and shared Mannheim’s Grand Prize in the Third World competition, distinct from that festival’s overall Grand Prize.1819 The Maya Miriga companion follows both its family story and its restoration.

Manmohan Mahapatra offers another essential route into this period. ଶୀତରାତି (Seeta Raati) received the Best Oriya Film honour at the 29th National Film Awards, for the 1981 award year. The festival catalogue for 1985 also records screenings in Paris and Montreal.20

In his account of Odisha’s New Wave, critic Ashok Palit describes the social worlds of Mahapatra’s films: a rural community facing upheaval in ନୀରବ ଝଡ଼ (Niraba Jhada), and a family negotiating social change in ନିଷିଦ୍ଧ ସ୍ଵପ୍ନ (Nisiddha Swapna). His survey places Mahapatra and Nirad Mohapatra among the filmmakers who developed a sustained interest in cinema as an art of close observation.21

These films brought close observation of households and communities into national and international circulation. Their companion essays return to the particular relationships and choices within those worlds.

Bhukha, and the importance of hearing western Odisha

Sabyasachi Mohapatra’s ଭୂଖା (Bhukha) (1989) makes the map of cinema in Odisha more expansive. It is a Sambalpuri-language film. Palit reads its conflict through the pressures between inherited ways of life and modernity.21

The story grew from a Sambalpuri play by Manglu Charan Biswal. It follows bajnia folk drummers whose livelihoods are threatened by changing musical practices. The stakes are both cultural and material: what happens to the people who carry a tradition when their work is no longer wanted?22

Naming the language matters. The cinema of a state can contain several cultural centres, accents and storytelling traditions. Sambalpuri cinema deserves to be heard in its own voice within this larger history.

The Bhukha chapter follows the film’s circulation as well as the livelihoods within its story.

Mohapatra’s later work offers further, separately documented milestones. ଶଳାବୁଢ଼ା (Sala Budha) was selected for Indian Panorama in 2013. ଆଦିମ ବିଚାର (Aadim Vichar) received the Best Odiya Film award at the 62nd National Film Awards, for 2014, announced in 2015.2324

Scholar Amrit Amlan Pattanaik approaches these Sambalpuri films through western Odisha’s cultural and literary worlds, asking how they widen the ways Odisha appears on screen. His study’s published abstract offers a valuable starting question for a deeper article: what becomes visible when we stop treating a region as if it has only one centre?25

Children, imagination and the screen

Children belong in this history as viewers with imaginative lives of their own. Live-action films made for children, local animation and films offered in an Odia-language version open different routes to those viewers.

Sadhu Meher’s ବାବୁଲା (Babula) provides a concrete starting point for the first history. A Lok Sabha answer of 7 December 1987 lists it among the Children’s Film Society of India’s productions for 1985–86, identifying it as an Oriya feature. A contemporary 1986 catalogue from Berlin’s Arsenal describes Meher’s CFSI-supported film through a lonely boy’s dreams of outer space. This places institutional support and a child’s imagination within the same account. 2627

A. K. Bir’s ଲାବଣ୍ୟ ପ୍ରୀତି (Lavanya Preeti) received Best Children’s Film for 1993. Its dedicated page follows young characters into the imaginative world of an inherited story.28

Children can also encounter films through Odia-language versions. CBFC’s 2022 list records an Odia video version of ରିଟର୍ଣ୍ଣ ଅଫ୍ ହନୁମାନ (Return of Hanuman), certified on 11 February 2022 and naming Shemaroo Entertainment. Airtel Xstream lists the Anurag Kashyap film as a 2007 animation title available in Odia. This is a route to a dubbed film, distinct from an original Odia production; current access depends on the provider.2930

The people audiences returned to

The history of a film culture also lives in the performers people recognise across different stories. A leading role, a comic interruption, a supporting character or a song can give an audience someone to return to. These careers connect that affection to less visible work: rehearsal, teaching, choreography, producing and helping another performer find her place.

Bijay Mohanty: performance and teaching

Bijay Mohanty carried theatre training into films and into the classroom. A National School of Drama graduate, he worked in the drama department of Utkal Sangeet Mahavidyalaya and retired as a professor. His screen career includes ଚିଲିକା ତୀରେ (Chilika Tire), ନାଗଫାଶ (Nagaphasa) and ଚକା ଭଉଁରୀ (Chaka Bhaunri).31

The theatre connection remained part of how colleagues remembered him. Director Manoj Pattanaik recalled his play Amari Bhagaban, staged by Manan Natya Sangstha.32 The classroom and the theatre group were part of the work he passed on.

Uttam Mohanty: a remembered encounter

Uttam Mohanty's ଅଭିମାନ (Abhiman) debut connects his story to Sadhu Meher, its director. In a 2018 interview, he recalled a Bengali viewer in Cuttack praising his portrayal of Salabega. The memory gives one audience encounter a place beside the career record.13

Akashvani records his work in Bengali cinema and the Hindi film ନୟା ଜହର (Naya Zahar) alongside his Odia career.33 His recollection of reflector-lit shoots also brings an actor’s experience into the changing conditions of filmmaking.

Aparajita Mohanty: acting, producing and learning from another woman

Aparajita Mohanty's work offers several points of entry. ଉଦଣ୍ଡୀ ସୀତା (Udandi Sita) is among the films associated with her screen career.34 She produced Love Master.35

Her remembrance of Anita Das opens another relationship. Aparajita described the senior actor as an elder-sister figure who taught her about acting and the workings of the industry.36 Her later appearance in ବୋଉ ବୁଟୁ ଭୂତ (Bou Buttu Bhuta) brings that working life into the recent films discussed later in this survey.37

The songs people carried home

A film's life need not end when the screen goes dark. A voice, a phrase or a melody can travel into another room and another generation. Following those songs brings composers, lyricists and playback singers into the centre of this story.

The credits of ଶ୍ରୀ ଜଗନ୍ନାଥ (Sri Jagannath, 1950) name Balakrushna Das and Ranjit Roy as music directors, with Kalyani Das, Chhabi Ray and Suryamoni Dei among the singers. Cinema was already a meeting place for many voices.43 Shantanu Mohapatra later composed Lata Mangeshkar's songs for ସୂର୍ଯ୍ୟମୁଖୀ (Suryamukhi) and ଅରୁନ୍ଧତୀ (Arundhati). His son Koushik remembers a young composer approaching her, and producer Dhiren Patnaik backing the later collaboration. Such journeys began with someone making a connection and someone else making room for it.44

Akshaya Mohanty belongs here as a composer, singer and producer. When ମଲାଜହ୍ନ (Malajanha) returned for a Film Journalists' Forum screening, actress Jharana Das, singer Pranab Patnaik, lyricist Binodini Debi and the filmmakers' families were part of the occasion. A literary adaptation had gathered actors, words and music; its return gathered people again.45

There is a smaller, telling story inside Prafulla Kar and Chitta Jena's collaboration. Film historian Surya Deo recalled that Jena wanted a solo and initially resisted another duet for ମମତା (Mamata). Actor Dhira Biswal persuaded him to sing it with P. Susheela. Kar later remembered Jena as a close friend whose voice gave him room to experiment. A song's making could depend on that mixture of ambition, persuasion and trust.46

Other voices deserve their own listening paths. Bhubaneswari Mishra's “Tiki Mora Naa Ti” in କୃଷ୍ଣ ସୁଦାମା (Krushna Sudama) is one such doorway.47 Pranab Patnaik's account of his long friendship and stage work with Prafulla Kar offers another.48 These are starting points for a wider account of the singers, lyricists, arrangers and instrumentalists behind the songs.

The question of writers' livelihoods returns here. A poem can become a lyric; a lyric needs a composer and a voice. Following that shared work also means asking how its authors are credited, paid and remembered. The Songs We Carry Home follows these connections through fifteen musicians, with dedicated stories of Akshaya Mohanty, Prafulla Kar, Pranab Patnaik and Tapu Mishra.

Actors whose journeys crossed languages

For performers, the path beyond Odisha sometimes ran through films made in other languages.

Sadhu Meher received the National Film Award for Best Actor for his performance in Shyam Benegal’s Hindi film ଅଙ୍କୁର (Ankur), honoured at the 22nd awards for 1974. The official catalogue records his upbringing in Odisha and his training at the Film and Television Institute in Pune. This is a major achievement by an actor from Odisha, within the history of Hindi cinema.49

Nandita Das carries a different connection. In her own essay about belonging, she recalls her father’s Baripada roots and her childhood visits. She was born in Bombay and grew up in Delhi. Her Odisha relationship is one of family and experience, rather than an Odisha birthplace.50

Her connection also includes acting in Odia: she appeared in Susant Misra’s ବିଶ୍ୱପ୍ରକାଶ (Biswaprakash / The Young Rebel, 1999), listed in her official filmography. The Nandita Das article explores that credit alongside her wider career.51

Cannes records her membership of its feature-film jury in 2005 and its Cinéfondation and short-film jury in 2013. Her film ମଣ୍ଟୋ (Manto) was selected for Un Certain Regard in 2018. These occasions mark both jury service and film selection across her career in acting and directing.52

A festival history and a history of popular affection answer different questions. We need both to understand what a film culture means to the people who sustain it.

A new search for stories

A film can leave something behind after its run: a new collaborator, an audience willing to try another story, or the confidence to begin a difficult script. ଦମନ (DAMaN) and ପ୍ରତୀକ୍ଷା (Pratikshya) offer two recent starting points for that conversation. Their subjects are different—healthcare in a difficult landscape, and the pressures inside a household—but both give recognisably local lives room on the screen.

We place them together here as an invitation to follow the next chapter of Odia filmmaking. The foundations reach back through the earlier films in this series. What these recent works add is a fresh reason to ask which stories, collaborators and audiences might come together next.

The Pratikshya companion follows the collaboration behind the film, from Gourahari Das’s source story to Dipanwit Dashmohapatra’s first leading role.58

Babushaan Mohanty offers another route into the period through ଦମନ (DAMaN). Our companion article follows the film’s making, its fictional doctor and the wider healthcare work behind its subject. These films invite us to follow performers and collaborators as well as prizes: who receives a substantial role, whose writing reaches the screen, and which stories find a larger audience?

The range also includes କର୍ମ (Karma, 2024). Anupam Patnaik directed Anubhav Mohanty as investigator Amar Patnaik in a murder-case thriller. The filmmaker behind Pratikshya was working with a different star and dramatic form; contemporary Odia cinema cannot be reduced to a single kind of revival.59

Recent releases and the work of reaching audiences

A film culture also depends on tickets sold and the chance to finance the next production. In 2025, ବୋଉ ବୁଟୁ ଭୂତ (Bou Buttu Bhuta) supplied a conspicuous commercial story. Directed by Jagdish Mishra, the horror-comedy brought Aparajita Mohanty, Babushaan Mohanty and Archita Sahu together in leading roles. On 24 September 2025, The New Indian Express reported box-office revenue of nearly ₹21 crore, against a reported budget of ₹2–3 crore. These are reported figures, not audited accounts or a calculation of producer profit.37

The same report described Dharma Productions as being in the process of acquiring remake rights. That was a commercial development reported at the time, not evidence of a completed remake. It suggests another way an Odia story can travel: through interest in adapting it for other audiences.37

Commercial momentum has a setting as well as a number. During Raja in June 2025, The New Indian Express reported packed shows for Bou Buttu Bhuta and ଅନନ୍ତା (Ananta). The latter starred Sabyasachi Mishra and was directed by Sabyasachi Mohapatra, drawing on Fakir Mohan Senapati’s Randi Pua Ananta. Literature and popular exhibition were meeting again.60

Yet distributor Prashant B, quoted in that report, said a shortage of cinema halls left some areas unreached. The point matters: a successful run in available theatres does not mean everyone has access to the film. The commercial story includes the work of exhibitors and distributors, ticket affordability, and the distance between a willing viewer and a screen.60

These recent films extend several parts of the history at once: literary adaptation, substantial acting roles, genre entertainment and the search for a sustainable audience. A national award, a busy screening and a remake negotiation each tell us something different. Taken together, they give a fuller picture of the work still being made.

New stories to follow

Hope becomes more tangible when we can name a film, the people behind it and the possibility it opens. These examples invite further viewing and discussion across different kinds of storytelling.

ବର ବଧୂ (Bara Badhu): work, friendship and dignity

Bara Badhu brings the search for a livelihood into a story of relationships. CBFC’s synopsis describes unemployed Biju entering a marriage arrangement with Suman, with consequences for love and loyalty. The record credits Biswaranjan Pradhan with direction, story, screenplay and dialogue, and Chandra Mani / Useful Garbage Creations with production. BookMyShow lists its release on 13 February 2026.6162

The ensemble includes Sailendra Samantaray, Sivani Sangita, Suryamayee Mohapatra and Subasis Sharma. Bijitesh De’s cinematography and Deepika Kalara’s editing are also part of the work behind the relationships on screen.6261

The premise gives it a place beside Pratikshya: economic insecurity enters intimate life, and a decision about work becomes a decision about other people. That is a thematic connection, rather than an established adaptation or influence. Its official Amara Muzik Odia trailer offers an introduction for readers who want to explore it.63

ମାଲିପୁଟ (Maliput Melodies): making room for local voices

At IFFI in November 2025, director Vishal Patnaik and producer Kaushik Das presented an anthology rooted in rural Koraput. The official account describes a production lasting more than two and a half years, with local performers coached for their roles. The filmmakers spoke of local food, speech and everyday culture as part of what they wanted audiences to encounter.57

The festival credits give that collective work names: Sudhakar Patnaik wrote the screenplay, Tosh Nanda composed the music, and Vishal Patnaik and Santosh Kumar Nanda edited the film. Its ensemble includes Md. Shariff, Sona Khemundu, Usha Rani Dash, Dhana Harijan and Brundaban Hantal, among others.64

Here the possibility lies in who gets to participate and whose surroundings become a film’s world. Its Indian Panorama screening is a concrete point of recognition, while the AAO NXT trailer offers a way into the work.65

ମଧୁମାଳତୀ (Madhumallati): another age of love

Madhumallati places older lovers at the centre of a musical drama. Its release listing names Sanjay Mishra and Mahasweta Ray, director Mrutyunjaya Sahu and writer Priyabrata Panda, with a story of reunion after 35 years apart. BookMyShow dates the release to 16 October 2026.66

The subject broadens the range of lives and relationships a popular film can hold. Alongside the younger characters, rural worlds and genre experiments elsewhere in this chapter, it gives audiences another kind of story to seek out.

These films do not need to resemble one another to sustain the same language. The commercial-successes chapter follows evidence of audience spending; the economics chapter asks how that income can support further work. The opportunity is to turn the desire for a good story into sustained support for writers, performers, crews and the next production.

On the horizon

These October 2026 announcements introduce two projects to follow. Their finished work and its reception remain ahead.

ଦଶାହ (Dassaaha): returning to an audience

Anupam Patnaik announced Dassaaha at a special screening of Pratikshya. That setting brings a new project before people already gathered around an earlier story. Its announced interest in tradition, emotion and life and death offers a direction to explore; a detailed plot, cast and release date were not established in the material available.67

The title poster gives the work a more concrete shape. Barsha Patnaik and Sanghamitra Behera are its producers, with Bishnu Sahoo on cinematography, Rashmi Ranjan Dash on editing, and Ashish Pradhan and Anurag Patnaik on music. Anupam Patnaik is credited with story and screenplay. These are announced production credits, under Amiya Patnaik Productions in association with Shubham Cine Productions.68

The continuity worth following is the relationship between a filmmaking team and its audience: what will people who connected with one film be invited to consider in the next? That remains an open question. A shared occasion or promotional connection does not establish that the new film is a sequel.

ଦେବକନ୍ୟା (Debakanya): a performance tradition meets the screen

Rasesh Mohanty’s Debakanya, starring Subhasis Sharma, places a folk performer’s personal and social life within a story involving Puri’s Ghoda Nacha. The production announced a November 2026 theatrical release.69

The tradition opens a wider path into the series. Odisha Tourism describes Chaiti Ghoda as a performance associated with coastal fishing communities: a dancer appears mounted on a dummy horse, mythological episodes are enacted, and improvised dialogue is part of the performance.70 That combination of movement, speech and shared cultural memory takes us back to the storytelling forms discussed at the beginning of this chapter.

A film can bring a performance tradition before viewers who have never encountered it in person. It can also prompt us to seek out the performers themselves. When Debakanya becomes available, there will be room to ask how its fictional story engages with the skills, livelihoods and voices of the people who carry this tradition. The announcement alone cannot answer that question.

Release plans can change. These projects are invitations to follow the work, with space for audiences and practitioners to add their own responses when the films can be seen.

Audiences and income beyond Odisha

The New Indian Express reported that ଦମନ (DAMaN) opened in 51 theatres in Odisha and 15 across nine other cities in November 2022.71 The DAMaN reading follows that wider reach and the responses to the film.

Overseas announcements show another route to audiences. On 26 April 2023, OrissaPOST reported plans to screen ଫାଲ୍ଗୁନ ଚୈତ୍ର (Phalguna Chaitra) on 13 May at Hoyts Docklands in Melbourne, Hoyts Blacktown in Sydney and Event Cinemas in Brisbane. A July follow-up in OrissaPOST subsequently reported overseas screenings, including Australia. Neither report supplied receipts or a country-by-country earnings breakdown.7273

Streaming can reach viewers who have no nearby Odia screening. In Lata Jha’s April 2025 report for Mint, Kaushik Das, founder of Odia platform AAO NXT, described overseas viewers seeking stories that reconnect them with home. 74

Paid screenings, streaming and rights agreements can provide income, but these sources do not establish the share of Odia cinema’s earnings from outside the state or overseas. The OTT companion follows the platform and audience questions further.

The story continues on television and online

Cinema is one part of a wider screen culture. Odia television created another rhythm of storytelling through recurring programmes and serials. Prasar Bharati dates the Odia satellite service to 1993 and its round-the-clock broadcasting to 2001.75

Streaming added further routes. Tarang Plus’s 2019 launch offering combined television channels, films, jatra and original web content. An inherited catalogue and a newly commissioned series could now reach viewers through the same service, although they remained different kinds of production.76

The Odia television history follows public broadcasting, private channels, performers and production workers. The Odia OTT journey examines original storytelling, platform economics and audiences beyond Odisha. Together with AIR Cuttack, these histories show how stories can move between forms without every new medium displacing its predecessors.

Keeping the next chapter within reach

The Bologna screening at the beginning of this story adds another question to this contemporary energy. What can another generation actually see?

Preservation depends on teams too. Shivendra Singh Dungarpur describes Film Heritage Foundation’s conservators repairing Maya Miriga. Its restoration credits also name L’Immagine Ritrovata, Digital Film Restore and Prasad Corporation, in association with the filmmaker’s family, and identify NFDC–NFAI’s surviving print. A restored image carries technical labour that a director’s name alone cannot acknowledge.77

The work behind those names deserves attention too.

The next chapter of Odia cinema history belongs partly to viewers: watching beyond the famous titles, learning the names behind the credits, asking for subtitled and restored editions, and making room for films whose worlds are unfamiliar even within Odisha.

For a viewing journey, one starting point is the household in Maya Miriga, followed by the livelihoods in Bhukha and today’s family dramas, thrillers and popular releases. The companion readings stay with their households, livelihoods and collaborators in greater detail. Which film would you want the next generation to be able to find?
