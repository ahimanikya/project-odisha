For an on-demand viewer, an episode can fit around the day rather than a fixed broadcast hour. A viewer may pause a story, return to it after work, or discover an Odia film far from a cinema that screens it. Streaming changes the circumstances of watching. Whether it also changes the stories depends on the people commissioning, writing and making them.

Odia OTT belongs within the longer history of theatre, radio, television and cinema. It provides another route to audiences, while drawing on works and performers already moving between those media.

What enters the catalogue

OTT, or over-the-top delivery, broadly describes media delivered through the internet. For this history, it helps to distinguish three kinds of offering: live or catch-up television, films acquired for streaming, and productions commissioned for a platform. An app can contain all three. “Original” and “exclusive” also need care: a work available exclusively through one service is not necessarily a work that service originally commissioned.

That is why the Odia journey has several starting points. A broadcaster can extend an existing television library; an independent service can build a catalogue and produce new work; a filmmaker can license a completed film.

From broadcaster to streaming service

A contemporary launch report dates Tarang Plus to 27 October 2019, after an earlier planned launch was delayed. It described a service combining the network’s television channels with films, jatra, early access to serials and original web content. This was a bridge between established broadcasting and a different way of watching.1

Chumbak and the possibility of different stories

By March 2020, ଚୁମ୍ବକ (Chumbak) was providing a concrete example. MyCityLinks reported on the Tarang Plus series directed by Susant Mani, with Tamanna Vyas and Divyadisha Mohanty, about a same-gender relationship. Mani described the opportunity to experiment within a limited budget; Mohanty discussed preparing for a role that initially took her outside her comfort zone.2

The new commission asked something of both director and performer: a story Mani wanted to attempt, and a role Mohanty had to prepare for.

Independent platforms build another route

Kaushik Das, founder of AAO NXT, described a progression from AAO TV in 2019, which brought existing online Odia content together, to a fuller OTT service launched in December 2020. In his February 2023 interview, he explained that the venture needed both a technology operation and a production unit. His account places the work of building a catalogue beside the work of making originals.3

Kanccha Lannka represents another initiative. In a March 2025 interview, co-founder Bibhu Prasad Rath dated its establishment to 2021, with Akshay Parija and CR Nayak. He named programmes including Gangs of Puri, Barang 1999 and Okha Express, and discussed the challenge of meeting production expectations shaped by other language industries.4

Building a service means keeping both parts working: a place where viewers can find programmes and a continuing relationship with the people who make them.

Finding a place among other apps

Distribution also changed through bundles. On 12 January 2023, Tata Play announced the addition of Tarang Plus to its Binge service, alongside other regional platforms. A viewer could encounter Odia programming through a shared subscription interface rather than searching for the standalone app. 5

Language can travel beyond the state

Streaming offers a route to people who cannot attend an Odia theatrical screening nearby. In an April 2025 Mint report, Das described overseas viewers seeking stories that reconnect them with their cultural roots. His account connects the search for an audience with the wish to hear familiar language and stories.6 A different encounter appears in Madhushree Chakrabarty’s December 2022 review of Abhishek Swain’s anthology Four: she described following its stories as a non-Odia viewer.7

Language access matters too. Kanccha Lannka’s App Store description advertises English subtitles for selected titles. “Selected” is significant: the availability of an app abroad does not mean every programme has subtitles or can be played in every territory.8

Subtitles can invite a viewer who does not know Odia into the story. They can also help a younger diaspora viewer follow a language heard at home. Careful translation, clear credits and reliable access can help that invitation reach more people.

Reach must become sustainable work

A catalogue needs more than an upload button. Das described the expense of maintaining the platform and producing content, alongside the difficulty of making potential viewers aware of a regional service.3 For an Odia production, being discoverable is only one part of the business. Payment arrangements, rights, promotion and continued availability shape whether a release supports the next project.

A durable home for these stories must serve both sides of the screen: viewers able to find the work, and makers paid on terms that help them continue. The economics chapter follows that question from an audience’s payment towards the next production.
