A collection of magazines, songbooks and photographs once needed a mini-truck to make its journey from Puri to Bhubaneswar. Recalling it in a 2022 interview, Surya Deo credited Srikant Mohanty, publisher of Manoranjan, with entrusting the material to him.1 There is something quietly moving about that scale: cinema’s memory travelling as things someone had kept.

The gesture belongs to a larger story. Deo’s writing, collecting, exhibitions and screenings have helped make Odia cinema available for another kind of encounter—one that continues after the lights come up.

A question sent him to the newspapers

Deo traced the beginning to a cinema-history column for Dharitri’s Sunday supplement, Chhutidina, developed with fellow journalists Gopal Mohapatra and Bibhuti Mishra. Dependable information was difficult to find. He remembered Akshaya Mohanty encouraging him to consult old newspapers, and acknowledged the help of journalists, Film Journalists’ Forum members and film workers. He also described setting aside part of his earnings for the work.1

A column needs an answer by its deadline. Research can leave the writer with more questions. A notice may establish that a film was advertised; a songbook may preserve a lyricist’s name. Neither, by itself, explains everything that happened. Their value grows when they can be read together.

When a singer finds a photograph she does not own

At Geeti Chhaya, an exhibition opened at the Odisha State Museum in 2017, singer Trupti Das expressed surprise at seeing photographs she did not herself possess. Reporting on the exhibition described more than a hundred photographs of film-music work from 1950 to 1986, alongside gramophone records. The Culture Department and Film Journalists’ Forum collaborated on it; Deo credited material gathered from producers, directors, composers, lyricists, researchers and actors.2

The photograph of a recording session can change the way we remember a song. It returns the song to a room of people working together. The singer remains visible, but so do the composer, the equipment and the other hands behind the sound.

Writing that travels beyond a screening

Some of the writing collected in Kali Paine Kalira Cinema grew from booklets prepared around Film Journalists’ Forum screenings. The book brings together accounts of 21 films, giving an evening’s encounter a life on the page.3 Its recognition was a Special Mention in Best Writing on Cinema at the 68th National Film Awards, for the 2020 award cycle, announced in 2022.4

Deo’s wider work includes Odia Cinema Rupa Rupantara, Jhura Mana Jhara Giti and compilations of Odia film lyrics. A narrative history, writing about music and a collection of song texts serve different readers and different questions.5 Our guide to books on Odia cinema places these alongside work by other authors.

The National Film Archive of India also lists his History of Odia Film among its completed research-fellowship projects.6

Finding a copy is also work

A 2024 account of efforts to screen ଉଲ୍କା (Ulka) describes a damaged film print that could not be projected. A U-matic video cassette was subsequently traced and cleaned for use.7 It was a way to bring the film back before viewers; the report does not establish a restoration from the original film negative.

A surviving video copy allowed an audience to meet the film again. Its recovery mattered, even while the future of the film elements remained a separate question.

Memory can become another film

Filmmaker Lipika Singh Darai’s project Rediscovering Sita Bibaha provides a particularly suggestive connection. India Foundation for the Arts names Deo as her collaborator and describes gramophone recordings, contracts, poster copies and press material in his collection informing her search. The project concerns the missing first Odia film and the world around its making.8

Here, the archive is part of a new creative process. A filmmaker asks what surviving traces can tell us—and how to tell a story when the film itself is absent. Our companion article follows that search.

Deo’s public record gives us a picture of sustained attention, supported by many people who kept something, shared something or helped an audience see it again. For a cinema seeking its next possibilities, that work offers more than reassurance about the past. It leaves material with which someone else can begin.
