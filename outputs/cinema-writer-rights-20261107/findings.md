# Odia writers and the passage from story to film

Two public posts by Gourahari Das give the *Bapa–Pratikshya* inquiry a more specific starting point. In his 5 December 2022 account, Das says he wrote *Bapa* in 2000 and identifies its inclusion in Rupa’s *The Little Monk and Other Stories*. He also names an earlier television film, *BAPA*, made by Nirmal Rout of Bhadrak. These are useful author-supplied leads for locating the text and another adaptation; the translated story title, translator and exact edition pages still need checking.[^bapa]

In a separate public post of 25 October 2026, Das reports being recognised as *Pratikshya*’s story writer at an OFDC event at Kalinga Studio the previous day. He names Roshan separately as screenwriter and Anupam Patnaik as director. This is a concrete account of recognition, attributed to the author; neither post discloses a source-story fee, royalty or contractual credit condition.[^recognition]

The last two entries come from a family retrospective, not inspected agreements. Its consent recollection is worth preserving as a lead, with that distinction intact.[^archive]

## Different kinds of money

A payment for permission to use an existing story answers a different question from a fee for developing the screenplay or dialogue. A person may undertake more than one role, but a combined credit does not tell us whether the payments were combined, separately negotiated or received. Later royalties, book-sales royalties and remake-rights transactions also need their own evidence.

Two existing leads illustrate why the distinction matters. The official IFFI account describes Anupam Kher giving filmmaker Anupam Patnaik a token signing amount for Hindi remake rights. It does not describe a payment to Gourahari Das. Manas Ranjan Mahapatra’s recollection of Basanta Kumari Patnaik concerns permission for an NBT book edition and her requested destination for book royalties, not her compensation for the earlier *Amada Bata* film.[^different-payments]

The bounded searches around Bibhuti Patnaik, Pratibha Ray and older adaptations did not produce a defensible source-author fee rate or verified payment evidence. That finding describes the records examined, not every Odia agreement. It cannot establish that anyone went unpaid.

## Practical distinctions for the next conversation

The Copyright Act separates the underlying work from the film. Its assignment provisions identify the work, rights, duration, territory and consideration; its royalty provisions have defined uses and exceptions, rather than prescribing a universal adaptation fee. Authorship remains distinct from ownership transfer. These principles require the actual agreement and applicable law before a conclusion about any individual case.[^act]

SWA’s published guidance distinguishes consideration from royalties and addresses credit, termination and the rights assigned. Its member service offers contract and copyright guidance. SRAI’s current registration terms accept eligible story, screenplay and dialogue writers across languages, but explicitly distinguish membership from immediate royalty payments. Neither organisation’s guidance proves an individual writer’s receipts.[^support]

The most useful next evidence would identify the particular work and transaction: who granted which rights, what was agreed, what payment is being described and whether it was received. An author’s dated account would add a participant’s experience; an authorised agreement or receipt would answer different questions. These can be recorded without publishing confidential terms or turning one experience into an industry-wide rate. For now, the strongest new finding is a clearer literary and credit trail for *Bapa*, alongside a compensation question that remains open.
