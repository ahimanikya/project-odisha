Godabarish Mahapatra and the Pages of Niankhunta

By Ahimanikya Satapathy

Godabarish Mahapatra brought poetry and public criticism into the work of editing a periodical. Remembered as a poet and short-story writer, he was the founding editor of Niankhunta, an Odia magazine whose humour addressed public life.12

A writer and a recurring public voice

Gouri Kumar Brahma’s account dates the start of Niankhunta to 1938 and describes Mahapatra’s use of wit to confront the conduct of socially powerful people. Brahma also draws attention to his sympathy for poorer people. Humour and concern for other lives belong together in this account of the writer: the joke can expose an abuse, while a story can give attention to the person living with its consequences.2

Mahapatra studied at Satyabadi Bana Bidyalaya. His mentor, Pandit Godabarish Mishra, was a different writer; their similar names should not merge their work. Brahma describes Mishra’s guidance in Mahapatra’s move to the school.12

What a magazine page can tell us

The first-volume scan places two-column political commentary and short verse among advertisements and publication notices. One imprint names D. M. Pattajosi as editor and a press at Berhampur; an adjacent notice records an editorial change. These details keep other participants visible. Mahapatra’s founding role does not establish his authorship of every unsigned passage.3

Read the imprint as carefully as the headline. Then follow the movement between prose and verse: where does the page slow the eye, and where does a shorter line interrupt it?

Poetry beyond the periodical

The Sahitya Akademi’s award register places Banka O Sidha in its 1966 Odia list, identifies it as poetry and marks the award to Godavarish Mahapatra as posthumous. Mahapatra had died on 25 November 1965. The award offers another entrance to his writing, alongside the magazine pages.14

Odia Bibhaba’s chronological index credits most of its copies to Bhabani Shankar Mahapatra, the writer’s son and a former joint editor. Its holdings extend beyond the founder’s lifetime, with missing issues explicitly acknowledged. The surviving copies give readers a place to begin, without promising a complete run.5
