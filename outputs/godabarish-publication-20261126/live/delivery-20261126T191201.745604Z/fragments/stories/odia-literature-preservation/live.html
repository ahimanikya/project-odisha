Odia Bibhaba and the Work of Keeping Literature Available

By Ahimanikya Satapathy

In this article

A book can survive and still be out of reach. Its title may appear in a bibliography, its author may be celebrated, and a copy may remain somewhere in a private collection. Yet a reader who wants to read the work may have no practical way to find it.

Odia Bibhaba’s Pathagara addresses that distance between remembering literature and being able to read it. Its entrance leads to newspapers, magazines, books, reference works and collected writings. Dictionaries and bibliographies sit alongside literary collections. This breadth matters: a language’s written life includes the tools people used to understand it, as well as the poems and stories they made with it.1

How a science project became an archive

The group behind Odia Bibhaba is Srujanika. Its account traces the preservation effort to science writing in Odia: collecting articles and examining dictionaries for terminology led it to Gopal Chandra Praharaj’s Purnachandra Odia Bhasakosha and to the difficulty of finding old publications.2

Srujanika dates Project Rebati to 2004 and the digital Bhasakosha to 2006. It describes homemade imaging equipment, open-source software and a see-through scanner for thick volumes. Before an archive becomes a website, somebody has to find a workable way of copying the book. Its later compilation of Odia periodicals from 1856 to 1950 included chronological and author indexes—tools for finding writing within the pages.2

The institutional history also needs its dates. In a post dated 23 May 2021, Srujanika said its digitisation work had ended and that work had begun at SOA’s PPRACHIN using its equipment and experience. It said it would continue managing the website for the time being. This records a transition; it does not establish the present arrangement or mean that the online collection closed.3

Preserving the conversation around a work

Old magazines preserve a different kind of literary history from a collected edition. An issue places writing beside other writing. Its contents, sequence and publication date can help a reader follow the discussion in which a poem, essay or story first circulated. Odia Bibhaba’s magazine introduction draws attention to the role of periodicals in debate and in the development of writers. It also describes the difficulty of locating surviving issues.4

For a reader, the value lies in being able to return to particular publications. A general statement about the richness of Odia literature becomes a more useful invitation when it leads to something one can open and read.

The Gopal Chhotray collection offers one such route. Its catalogue brings together plays, one-act plays, operas, collected works, translations and material labelled as unpublished manuscripts. It lists a 1974 edition of Srimati Samarjani, which the collection identifies as Chhotray’s opera adaptation of Fakir Mohan Senapati’s Patent Medicine. It also lists his translation Natasamrat in a 1994 edition.5

Together, these entries suggest questions worth pursuing in the works themselves. How does a short story change when it becomes musical theatre? What happens when a play travels into another language? A useful archive allows the reader to move beyond an author’s reputation towards the forms, editions and relationships that make up a writing life.

More ways to keep Odia literature in circulation

Odia Bibhaba belongs within a wider effort to keep Odia literature available to readers and listeners. Recite World, Odia Wikisource and Odia Virtual Academy offer different ways to encounter that literature, hear its words and return to its texts.

Recite World brings listening into this picture. Its own account identifies it as a programme of the Bhubaneswar-based Udaygiri Foundation Trust, intended to make Odia literary heritage accessible through listening and reading. It gives particular importance to dramatisation, pronunciation and encounters with unfamiliar words.6

Its page for Fakir Mohan Senapati’s Patent Medicine brings together an author introduction, a story description inviting the visitor to listen, and a PDF link.7 The title connects with the Gopal Chhotray adaptation catalogued by Odia Bibhaba. A reader can therefore approach the same literary work through a story presentation and the record of its transformation for the stage.

This adds another dimension to preservation: a voice can draw attention to rhythm, dialogue and humour, inviting someone to return to the written work. A recorded reading is also an interpretation. Its performer and recording deserve their own credits alongside the writer and the edition used.

Odia Wikisource shows how preservation can continue beyond the scan. Its page for Fakir Mohan Senapati’s Rebati presents the story as readable Odia text, with editing history and links to reading formats. A Wikimedia account published in January 2015 describes work on Bhagabata that included converting legacy-font text, proofreading and formatting. This was work on the usability and accuracy of the words, as well as their availability.8

The project also makes unfinished work visible. Its scan-linked index for the 1901 Barnabodha has numbered pages and a status indicating that the file needs checking. Such signals help readers distinguish a text that is available from one whose checking is complete.9

Odia Virtual Academy provides another entrance to Odia material. Its stated remit includes books, journals, manuscripts and archival records. Its novel catalogue offers discovery by title and author and lists works such as Antima Jatra and Matimatala. This is useful to a reader who knows a writer’s name but needs help finding a work. A catalogue listing, however, should not be mistaken for a guarantee that every linked file is complete or searchable.10

These collections can overlap. A scan may travel between a digitising group, a hosting institution and a volunteer transcription project. Recognising each contribution gives a clearer account of the work than treating every website as an entirely separate rescue of the same book.

What makes preservation useful

These efforts deserve attention for the patient work they make possible: locating a copy, identifying an edition, imaging a page, recording its details, and checking its words. The finished website is the visible point at which that work reaches a reader.

For someone using an archive, a few small habits help preserve the value of what has been found. Note the author and title, but also the edition, publication year and page. Retain the link to the particular copy. If a word is unclear, keep the uncertainty visible rather than silently supplying a more familiar spelling. When quoting or republishing, look for the item’s stated reuse conditions; a download button alone does not explain them.

For families and institutions holding old publications, the first contribution may be a careful list of what survives. An incomplete run of a magazine can still be worth identifying. A title page, a missing issue, or a different edition can matter to someone trying to reconstruct a work’s history. Any digitisation should be arranged with the custodian and with appropriate care for the original.

Odia Bibhaba gives us a place to begin reading. The next step is to use it attentively: open a magazine, follow an author’s collection, consult a reference work, and carry the source details into whatever we write next. Preservation becomes part of a language’s continuing life when the material can be found, examined and read again.
