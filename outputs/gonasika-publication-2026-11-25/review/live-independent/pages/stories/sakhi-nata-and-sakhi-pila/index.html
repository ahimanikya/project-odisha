Sakhi Nata and Sakhi Pila: song, dancer and companion

By Ahimanikya Satapathy

In this article

In Sakhi Nata, watch the dancer who is responding as well as the dancer taking the lead. Dinanath Pathy’s account describes an exchange: one performer expresses a feeling, a companion answers, and their roles can change. Song and gesture acquire meaning through that relationship.1

A companion who performs

Pathy describes sakhipilas as boy performers dressed in female attire and places Sakhi Nata in southern Odisha. His account emphasises duet and group interaction. The companion is an active part of the presentation, helping make the song’s feeling legible through a response.1

He also describes dancers singing, with an ensemble of Mardala, gini and harmonium. This makes the voice central to his account of the form. It offers a useful viewing question: how do the sung phrase, gesture and partner’s response work together?1

The word sakhi also appears in poetry as a friend or confidante. Our Champu article follows that literary role. Here the subject is a performance practice and the people who carry it; a shared word is a starting point for comparison, not a complete definition.

A musician and a place

Prateek Pattanaik’s The Odissi Project identifies Balaram Gouda of Naikanipalli, Khallikote, Ganjam, as a musician trained in Sakhi Nata. Its record of a session with him gives the subject a named voice and a specific place.3

The catalogue lists Malli Mala Syamaku Debi by Upendra Bhanja, Chhaila Bara Chhamure Padili by Gopalakrusna Pattanayaka, and Nua-nata-patali-mukuta He, the ଙ song from Kabisurjya Baladeba Ratha’s Kisora-Chandrananda Champu. The last entry identifies Raga Kumbhakamodi.3

The session credits also name Bhimasen Mahankud and Praphulla Swain for Mardala and Khola, and Gaurahari Khuntia and Jagannath Dora for gini and other accompaniment. These are credits for this documented session, not a prescribed ensemble for every Sakhi Nata troupe.3

Following the repertoire makes several of our earlier subjects meet: Bhanja’s poetry, Champu’s organised sequence of songs, and the skill of a singer who carries them into performance. A poet’s name alone cannot explain a rendition. Keeping the performer and accompanists in view gives the song a fuller history.

Sakhi Nata in the history of Odissi

In her account of Odissi’s development, Ileana Citaristi identifies Dakhina Nata or Sakhi Nata from southern Odisha among the traditions that entered the theatre movement of the early 1940s. She discusses it alongside other streams, including Gotipua, rasleela and Chhau Nata.4

This is a more useful historical route than treating modern Odissi as the unchanged continuation of one older form. Theatre brought artists and practices into contact. Naming those contributing traditions helps us see the work of transmission without reducing Sakhi Nata to a preliminary stage whose only significance lies elsewhere.

Sakhi Nata and Gotipua should therefore be read in relation, with attention to the author, locality and period in which each name is used. A rigid rule based only on whether one dancer or several dancers appear would obscure the actual practices.

The film called Sakhi Pila

The JD Centre of Art’s February 2020 festival programme lists Sakhi Pila (Boy Odissi Dancer) as a 1999 Indian film by Santosh Gour, with a running time of 18 minutes. Its synopsis describes young boys performing devotional dances and places the film under Gotipua.5

The Films Division catalogue independently supplies a production record: entry 6236, film NM0401, credits Santosh Gour as both director and scriptwriter, with the year 1999 and a duration field of 17.30. This is stronger evidence for the production credit than a social caption, although it does not establish which film an unidentified excerpt comes from.6

That catalogue usage matters: the name Sakhi Pila does not automatically identify every source as a study of the southern Sakhi Nata tradition. The programme gives us a film to seek and a director to credit, but its short description cannot stand in for watching the film.

The post that prompted this inquiry credits “Santosh Kumar.” Whether its excerpt comes from the film catalogued under Santosh Gour remains unconfirmed. The person called “Kelu sir” in the caption also needs to be identified from the film’s credits or another reliable record.7

Listening to the whole exchange

For a first encounter, begin with a named performance or recording. Follow one phrase through the voice, the movement and the companion’s answer. Then return to the lyric and its poet. This keeps the interest of the tradition where it belongs: in the work of performers making words and music intelligible together.
