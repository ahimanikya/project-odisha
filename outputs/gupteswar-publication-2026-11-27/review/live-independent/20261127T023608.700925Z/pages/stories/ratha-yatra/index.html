The Year That Feeds Us

Ratha Yatra: When the Deities Come Out to the Road

By Ahimanikya Satapathy

In Baripada, a woman pulling Subhadra’s chariot can leave part of the journey for tomorrow. On the first day the deities of Haribaldev Jew temple come out in procession and take their seats. The pulling begins the next day. Balabhadra travels all the way to the Gundicha temple, while the women drawing his sister stop about halfway. They return to the ropes to finish the journey before Jagannath follows. A photograph published by The Telegraph in 2016 shows women at work on that middle day. 1 2

Only a fortnight earlier, in the story of Snana Purnima, devotees had watched the siblings bathe and then part from them. During Anasara, the deities are believed to be unwell and rest away from public view. In Puri their reappearance is called Naba Jaubana Darshana, the sight of them in renewed youth. People come to the shrine to see them again. At Ratha Yatra (ରଥଯାତ୍ରା), on the second day of the bright fortnight of Ashadha, the welcome reaches the road. 3 4

Before the ropes are drawn

Subarnapur in western Odisha has its own reckoning of the reunion. Writing in 2005, Pabitra Mohan Nayak placed the town’s Netrotsava, the festival associated there with the deities’ renewed youth, on the first day of the waxing moon of Ashadha. In his account, a Maharana who painted the images received a length of cloth from the Maharaja. So did the Mahalik who brought a food offering on behalf of the Keot community. There was work for whole families before anyone could draw a wheel. Carpenters built the chariot, blacksmiths supplied its nails, tailors stitched its striped cloth, and the malis supplied flowers. 5

In Puri, the gathering has three chariots to watch. Servitors escort the deities out in the swaying procession called pahandi. Balabhadra takes his place on Taladhwaja, Subhadra on Darpadalana and Jagannath on Nandighosa. The Gajapati, Puri’s king, comes to sweep the chariot decks with a golden broom in chhera pahanra. Only after the sovereign has done a sweeper’s service do the chariots set out for Gundicha. As the devotees take up the ropes on the Bada Danda, the Grand Road, it is the elder brother’s Taladhwaja that moves first. 6 7

At Ichhapur in Kendrapara, a person waiting beside the road can see the whole family pass together. Baladevjew, the elder brother who presides at the temple, takes Subhadra and Jagannath with him on a single chariot called Brahma Taladhwaja. Sudarshan travels with them. The local account describes an opening pull of only a short distance. The journey continues the following day. 8

Who takes the broom, who takes the rope

At Koraput’s Sabara Srikhetra, the welcome begins with food from named villages. In 2024 the Sabar community of Kandapali under Nandapur block supplied the first offering, which the temple servitors prepared. People from Siribeda and Kakrigumma also brought bhog. There were three chariots and three people to sweep them. Dasi Nayak of Rangabali Kumbha swept Jagannath’s, Sadhu Saunta of Kakrigumma swept Balabhadra’s, and Danu Tadingi of Kotia swept Subhadra’s. Only then were the chariots drawn towards the Gundicha temple. For a family coming down from one of those villages, the day’s service could begin at home with what it had to bring. 9

In Baripada, taking the rope of Subhadra’s chariot has a history that women can date. In 1975, International Women’s Year, five women made a symbolic pull. Women have since taken responsibility for drawing her chariot. The overnight stop is near the town police station, a landmark familiar to anyone giving directions in Baripada. A devotee who has missed the first stretch can find Subhadra there and come back for the next. 1 2

A child’s first meal of rice could bring a family to the chariots in Sambalpur. A 2009 account records a custom called Ratha Chhuan. After the first-rice ceremony, parents brought the child to touch the chariot and the deity and prayed for the child’s wellbeing. Some came in from neighbouring villages. For those parents the occasion had a date in their child’s life as well as one in the temple calendar. 10

The outward journey also makes a promise about coming home. In Puri, after the stay at Gundicha, the siblings travel back on the chariots in Bahuda Yatra. Read together in The Year That Feeds Us, the two stories begin on a bathing platform and end on a road. At Snana Purnima people made room for the deities to rest. At Ratha Yatra they make the journey possible themselves, with an offering from Kandapali, a blacksmith’s nails in Subarnapur, or hands back on the rope where Subhadra stopped the night before. 5 7 9
