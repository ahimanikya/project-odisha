The Year That Feeds Us

Snana Purnima: After the Deities Have Bathed

By Ahimanikya Satapathy

In Koraput, devotees could still see Jagannath, Balabhadra and Subhadra late into the evening of Snana Purnima in 2022. Servitors at Sabara Srikhetra had carried the three deities to the bathing platform, bathed them in fragrant water and dressed them in Hati Besha, their elephant attire. Jagabandhu Samal, secretary of the temple management committee, reported that darshan continued late that day. In the days that followed, another kind of service took its turn. Adivasi participants offered the deities pathi, a herbal drink for the fever they are believed to develop after their bath. 1 2

Snana Purnima (ସ୍ନାନ ପୂର୍ଣ୍ଣିମା) falls on the full moon of Jyeshtha. Readers of Chandan Yatra have already followed Madan Mohan, Jagannath’s travelling image, out onto Puri’s Narendra tank. There, servitors prepared a sandalwood bath for the visiting deities. On this full moon it is Jagannath himself who leaves the jewelled throne with his siblings. Their destination is close enough to the outer wall of the temple for people outside to see them bathe. It will be their last sight of the three for roughly a fortnight. 3 4

Water for the full moon

At Puri, the water comes from the Suna Kua, the Golden Well inside the temple precincts. The water is consecrated for the bath. Daita servitors escort the deities in the ceremonial procession called pahandi. Sudarshan accompanies the siblings to the Snana Bedi, the bathing platform. The count of 108 pitchers belongs to the ceremony as a whole. Once the bath is over, devotees see the deities dressed in elephant attire before they retire from public view. 3 4

The Haribaldev Jew temple in Baripada has its own account of the day. In 2022 servitor Arun Kumar Mishra described the offerings that followed the fragrant bath. Sandalwood paste was applied to the deities’ faces, tulasi and flowers were offered, and food was served on the platform. Only after the rites could devotees approach the Snana Bedi for darshan, the chance to see the deities. In the evening came Gaja Besha, another name for the elephant attire. At both Baripada and Koraput, the evening observance gave devotees a reason to return after the bath. 1

Who looks after them now?

In Puri the interval is called Anasara. In temple tradition the deities have taken ill, and Daitapati servitors attend to them away from public view. Part of that care begins well before the full moon, at the Bada Odia Math. There, sesame oil is steeped with fragrant flowers and other ingredients in a sealed earthen pot. The pot is buried, then unearthed on the eve of the bath. The oil is strained. When the temple sends for it during Anasara, the math delivers the phuluri taila for the servitors to apply. That part of the fortnight belongs to the attendants. 3 5

In Koraput the pathi is a local offering during the rest that follows the bath. There is also a route by which southern Odisha’s offerings reach Puri. In 2026, members of the Sabar Samaj Kalyan Samiti came to Puri’s Grand Road from Rayagada, Gajapati and Koraput. They brought roots, fruit, honey and sesame oil, as well as paddy and rice. Daitapati servitor Biswabasu Binayak Dasmohapatra received the gifts. Some were destined for the deities’ care and grain could go on to the temple kitchen. Samiti president Dalapati Mandal described this as the first time his organisation had undertaken the service. 2 6

What devotees see during Anasara

While the deities rest in Puri, worship continues before painted images known as Anasara Patti. Three paintings on cloth hang on a bamboo screen. Making them is part of the work of the chitrakars, the painters. Artist Binod Kumar Maharana, born in Puri’s Chitrakar Sahi, remembered two members of his own family who did that work. His father Harihara and his grandfather Markanda made the Patti Dian for Anasara. They also painted the chariots for Ratha Yatra. The same family’s work met the devotee at the screen and later out on the road. 3 7

In Subarnapur in western Odisha, a 2005 account remembers the sound of the bath as well as the quiet that followed. At the town’s Jagannath temple, the three images were taken to the bathing platform the night before the full moon. There was congregational singing for the bath in the presence of the Maharaja. During the ensuing fortnight the deities rested and received drinks known as Anasara pana. 8

In Puri the deities appear again for Naba Jaubana Darshana, the sight of them in renewed youth before the chariot festival. In The Year That Feeds Us, the distance from the bathing platform to that reunion is measured in ordinary things people know how to give someone who is unwell. In Koraput there is a drink to offer. In Puri there is oil someone had the foresight to put away months ago, and time allowed for rest. 2 4 5
