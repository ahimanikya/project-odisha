What shell offcuts reveal about Odisha’s coastal craft

By Ahimanikya Satapathy

A finished bangle shows the maker’s desired shape. An offcut can reveal how that shape was obtained. At Manikapatana on Odisha’s coast, discarded shell fragments provide evidence of a manufacturing process that would be difficult to reconstruct from ornaments alone.

Archaeologist Tilok Thakuria examined material collected during surface surveys between 2008 and 2011. His study identifies waste from working the conch Turbinella pyrum, including parts removed as the shell was prepared for cutting. The findings indicate bangle manufacture at the site, while their surface context limits how precisely individual fragments can be dated. 1

Making a ring from a spiral shell requires a sequence of decisions about what to retain. Parts of the apex and central structure are removed before usable sections can be obtained. Different kinds of waste therefore correspond to different operations. Thakuria notes the absence of finishing debris in his collection and proposes that some blanks may have been finished elsewhere. It remains an interpretation of that assemblage, not proof of the location of another workshop. 2

A much later written record shows that production could indeed be divided between places. James Hornell’s 1914 The Sacred Chank of India lists Balasore among districts with bangle factories. In his wider survey of the eastern Indian trade, he describes centres that cut shells into working sections and others that bought those sections for rubbing down and ornamenting with files and small saws. His observations document an early twentieth-century trade, rather than a direct continuation of Manikapatana’s archaeological workshop. 3

Shell work also appears in Odisha’s more recent craft administration. The Directorate of Handicrafts’ Baleswar directory includes sea-shell work in Bhogarai. That category is broader than conch-bangle making and should not be used to assign the same product or technique to every listed artisan. 4

Taken together, these records reveal several histories of working shell along the coast. They do not establish one unbroken workshop lineage. Their common interest is the transformation of a difficult natural form into usable objects, through cutting, abrasion and finishing.

The waste is especially eloquent. It preserves choices that the completed ornament conceals, allowing a craft history to begin with the pieces a maker had no reason to keep.
