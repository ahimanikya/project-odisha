SAYING 01 · proverbial saying#

Squeezing past the useful point

ଅତି ଲେମ୍ବୁ ଚିପୁଡ଼ିଲେ ପିତା ।

ଅତି କଲେ ଭଲ କାମର ଫଳ ମଧ୍ୟ ଖରାପ ହୋଇପାରେ।

Historical attestation located; diplomatic transcription and human Odia review pending.

moderation

Literal meaning

If you squeeze a lemon too much, it turns bitter.

What it means

The useful action—extracting juice—becomes counterproductive when carried too far. The warning is about proportion: pressing for one more favour, one more concession or one more advantage may damage what was already achieved. It does not mean that effort itself is bad.

When you might use it

After a neighbour has agreed to help for an afternoon, repeatedly asking for more time may invite this saying. (New editorial example, not a collected quotation.)

Cultural context

The image makes a social judgement through an ordinary kitchen action. This reading explains the metaphor; it is not a scientific claim about every citrus fruit or a documented account of how the proverb originated.

SAYING 02 · proverbial saying#

Inviting trouble

ଆ ବଳଦ ମୋତେ ବିନ୍ଧ ।

ନିଜେ ଡାକି ଅଯଥା ବିପଦରେ ପଡ଼ିବା।

Explained seed; historical collation and human Odia review pending.

judgement

Literal meaning

Come, ox, gore me.

What it means

The speaker imagines someone summoning the very trouble they could have avoided. Used ironically, it criticises an unnecessary provocation or a knowingly risky decision. It should not be used to blame someone for harm they did not choose.

When you might use it

A person interrupts an argument that has nothing to do with them merely to insult both sides; a friend may use this saying. (New editorial example, not a collected quotation.)

Cultural context

ବଳଦ is rendered here as ox or bullock. The horned animal supplies the force of the image; the wording alone cannot establish a particular village, farming community or date of origin.

SAYING 03 · proverbial saying#

There will be another winter

ଏକା ମାଘକେ ଶୀତ ଯାଏନାହିଁ।

ଆଜିର ଦରକାର ସରିଗଲେ ମଧ୍ୟ ଆଗକୁ ପୁଣି ଦରକାର ପଡ଼ିପାରେ।

Explained seed; historical collation and human Odia review pending.

foresight

Literal meaning

Cold does not finish with a single Magha.

What it means

One difficult season is not the last occasion on which we may need help. The saying can warn against treating a temporary advantage as permanent, or remind someone who refuses help that circumstances may change. Tone matters: it can sound like practical advice or a pointed warning.

When you might use it

After borrowing a tool successfully once, someone dismisses the neighbour who lent it. Another person reminds them that they may need that neighbour again. (New editorial example, not a collected quotation.)

Cultural context

Magha supplies the seasonal image. The central point is recurring need and changing fortunes, not a precise forecast or a claim that cold is confined to one calendar month.

SAYING 04 · proverbial saying#

Learning when responsibility arrives

କାନ୍ଧରେ ପଡିଲେ ବଜାଇ ଶିଖନ୍ତି

ଦାୟିତ୍ୱ ପଡ଼ିଲେ କାମ କରିବାର ଉପାୟ ଶିଖିହୁଏ।

Explained seed; historical collation and human Odia review pending.

learning

Literal meaning

When it falls on the shoulder, one learns to play.

What it means

Taking responsibility can force a beginner to learn the necessary skill. The compressed wording leaves the instrument unnamed; imagining a carried percussion instrument helps explain the metaphor, but does not establish which instrument first inspired it. This is encouragement to learn through doing, not a guarantee that preparation is unnecessary.

When you might use it

A first-time organiser worries about arranging a neighbourhood reading session. A more experienced friend uses the saying while offering help with the practical tasks. (New editorial example, not a collected quotation.)

Cultural context

The body carrying a burden and the hands learning a performance meet in one image. No historical link to a named musical tradition has been verified.

SAYING 05 · idiomatic saying#

Not even a straw broken

କୁଟା ଖଣ୍ଡକ ଦିଖଣ୍ଡ ନ କରିବା

ସାମାନ୍ୟ କାମଟିଏ ମଧ୍ୟ ନ କରିବା।

Explained seed; historical collation and human Odia review pending.

work

Literal meaning

Not to break one piece of straw into two.

What it means

This expression makes a very small action stand for any contribution at all. Saying that someone has not done even this means they have done no work in the situation being discussed. It is an idiomatic phrase rather than a complete rhymed maxim.

When you might use it

Several people clean up after a gathering while one person only gives instructions. Someone complains that the person has not lifted a finger—the comparable English idiom. (New editorial example, not a collected quotation.)

Cultural context

Straw provides a small, light object in the verbal image. The comparison with an English idiom explains the sense; it is not a claim of translation, borrowing or shared origin.

SAYING 06 · proverbial saying#

Advice that stops at the doorstep

କହି ଦେଉଥାଇ ପରକୁ, ବୁଦ୍ଧି ନ ଦିଶଇ ଘରକୁ

ଅନ୍ୟକୁ ଉପଦେଶ ଦେଇ ନିଜେ ତାହା ନ ମାନିବା।

Explained seed; historical collation and human Odia review pending.

self-awareness

Literal meaning

One keeps advising others, but wisdom is not seen at home.

What it means

The criticism is the gap between giving advice and practising it. The outward-facing adviser appears wise, yet fails to apply that wisdom to their own affairs. The saying invites self-examination; it does not imply that advice is worthless whenever its giver has difficulties.

When you might use it

A person lectures everyone about keeping accounts but repeatedly loses track of their own spending. A relative uses this line to point out the inconsistency. (New editorial example, not a collected quotation.)

Cultural context

The rhyme of ପରକୁ and ଘରକୁ reinforces the contrast between others and home. Here the structure is part of the saying’s force, not just decoration.

SAYING 07 · proverbial saying#

Noise is not the same as action

ଗରଜିଲା ମେଘ ବରଷେ ନାହିଁ

ବଡ଼ ବଡ଼ କଥା କହିବା ଓ କାମ କରିବା ଏକ କଥା ନୁହେଁ।

Explained seed; historical collation and human Odia review pending.

speech

Literal meaning

The cloud that thunders does not rain.

What it means

The contrast between loud thunder and absent rain is used to question bluster: impressive promises or threats do not necessarily lead to action. Read the sentence figuratively. Thunderstorms can, of course, bring rain; the proverb is not a dependable weather rule.

When you might use it

Someone loudly promises to repair a shared bench, but nothing happens. Others use the saying to ask for action rather than another announcement. (New editorial example, not a collected quotation.)

Cultural context

Weather becomes a way of talking about human speech. Similar imagery elsewhere would not by itself prove where this Odia wording originated.

SAYING 08 · proverbial saying#

Planting when the meal is due

ବାହାଘର ବେଳେ ବାଇଗଣ ରୂଆ

ଦରକାର ବେଳ ଆସିଗଲା ପରେ ପ୍ରସ୍ତୁତି ଆରମ୍ଭ କରିବା।

Explained seed; historical collation and human Odia review pending.

timing

Literal meaning

Planting eggplant at wedding time.

What it means

The joke is a mismatch between a long preparation and an immediate need. Planting a crop when the occasion has arrived cannot supply that occasion’s meal. The saying criticises starting essential work too late, rather than the act of planting itself.

When you might use it

The event starts tomorrow, but the organisers only now begin looking for a venue. Someone compares their timing to planting eggplant at wedding time. (New editorial example, not a collected quotation.)

Cultural context

The wedding and vegetable crop create the contrast. This does not prove eggplant is required at every Odia wedding or identify a particular community’s menu. The graphic illustrates lead time, not an agricultural timetable.

Anger aimed at the bowl

emotion

Literal meaning

Biting the bowl in anger at the medicine.

What it means

The unpleasant thing is the medicine, but the anger is directed at its container. The expression describes displaced frustration: attacking a messenger, object or bystander instead of addressing the actual problem.

When you might use it

A delayed delivery frustrates a customer, who shouts at a neighbour merely passing on the update. Another person uses the saying to identify the misplaced anger. (New editorial example, not a collected quotation.)

Cultural context

The source writes ଓଷଦ and କାମୁଡିବା; this edition preserves those forms rather than silently replacing them with modern spellings. The image explains behaviour and gives no advice about taking medicine.

SAYING 10 · proverbial saying#

A little game, an enormous quarrel

ଝିମିଟି ଖେଳରୁ ମହାଭାରତ

ସାମାନ୍ୟ କଥାରୁ ବଡ଼ ଝଗଡ଼ା ହେବା।

Explained seed; historical collation and human Odia review pending.

conflict

Literal meaning

From a game of jhimiti to the Mahabharata.

What it means

A small disagreement grows into an outsized conflict. The Mahabharata provides a culturally recognisable scale of conflict. The expression is not evidence that the epic war literally began with this named game, and the game’s precise historical rules have not been established here.

When you might use it

Two friends disagree over a turn in a game; soon several families are arguing. A neighbour uses the saying to describe how far the quarrel has grown. (New editorial example, not a collected quotation.)

Cultural context

ଝିମିଟି is retained rather than replaced by an invented modern game. The contrast is between a modest starting point and epic-scale escalation.

SAYING 11 · proverbial saying#

When dishonest gain takes the roots with it

ଅଧର୍ମ ବିତ୍ତ ବଢେ ବହୁତ, ଗଲା ବେଳେ ଯାଏ ମୂଳ ସହିତ ।

ଅସତ୍ ଉପାୟରେ ଅର୍ଜିତ ଧନ ଶେଷରେ ସବୁ କିଛି ହରାଇବାର କାରଣ ହୋଇପାରେ।

Explained entry; human Odia proofreading pending.

integrity

Literal meaning

Wrongfully gained wealth grows greatly, but disappears with its roots.

What it means

The image contrasts rapid growth with loss at the root. Wealth obtained dishonestly may look impressive, yet the conduct behind it can endanger the whole household or enterprise. This is a moral warning about how gain is obtained, not a reliable prediction that every dishonest person will become poor.

When you might use it

A trader is tempted to falsify weights for a little extra profit. An older colleague uses the saying to warn that losing customers’ trust could destroy the business. (New editorial example, not a collected quotation.)

Cultural context

The rhyme of ବହୁତ and ସହିତ gives the warning a memorable close. The root image suggests losing the foundation, not merely the most recent gain. A version appears as the opening proverb of the1893 collection.

SAYING 12 · proverbial saying#

Keeping one’s own dignity

ଆଲୋ ସଖି, ଆପଣା ମହତ ଆପେ ରଖି

self-respect

Literal meaning

Friend, preserve your own dignity yourself.

What it means

The friendly address makes this advice sound like one person speaking directly to another: preserve your dignity through your own conduct. It may counsel restraint when someone is tempted to join a humiliating quarrel or chase approval. It does not make a person responsible for mistreatment inflicted by others.

When you might use it

During an argument, a friend is about to answer an insult with another insult. Someone close to them uses the line to encourage a calm withdrawal. (New editorial example, not a collected quotation.)

Cultural context

The contemporary witness begins ଆଲୋ ସଖି. The1893 scan appears to begin ଆରେ ସଖି; that difference is retained as a provisional historical reading. The wording does not establish who first used the saying or confine its advice to one gender.

habit-and-change

What it means

The joke gives a rice-pounding implement a grand change of address but leaves its work exactly as before.
